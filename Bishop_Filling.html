⛽ Filling Station

An oily, grimy filling station hides something extraordinary: quiet, invisible love. Someone has made this ugly place feel like a home — somebody loves us all.

~42 lines · 6 Stanzas Conversational Tone Narrative → Allegorical Epiphany Ending
🤔
The Simple Version

Bishop visits a very dirty, oil-covered filling station somewhere in California. A father and his sons work there, completely covered in grease. She is amused and slightly appalled. But as she looks more carefully, she notices things that don't belong here: a hand-embroidered doily, a plant (a begonia), oil cans arranged neatly. Someone has tried to make this ugly, functional place feel like a home. At the end, she realises there is an absent figure — probably a mother — whose love has quietly shaped this place. The poem ends with one of her most famous lines: "Somebody loves us all."

📋 At a Glance

  • Theme: Family; a mother's love; the ordinary made extraordinary
  • Tone: Playful, feigns confusion and disapproval → genuine wonder → moved
  • Form: Narrative account with allegorical lesson at the end
  • Mood: Wryly humorous → curious → deeply emotional
  • Effect: Entertaining, then suddenly moving; evokes sympathy for the poet

🎯 The Central Journey

Bishop enters the filling station thinking it is just a dirty, ugly, functional place. She observes the family with gentle mockery. Then she notices the domestic details — the doily, the plant, the arranged cans — and they don't fit. Why are they here? Who put them here?

The answer to that question is the poem. Someone — not present — has quietly, lovingly shaped this place. Their love is invisible but its effects are everywhere.

Stanza 1 — Setting the Scene
Oh, but it is dirty!
—oil-soaked, oil-permeated
to a disturbing, over-all
black translucency.
Be careful with that match!

What's Happening

"Oh, but it is dirty!" — The exclamation mark signals Bishop's characteristic playfulness. She is almost delighted by the dirtiness. The conversational opener ("Oh, but") makes her sound like she is talking directly to us — chatty, amused, present.

"black translucency" — a striking paradox. Something dark but still letting light through. Even in the very first stanza, in the very first description, Bishop finds something that is not simply ugly. Light exists even in the blackness.

"Be careful with that match!" — A sudden, playful warning. She imagines the station catching fire. But it also suggests something more: the place is vulnerable, precarious. A home that could easily be destroyed.

Stanzas 2–3 — The Family
It is a father and his sons;
they are quite thoroughly dirty,
this little family. They seem
to quite thoroughly enjoy it,
the "quick and saucy" sons,
the father and his grease-soaked suit.

What's Happening

The family is introduced with warmth and gentle humour. They are "quite thoroughly dirty" — the repetition of "quite thoroughly" is amused, not disgusted. She likes them.

The sons are "quick and saucy" — Bishop seems charmed by their energy and hustling. The father wears a "monkey suit" that "cuts him under the arms" — funny, human, real.

Despite the griminess, there is a sense of this family belonging here, being at home in their work. They are comfortable in this environment. This is their world — and she is a visitor in it.

Stanza 3 introduces the "cement porch" and "dirty dog" — further domestic details. Someone has tried to create a home here. The dog is a pet. The porch is a place to sit. These small details hint at what is to come.

Stanzas 4–5 — The Domestic Details That Don't Fit
Some comic books provide
the only note of color —
of certain color. They lie
upon a big dim doily
draping a taboret
(part of the set), beside
a big hirsute begonia.

What's Happening

The comic books are mentioned first — the only colour in this drab place. They also suggest a child nearby, or a welcome for children — someone has made this a place where children are comfortable.

Then the doily appears: "a big dim doily". Why is there a doily in a filling station? A doily is a delicate, hand-embroidered domestic object — entirely out of place here. But it is here. Someone put it here.

The begonia — a plant. Plants need care; they need watering. Someone is tending this plant. Someone cares enough about this grimy place to nurture something living in it.

Stanza 5 focuses further on the doily, now described in loving detail: "embroidered in daisy stitch / with marguerites." The detail is precise — someone spent real time making this. It was not bought; it was made. This is an object of love.

Stanza 5 — "Why Oh Why the Doily?"
Why oh why the doily?
(Embroidered in daisy stitch
with marguerites, I think,
and heavy with gray crochet.)

What's Happening

"Why oh why" — the double "why" shows genuine puzzlement. The repeated question is humorous on the surface but serious underneath: Bishop really wants to know. The answer will be the poem's revelation.

The doily is described in extraordinary detail — "daisy stitch," "marguerites," "gray crochet." This is someone who knows needlework. The poem slows down here, dwelling on the object with the same attention that Bishop gives to the fish's scales or the armadillo's flight. Close observation is her method. The object will tell her something if she looks closely enough.

Stanza 6 — "Somebody Loves Us All" (THE EPIPHANY)
Somebody embroidered the doily.
Somebody waters the plant,
or oils it, maybe. Somebody
arranges the rows of cans
so that they softly say:
ESSO—SO—SO—SO
to high-strung automobiles.
Somebody loves us all.

What's Happening — THE EPIPHANY

Anaphora "Somebody...Somebody...Somebody" — three repetitions building to the final revelation. Each "somebody" names another act of love: the embroidery, the watering, the arranging. An absent figure has been quietly at work throughout.

"ESSO—SO—SO—SO" — playful, musical. The oil can labels become a little lullaby. Even a brand name, in Bishop's hands, becomes gentle and comforting. "High-strung automobiles" — even cars are given feelings, are "soothed" by the arranged cans. This is Bishop's humour and her ear for language at its best.

"Somebody loves us all." — Five words. The entire poem has been building to these five words. The "somebody" is almost certainly an absent mother figure — a woman whose love has shaped this place even in her absence.

🧠
Why This Line Is So Powerful for Bishop

Bishop grew up without her mother (committed to an institution when Bishop was 5). She never had the domestic, maternal love that this filling station quietly displays. The realisation that love can be present even when the person is absent — that love leaves traces in the objects it touches — may be Bishop coming to terms, late in life, with the love her own absent mother may have had for her. This line contains everything.

📝
Exam Tip — The Ending

"Somebody loves us all" is one of Bishop's most important lines. Use it to demonstrate: (1) her observational style leading to epiphany; (2) her use of the ordinary to reveal the extraordinary; (3) the personal emotional depth hidden beneath her careful, descriptive surface. Always connect it to her motherless childhood.

"Oh, but it is dirty!"
Line 1 — Opening

Tone Conversational The exclamation mark signals amusement, not horror. "Oh, but" is conversational — she is talking to us directly. This sets up the poem's tone: playful observation that will deepen into something unexpected. Compare to the Armadillo's tone of wonder — both poems move from surface observation to a deeper emotional revelation.

"Why oh why the doily?"
Stanza 5

Repetition Rhetorical Question The repeated "why" shows genuine puzzlement — the doily is the pivot of the poem. It is the detail that doesn't fit, the detail that demands explanation. The question drives the poem forward toward its answer. The doily is described in precise, expert detail — "daisy stitch," "marguerites" — someone who knows embroidery made this with care and love.

"so that they softly say: / ESSO—SO—SO—SO / to high-strung automobiles."
Stanza 6

Sound Humour Personification The oil cans seem to hum a little lullaby to the cars. Even a commercial brand name becomes musical and comforting in Bishop's hands. The cars are "high-strung" — given anxiety, feelings. This playfulness makes the subsequent emotional landing ("Somebody loves us all") even more powerful by contrast.

"Somebody embroidered the doily. / Somebody waters the plant, / or oils it, maybe. Somebody / arranges the rows of cans... / Somebody loves us all."
Final Stanza — The epiphany

Anaphora Epiphany Three "Somebodys" building to the final revelation. An absent figure — almost certainly a mother — has been quietly at work, making this ugly place feel cared for, making it feel like a home.

"Somebody loves us all" — the "us all" is significant. Not just the family. All of us. Love is available everywhere, hidden in overlooked objects, tended by invisible hands.

🔗
Link to Other Poems

Compare to the ending of The Fish: "And I let the fish go." Both are short, quiet lines after long build-ups. Both are epiphanies — moments where close observation reveals something profound and universal. Both are about recognising something that was always there, just unseen.

🧭 Bishop's Motherless Childhood

Bishop's mother was committed to a mental asylum in 1916, when Bishop was 5. She never saw her again. Growing up, Bishop moved from relatives to relatives — her grandparents in Nova Scotia, her father's family in Massachusetts, an aunt in Boston. She never had the stable maternal home that this filling station quietly displays.

The realisation in the poem's final line — that an absent person's love can still be present in the objects they have touched and tended — may be Bishop's way of coming to terms with her own mother's love for her, which she never directly experienced. Your notes suggest she "realizes now that her mother loved her, despite what she might have previously thought." This makes "Somebody loves us all" one of the most personally significant lines she ever wrote.
