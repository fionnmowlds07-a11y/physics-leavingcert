Poem 3 · Responsibilities · 1914

September 1913

A savage rhetorical attack on the materialistic Irish middle class — who have betrayed the heroic sacrifice of Romantic Ireland for the sake of money and slavish prayer.

Nationalism Materialism Heroic Past Bitter Irony Irish Identity

📋 Background & Overview

The immediate trigger for this poem was the Dublin Corporation's refusal to build a gallery to house Sir Hugh Lane's magnificent art collection — Lane had offered it to the city on the condition that a proper gallery be provided, but the miserly councillors refused. For Yeats, this was not just about paintings — it was a symbol of the Irish middle class's contempt for culture.

The year 1913 was also a year of massive social unrest in Dublin: the Lockout of that year saw workers strike against employers, and poverty and deprivation were widespread. Yeats saw the middle class as oppressors of both culture and workers.

The poem is a rhetorical attack — published first in The Irish Times, it was written to be read and to provoke. The "you" addressed is the Catholic middle class, the target of Yeats's furious disappointment.

🎯 Core Themes

  • Materialism vs. Culture: The middle class cares only for money and religion — not for art, culture, or heroism. Yeats sees this as a betrayal of Irish identity.
  • The Heroic Past: Ireland's Romantic heroes — Wolfe Tone, Robert Emmet, Lord Edward Fitzgerald — are held up as models of selfless sacrifice that the current generation cannot understand.
  • The Death of Romantic Ireland: The repeated refrain — "Romantic Ireland's dead and gone / It's with O'Leary in the grave" — is a funeral cry for an idealism that has been replaced by greed.
  • Identity & Culture: What does it mean to be Irish? Yeats argues that culture, heritage, and self-sacrifice — not commerce — are what define the Irish nation.
  • Bitterness & Disillusionment: This is one of Yeats's angriest poems. The irony is sharp, the contempt barely contained.

📅 Who was John O'Leary?

John O'Leary (1830–1907) was an Irish Nationalist and member of the Irish Republican Brotherhood, exiled for his involvement in the movement. He was passionate about Irish culture as an essential component of Irish Nationalism — "there is no literature without nationality and no nationality without literature." Yeats regarded him as the epitome of Irish patriotism. O'Leary died in 1907, and his death symbolises for Yeats the death of the whole tradition he represented.

🎓
Exam Tip

This is Yeats's most politically combative poem. Focus on the rhetorical strategy: he addresses "you" directly, uses repetition and refrain to hammer his point home, and contrasts the degraded present with a heroic past. Always discuss the refrain — "Romantic Ireland's dead and gone / It's with O'Leary in the grave" — as a funeral cry that anchors the poem's argument.

Stanza 1
What need you, being come to sense,
But fumble in a greasy till
And add the halfpence to the pence
And prayer to shivering prayer, until
You have dried the marrow from the bone;
For men were born to pray and save:
Romantic Ireland's dead and gone,
It's with O'Leary in the grave.
Stanza 2
Yet they were of a different kind,
The names that stilled your childish play;
They have gone about the world like wind,
But little time had they to pray
For whom the hangman's rope was spun,
Or what, God help us, could they save?
Romantic Ireland's dead and gone,
It's with O'Leary in the grave.
Stanza 3
Was it for this the wild geese spread
The grey wing upon every tide;
For this that all that blood was shed,
For this Edward Fitzgerald died,
And Robert Emmet and Wolfe Tone,
All that delirium of the brave?
Romantic Ireland's dead and gone,
It's with O'Leary in the grave.
Stanza 4
Yet could we turn the years again,
And call those exiles as they were
In all their loneliness and pain,
You'd cry, 'Some woman's yellow hair
Has maddened every mother's son':
They weighed so lightly what they gave.
But let them be, they're dead and gone,
They're with O'Leary in the grave.
Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis
"But fumble in a greasy till / And add the halfpence to the pence"

Yeats opens with savage irony — "being come to sense" suggests the middle class think they have become wise, rational, practical. But Yeats means the opposite: they have come to their senses in the worst possible way, abandoning idealism for greed. The "greasy till" is a brilliant image — the grease suggests both the tainted nature of the money and the physical revulsion Yeats feels. "Halfpence to the pence" — a pitiful sum, all that small-minded calculation amounts to.

"Prayer to shivering prayer" — religion here is not genuine faith but fearful, repetitive, mechanical. The word "shivering" is telling — they pray out of cold fear, not warm devotion. Both work and prayer become equally futile, equally joyless, equally soul-destroying.

"You have dried the marrow from the bone" — a devastating physical metaphor. The marrow (the rich, living inner substance) has been sucked dry. Nothing vital remains. The nation is a hollow bone.

"The names that stilled your childish play"

A beautiful, poignant image. The heroes of Romantic Ireland — Emmet, Tone, Fitzgerald — were names so powerful that they stopped children in their games, held them in awe. Children understood heroism instinctively. The adults these children became have forgotten it. Yeats implies that the middle class were once capable of this admiration — and have since abandoned it.

"They have gone about the world like wind" — the heroes were free, unpredictable, powerful, elemental. The simile elevates them above ordinary mortals. The middle class, by contrast, is earth-bound, counting pennies in shops. The contrast is brutal. "For whom the hangman's rope was spun" — these men accepted execution. They "weighed so lightly what they gave" — their lives meant less to them than the cause. The middle class cannot conceive of this scale of sacrifice.

"Was it for this the wild geese spread / The grey wing upon every tide"

The "Wild Geese" refers to the Irish soldiers who fled abroad to fight on every front — exiled, fighting for other countries because their own had no place for them. They spread their wings "upon every tide" — an image of ceaseless, graceful, selfless movement. Their sacrifice was total and global. And for what? "For this" — Yeats's withering sarcasm: for a nation of shopkeepers.

The listing of names — "Edward Fitzgerald died, / And Robert Emmet and Wolfe Tone" — creates a roll-call of the martyred dead. The anaphora of "For this" in the preceding lines hammers home his contempt. All that blood, all that idealism, all that "delirium of the brave" — and the result is a greedy, culture-hating, church-fearing middle class. "Delirium of the brave" is magnificent: the bravery was a kind of madness, an irrational, magnificent excess of passion. The current middle class is entirely rational — and utterly without passion.

"You'd cry, 'Some woman's yellow hair / Has maddened every mother's son'"

In the final stanza, Yeats imagines the middle class meeting the exiled heroes and dismissing their sacrifice as irrational passion — as if their heroism was no more than being driven mad by a woman's beauty. "Some woman's yellow hair" is a symbol of Ireland as a beautiful woman (Cathleen Ní Houlihan) — the heroes were driven by love of Ireland. But the materialistic mind cannot see beyond the literal; it reduces noble sacrifice to foolishness.

The refrain changes in this final stanza — "But let them be, they're dead and gone" — a subtle but devastating shift. "Romantic Ireland's dead and gone" becomes "they're dead and gone." The mourning becomes more personal, more tender, as if Yeats is finally accepting what he has been lamenting throughout. The heroes deserve better than this country gave them. Let them rest.

Key Quotes
ImageryVerb ChoiceTone
Technique: Visceral imagery of greed
"fumble in a greasy till"

"Fumble" suggests clumsiness, desperation, obsession. The "greasy till" is physically revolting — the grease suggests moral contamination, the taint of commerce on everything it touches. Yeats makes us feel physical disgust at money-obsession. One of his most effective images.

AlliterationRepetitionIrony
Technique: Sibilance and repetition revealing hollow religion
"prayer to shivering prayer"

"Shivering" is devastatingly precise — these prayers are born of cold fear, not warm belief. The repetition parallels "halfpence to the pence" — both work and prayer are equally mechanical, equally empty, equally joyless. Religion becomes no different from commerce: both are ways of accumulating something through repetitive, fearful effort.

RefrainElegiac ToneRepetition
Technique: Refrain as funeral cry and rhetorical hammer
"Romantic Ireland's dead and gone, / It's with O'Leary in the grave."

The repetition after each stanza is both lament and accusation. Each stanza builds its case, and the refrain closes it with the same verdict: this is a funeral, and the middle class is both the cause of death and the chief mourner. The rhythm is almost songlike — Yeats wants these lines to be remembered, to stick, to haunt.

OxymoronAdmirationIrony
Technique: Paradoxical admiration for irrational heroism
"All that delirium of the brave"

"Delirium" suggests madness, irrationality, excess — and yet it is "of the brave." Yeats admires the very irrationality of the heroes' sacrifice. Their passion exceeded reason, and that was their greatness. The rational middle class cannot produce this kind of magnificent, irrational excess — they are too careful, too calculating, too sane. This line summarises what has been lost.

MetaphorContrastWeight Imagery
Technique: Physical metaphor of weight to convey the scale of sacrifice
"They weighed so lightly what they gave."

The middle class weighs everything — counting halfpence. The heroes weighed their lives so lightly that they gave them without calculation. The contrast in weighing is the contrast in values. The merchant class calculates every transaction; the heroic class gave away everything without a second thought. This is what Yeats means by Romantic Ireland — not romance as in love, but as in the Romantic ideal of passion over reason.

Poetic Techniques

📏 Form: Iambic Tetrameter

Eight-line stanzas with iambic tetrameter (four beats per line) and rhyme scheme ababcdcd. The tight, controlled form reflects the rigour of Yeats's argument. But the pace also moves briskly, almost like a march or chant — the poem wants to be heard, not just read.

🔁 Refrain as Rhetoric

The refrain "Romantic Ireland's dead and gone / It's with O'Leary in the grave" is repeated after each stanza. Each repetition is a rhetorical hammer — the verdict after each indictment. It also creates a musical, incantatory quality: Yeats wants these words to be unforgettable.

🎭 Direct Address

"You," "they," "we" — the shifting pronouns are deliberate. "You" is accusatory, addressing the middle class directly. "They" elevates the heroes to a separate category. "We" in stanza 4 draws us into the scene, making us complicit. The reader cannot escape Yeats's argument.

🌊 The Wild Geese Image

The "wild geese" spread their wings "upon every tide" — an image of freedom, flight, and relentless, selfless movement. Comparable to "The Wild Swans at Coole" — in both poems, free birds flying contrast with the earthbound, static Yeats or the earthbound middle class. Flight = heroism. Earthbound = mediocrity.

💬 Rhetorical Questions

"Was it for this the wild geese spread..." — rhetorical questions force the reader to engage and to feel the absurdity of what has happened. Yeats does not need answers — the questions themselves are the answer. "What, God help us, could they save?" is also darkly ironic: nothing, of course — but the attempt was glorious.

🔤 Sound: Hard Consonants

Hard "p," "b," "d," and "g" sounds throughout stanza 1 — angry, biting, spitting consonance. The sounds of the poem are harsh where the subject is contemptible, and flowing where the heroes are described. Sound mirrors meaning throughout.

Context & Exam Preparation
🎓
Key Exam Argument

This poem is a rhetorical attack, so analyse Yeats's techniques of persuasion. The refrain, the direct address, the visceral imagery, the contrast between past heroes and present merchants — these are all deliberate rhetorical strategies. Also argue about the poem's tensions: is Yeats too elitist? Is he romanticising the past? The examiner rewards nuanced thinking.

Links to Other Yeats Poems

  • Easter 1916: The single most important link. The middle class Yeats vilified here are the very people he comes to admire after the Rising. His view shifts entirely. This pair of poems shows Yeats's growth as a political thinker.
  • Politics: Both are "political" poems, but with very different registers. September 1913 is furious; Politics is playful and self-deprecating. An interesting contrast in tone.
  • Wild Swans: The image of wild geese "spreading their wing upon every tide" echoes the swans. In both poems, free-flying birds contrast with earthbound, static human weakness.
💡
Exam Quote to Use

"All that delirium of the brave" — a perfect phrase to quote when discussing Yeats's admiration for the irrational, passionate heroism of Romantic Ireland. It captures both his admiration and his understanding that their sacrifice exceeded rational calculation.
