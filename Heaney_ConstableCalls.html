Poem 4 · North · 1975

A Constable Calls

A young Catholic boy watches an armed RUC constable visit his family — every observed detail charged with the fear, power imbalance, and sectarian tension of the Troubles.

The TroublesPower & FearCatholic ExperienceChildhood MemorySymbolism of Authority

📋 Background & Overview

Written in 1975, this is the second poem in a sequence of six called "Singing School" — autobiographical poems highlighting key moments that helped shape Heaney as a person and a poet. It is written from the viewpoint of the young Heaney as a boy, watching an event unfold with the intense, fearful attention of a child who understands more than he can articulate.

The Heaney family were Catholic nationalists in Northern Ireland. The Royal Ulster Constabulary (RUC), disbanded in 2001, was the armed police force of Northern Ireland — predominantly Protestant and widely regarded by the Catholic community as an instrument of state oppression. They could stop, question, and detain without the usual legal protections. "We knew," Heaney said, "that Ulster wasn't meant for us."

The constable calls to check the family's "tillage return" — a record of what crops they have grown. The father lies about some turnips. The boy knows. And the tension of that knowledge — of a small lie told to an armed representative of a hostile state — fills the poem.

🎯 Core Themes

  • Power and Fear: The constable's weapons, uniform, and authority create overwhelming fear in the Catholic household. Every detail is charged with threat.
  • Catholic Alienation: The Catholic nationalist community experienced the state as hostile, intrusive, threatening. This poem captures that experience from the inside, through a child's eyes.
  • Guilt and Complicity: The boy's guilt at knowing his father has lied — and imagining the consequences — is the emotional heart of the poem's second half.
  • Childhood Perception: The poem's power comes from the child's intense, detailed, fearful observation — every object noted and weighted with menace.
  • Doomsday Symbolism: The ledger of crop records becomes the "domesday book" — the ultimate book of reckoning. The state has the power of judgement over Catholic lives.
🎓
Key Exam Insight

The poem creates tension not through dramatic violence but through intense, meticulous observation. Every detail of the constable's equipment — holster, baton, cap, ledger — is noted with fearful precision. The tension builds through accumulation. Always discuss the symbolism of the constable's weapons and uniform as instruments of state power, and the child's position as fearful, powerless observer.

Stanzas 1–2: The Bicycle
His bicycle stood at the window-sill,
The rubber cowl of a mud-splasher
Skirting the front mudguard,
Its fat black handlegrips

On its fat black handlegrips.
He had unstrapped
The heavy ledger, and my father
Was making tillage returns
Stanzas 3–4: The Constable Observed
In acres, roods, and perches.
Arithmetic and fear.
I sat staring at
The polished holster

With its buttoned flap, the braid cord
Looped into the revolver butt.
His cap was upside down
On the floor, beside his chair.
Stanzas 5–6: The Questioning
The line of force
The black boot of the law.
On the strip of ledger
All his equipment.

'Any other root crops?
Mangolds? Marrowfat? Anything else?'
'No.' But was there? I knew
There were turnips in the field.
Stanzas 7–8: Guilt and Departure
I recalled
A shadow bobbing in the lane,
Dragging his boot,
The domesday book

His pale face towered
Over the barracks' roof.
He stood up, shifted the baton-case
Further round on his belt,

Closed the domesday book,
Fitted his cap back with two hands,
And looked at me as he said goodbye.
A shadow bobbed in the window.

He was snapping the carrier spring
Over the ledger. His boot pushed off
And the bicycle ticked, ticked, ticked.
Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis
"His bicycle stood at the window-sill, / The rubber cowl of a mud-splasher / Skirting the front mudguard"

The poem begins with the constable's bicycle, described in precise, almost threatening detail. The bicycle stands — present, dominant, watching. "Stood at the window-sill" — as if on guard, watching the household. The "spud of the light generator" gleaming and "cocked back" like a weapon. Even the bicycle is threatening.

The "fat black handlegrips" — the repetition of "fat black" has a heavy, suffocating quality. Black throughout the poem is the colour of authority, threat, and the law. The bicycle is personified as an extension of the constable's power. "The boot of the law" — even the vehicle embodies the weight and oppression of the legal system.

"Arithmetic and fear. / I sat staring at / The polished holster / With its buttoned flap, the braid cord / Looped into the revolver butt."

"Arithmetic and fear" — perhaps the most compressed and brilliant phrase in the poem. The tillage return is arithmetic — numbers, facts, recorded information. But the act of giving this information to an armed constable is fear. The two words together capture the entire experience of the Catholic community under the RUC: ordinary bureaucratic life saturated with terror.

The boy stares at the holster, the revolver, the braid cord. His observation is fearful and precise. The constable's cap lies upside down on the floor — a subtle image: the law is not the right way up here; it is inverted, wrong, not functioning as it should. Or perhaps the officer's bevel mark shows his own pressure. The equipment is described as "symbols of power" — holster, cap, ledger, revolver, baton all become instruments of state authority in this Catholic kitchen.

"'Any other root crops? / Mangolds? Marrowfat? Anything else?' / 'No.' But was there? I knew / There were turnips in the field."

The dialogue arrives — four questions fired rapidly by the constable, overwhelming the father's monosyllabic "No." The father has lied. He has not declared the turnips. In itself this is trivial. But the boy knows, and the knowledge opens a chasm of fear.

The boy imagines the consequences: a "jail cell in the barracks," a "black hole" — the gravity of the situation is immense for a Catholic child who knows that the barracks and the law are not his friends. "Black hole" suggests both the cell itself and the sense of total powerlessness — being swallowed by the state. The guilt of knowing his father has lied to the representative of a hostile state is the most precise rendering of what it felt like to be Catholic in Northern Ireland: always on the wrong side of the law, always slightly guilty, always at risk.

"He was snapping the carrier spring / Over the ledger. His boot pushed off / And the bicycle ticked, ticked, ticked."

The constable packs up his "domesday book" — the ledger has been given a name resonant with judgement and finality. The Domesday Book was William the Conqueror's record of England's population — a book of reckoning, of ownership, of power over land and people. The tillage ledger becomes this: the state recording and controlling the Catholic farmer's land.

As he leaves, "he looked at me as he said goodbye" — the boy is noticed. Maybe paranoia brought on by guilt; maybe a real moment of contact. Either way, the constable's eye falls on the child. There is no escaping the watchful eye of the state.

The final image is unforgettable: "the bicycle ticked, ticked, ticked." The ticking is both the bicycle's mechanical sound and the sound of a time-bomb — the Troubles ticking forward, the tension that will eventually explode. The repetition of "ticked" creates an ominous, unstoppable rhythm. The poem ends not with relief but with danger quietly continuing.

Poetic Techniques

📐 Form: 8 Stanzas of 4 Lines

The regimental, rigid structure mirrors the constable's authority — controlled, disciplined, formal. Short, snappy statements add to the tension. The consistent form suggests a state machine that grinds forward without deviation or mercy.

👁️ Observational Precision

The poem's power comes from precise, fearful detail: the rubber cowl, the braid cord, the polished holster, the slightly sweating hair. The boy notices everything because fear makes you hyperaware. This clinical, detailed observation is the poem's primary technique — it enacts the terror by making everything visible and threatening.

🔊 Cacophony: Hard "B" and "CK" Sounds

"Buttoned flap, the braid cord, looped into the revolver butt" — the harsh "b" sounds are cacophonous, heavy, physically intrusive. The sounds of the poem enter the quiet Catholic home like the constable himself — hard, uncomfortable, impossible to ignore.

⚖️ Symbolism of Equipment

Every piece of the constable's equipment is a symbol of state power: the holster = violence; the ledger/domesday book = recording and control; the cap = authority; the baton = physical force; the boot = oppression. The accumulation of these symbols makes the constable less a person and more an embodiment of state power.

⟶ Enjambment & Tension

Enjambment speeds the poem forward in moments of action and tension; full stops create sudden pauses that heighten the threat. The juxtaposition of flow and pause creates the poem's distinctive rhythm of anxiety — moments of apparent calm punctuated by jolts of fear.

💬 Dialogue

The constable's four rapid questions ("Any other root crops? Mangolds? Marrowfat? Anything else?") overwhelm the father's single monosyllabic "No." The imbalance of language mirrors the imbalance of power. The constable speaks; the Catholic farmer is reduced to bare denial. Dialogue here is an instrument of authority.

Context & Exam Preparation
🎓
Key Exam Argument

This poem shows how the Troubles invaded even the most ordinary domestic moments. The constable's visit is routine bureaucracy — but in the context of armed sectarian policing, it becomes a scene of oppression and fear. Always discuss the child's perspective (innocent but observant, fearful but precise), the symbolism of the equipment (holster, domesday book, bicycle), and the ticking ending as an image of the ongoing tension of the Troubles.

Links to Other Heaney Poems

  • The Tollund Man: Both are political poems about violence and its context. Tollund Man approaches through mythology and history; Constable Calls through intimate domestic observation. Together they show the Troubles operating on every scale — mythological and mundane.
  • Mossbawn: Sunlight: A perfect companion — both are set in the same Catholic rural world, at roughly the same time. Sunlight is peace; Constable Calls is threat. They are the twin faces of Heaney's childhood: the private sanctuary and the public danger.
  • Bogland: Both deal with the instability and complexity of Irish/Northern Irish identity. Bogland works through landscape metaphor; Constable Calls through a specific human encounter.
