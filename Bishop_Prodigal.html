Poem 2 · A Cold Spring, 1955

🐷 The Prodigal

Alcoholism, shame, and the long road to recovery. A man lives in a pig sty, hiding his shame and his bottles, as he slowly, painfully faces the truth that he needs to go home.

28 lines · Double Sonnet Shakespearean + Looser Form Slant Rhyme at End Autobiographical
🤔
The Simple Version

Think of someone who has hit rock bottom. They know they need help, but admitting that is the hardest thing in the world. So they keep telling themselves things are fine — hiding their bottles, watching a sunrise, thinking "maybe I can last another year." This poem is about that long, painful journey from denial to finally deciding to face reality. It is a poem about addiction — but written with deep empathy, not judgment.

📋 At a Glance

  • Theme: Debasement, addiction, and redemption
  • Tone: Resigned, fretful, nervously hopeful
  • Form: Double sonnet (two 14-line poems)
  • Mood: Resignation → tentative optimism
  • Effect: Disturbing, evoking sympathy, biographical
  • Biblical source: The Prodigal Son (Luke 15:11–32)

🗓️ Background

  • From A Cold Spring (1955) — written 4 years earlier
  • Inspired by an experience in Nova Scotia in 1946: her aunt's stepson offered her rum at 9am in the pig sties
  • Bishop struggled with alcoholism throughout her life
  • She identifies with the Prodigal — both were living in shame, both had to face a long road home
  • Her understanding of the character is from the inside, not the outside
📝
Exam Tip — What to Argue

This poem shows Bishop at her most confessional and most compassionate. She doesn't pity the Prodigal — she understands him. Key argument: Bishop uses the double sonnet form to mirror the psychological journey from denial (Sonnet 1, ordered, structured, self-deceiving) to reality (Sonnet 2, looser, darker, more honest). The form IS the meaning.

📐 The Double Sonnet — Why Two?

The poem is split into two sonnets of 14 lines each. Each sonnet has a different structure — and the difference is intentional. The two sonnets represent two stages of the Prodigal's psychological journey.

Sonnet 1 (Lines 1–14) — The Shakespearean Sonnet

More ordered and structured. Three quatrains and a rhyming couplet. The form is controlled, disciplined — mirroring the Prodigal's denial. He is imposing a false sense of order on his chaotic life, telling himself things are manageable, finding hope in sunrises.

  • Regular rhyme scheme (ABAB etc.)
  • Controlled, contained energy
  • Represents: denial, false hope, self-deception

Sonnet 2 (Lines 15–28) — The Looser Sonnet

Less ordered. The rhyme scheme weakens. This represents reality crashing in — the dark evening, the nightmarish imagery, the dawning truth. The form unravels as his denial unravels.

  • Looser rhyme scheme, more irregular
  • Darker, more chaotic imagery
  • Represents: the collapse of denial, facing reality
  • Ends with slant rhyme — "home" / "more" — all is still not resolved

🎭 The Slant Rhyme Ending

"home" and "more" do not perfectly rhyme — this is called slant rhyme or off-rhyme. It is deliberate. The poem's structure, like the Prodigal's life, is not neatly resolved at the end. He has made the decision to go home, but the journey is only beginning. The broken rhyme mirrors the incomplete recovery.

📝
Exam Tip

Always mention the slant rhyme when discussing this poem's form. It is one of Bishop's most sophisticated structural choices. Say: "The slant rhyme of 'home' and 'more' at the poem's close suggests that even after making the decision to recover, all is not yet well — the journey is only just beginning."

🌅 Morning vs Evening — The Two Halves

Sonnet 1 is set in the morning. Sonnet 2 begins with the evening. This is not accidental:

  • Morning = hope, light, possibility, self-deception (the sunrise seems to "reassure")
  • Evening = reality, darkness, truth (the star "warns," the night closes in)

The structure of the two sonnets mirrors the arc of a day — and of recovery: you cannot stay in the comfortable morning light of denial forever. Eventually, evening comes and forces you to face the truth.

Lines 1–8 — Rock Bottom (Sonnet 1, Octave)
The brown enormous odor he lived by
was too close, with its breathing and thick hair,
for him to judge. The floor was rotten; the sty
was plastered halfway up with glass-smooth dung.
Light-lashed, self-righteous, above moving snouts,
the pigs' eyes followed him, a cheerful stare —
even to the sow that always ate her young —
till, sickening, he leaned to scratch her head.

What's Happening

The poem opens at rock bottom. The man is living in a pig sty — not metaphorically but literally. The opening line begins with "The" (not "A") which drops us straight into a world that already exists, with no romantic lead-in.

Imagery "Brown enormous odor" — dull, suffocating, inescapable. He has been living so long in this environment he can no longer judge it; he has normalised his degradation.

Personification The pigs are "self-righteous" — they seem to morally judge him. Even the sow that eats her own young has an air of superiority over him. This disturbing dark comedy conveys how low he has sunk: even the worst animals seem to have moral authority over him.

"till, sickening, he leaned to scratch her head" — He is so lonely and desperate for any kind of companionship that he tries to make friends with the pig that eats her young. This is heartbreaking.

Lines 9–14 — False Hope (Sonnet 1, Sestet)
But sometimes mornings after drinking bouts
(he hid the pints behind a two-by-four),
the sunrise glazed the barnyard mud with red;
the burning puddles seemed to reassure.
And then he thought he almost might endure
his exile yet another year or more.

What's Happening

A sudden shift: the morning after drinking, the sunrise makes the muddy barnyard briefly beautiful. Red light, "burning puddles." For a moment, things seem hopeful.

"seemed to reassure" — "Seemed" is doing critical work. Not "did reassure" — only "seemed." It is an appearance of hope, not real hope. The language is already undermining the comfort it appears to offer.

"he almost might endure" — triple qualification: "almost," "might," and "endure" (not "enjoy" or "thrive" — just "endure"). This is the language of someone desperately trying to convince themselves to keep going, without actually convincing themselves.

⚠️
Key Critical Idea

Can hope be misplaced? The Prodigal uses a beautiful sunrise to justify staying in a terrible situation. This is the paradox of addiction: moments of beauty or relief become reasons not to change. Bishop doesn't preach — she simply shows us the mechanism of denial working in real time.

Lines 15–20 — Reality Arrives (Sonnet 2, Opening)
But evenings the first star came to warn.
The farmer whom he worked for came at dark
to shut the cows and horses in the barn
beneath their overhanging clouds of hay,
with pitchforks, faint forked lightnings, catching light,
safe and companionable as in the Ark.

What's Happening

"But" — the single most important word in the poem. It instantly destroys everything the first sonnet built up. "But evenings" — the morning illusion cannot last. Reality arrives with the dark.

Personification The star "warns" him — nature itself is sending a message. The evening will not let him maintain his daytime self-deception.

Storm Imagery "overhanging clouds of hay," "pitchforks as faint forked lightnings" — the pitchforks become lightning bolts, the hay becomes storm clouds. This is foreboding, ominous. A storm is coming — metaphorically, the storm of reality he has been avoiding.

"safe and companionable as in the Ark" — the animals inside are safe, warm, together. The Prodigal is outside, alone. This contrast is devastating: even the animals have more security and companionship than he does.

Lines 21–28 — Nightmarish Reality and the Decision
The pigs stuck out their little feet and snored.
The lantern — like the sun, going away —
laid on the mud a pacing aureole.
Carrying a bucket along a slimy board,
he felt the bats' uncertain staggering flight,
his shuddering insights, beyond his control,
touching him. But it took him a long time
finally to make his mind up to go home.

What's Happening

"The lantern — like the sun, going away" — hope is literally fading. The lantern, like the false hope of the morning sunrise, is going away. Darkness is taking over.

Imagery "slimy board," "bats' uncertain staggering flight," "shuddering insights" — nightmarish, disorienting. The bats' "staggering" flight may echo his own drunken unsteadiness. These "shuddering insights" are the truths he cannot escape — they touch him, involuntarily, "beyond his control."

"But it took him a long time / finally to make his mind up to go home." — The understatement is devastating. After all the horror, the nightmarish imagery, the isolation — this simple, plain sentence. Recovery is not dramatic. It is slow, it is reluctant, it takes a long time. Bishop doesn't romanticise it.

"The brown enormous odor he lived by"
Line 1

Imagery Colour "Brown" is dull, earthy, associated with decay and filth. "Enormous" — this smell has swallowed his entire world. He has been living in it so long "he cannot judge" it.

Starts with "The" not "A" — we are dropped into a world that already exists. No build-up, no introduction. This is his life, already in progress, already at the bottom.

"Light-lashed, self-righteous, above moving snouts, / the pigs' eyes followed him, a cheerful stare — / even to the sow that always ate her young"
Lines 5–7

Personification Dark Humour The pigs are described as "self-righteous" — morally superior. Even the most monstrous pig (the one that eats her young) appears to look down on him. This is darkly comic and deeply disturbing at the same time.

It shows the distorted reality of someone in the grip of addiction — you feel judged by everything, even by things that have no right to judge. His sense of shame is so extreme it infects his whole perception of reality.

"the sunrise glazed the barnyard mud with red; / the burning puddles seemed to reassure. / And then he thought he almost might endure / his exile yet another year or more."
Lines 11–14 — End of Sonnet 1

Qualified Language Colour Imagery "Seemed" — not "did." The hope is only an appearance. "Almost might endure" — three layers of qualification. He is desperately trying to convince himself.

The red sunrise is momentarily beautiful, transforming dirty mud into something glowing. But it is still mud. He is still hiding his pints. The beauty is real but the comfort is false.

⚠️
Key Idea

This is a perfect illustration of how denial works in addiction — using genuine moments of beauty as excuses not to change. Bishop understands this mechanism because she lived it.

"But evenings the first star came to warn."
Line 15 — First line of Sonnet 2

Structure Personification The single word "But" at the start of Sonnet 2 is the hinge of the whole poem. It destroys everything the first sonnet built. "But evenings" — the daytime denial cannot last.

"Warn" — the star is not beautiful; it is a warning. Nature is not on his side in the evening. The evening brings truth.

"But it took him a long time / finally to make his mind up to go home."
Lines 27–28 — The final lines

Understatement Slant Rhyme After the nightmarish imagery of the second sonnet, these plain, quiet words carry enormous weight. Recovery is not dramatic. It is slow and reluctant.

"Home" and "more" (from the previous line) do not rhyme. This is deliberate slant rhyme — the journey is not complete. Even the poem's structure refuses to resolve neatly.

"Finally" — this word contains multitudes. It took everything. The long time. All the sunrises and all the dark evenings. And finally, finally, the decision.

🔗
Link to Other Poems

Compare to The Fish's ending — "And I let the fish go." Both are short, understated lines after a long build-up. Both carry enormous emotional weight through their very simplicity. Bishop's epiphanies are always quiet.

🧭 Why Bishop Wrote This — The Personal Connection

Bishop struggled with alcoholism for much of her adult life. She frequently had to leave friends' homes and accommodation out of shame following drinking episodes. She understood the Prodigal's situation from the inside — not as an observer but as someone who had lived through similar experiences of shame, hiding, and the long process of deciding to face reality.

The poem was inspired by a specific memory: in 1946, visiting Nova Scotia, her aunt's stepson offered her rum at 9 in the morning in the pig sties. This small, specific memory became the catalyst for the poem.

📖 The Biblical Reference — The Prodigal Son

The title alludes to the Parable of the Prodigal Son (Luke 15:11–32): a young man demands his inheritance early, spends it recklessly, ends up feeding pigs in poverty, and eventually returns home to his father's embrace.

Bishop's version updates this parable for the modern experience of addiction. She removes the father waiting at home, the embrace, the celebration. Her poem focuses entirely on the time in the pig sty — the experience of rock bottom, denial, and the slow decision to return. The return home is mentioned in the very last line, but the reception is not described. The focus is the struggle, not the rescue.
