🌿 The Lake Isle of Innisfree

Innisfree
"I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree"
Tap to reveal technique →
Innisfree
Biblical Allusion + Repetition
Echoes Luke 15:18 (Prodigal Son). The repeated "and go" gives the longing the force of a spiritual vow. Not a casual wish — a determined declaration.
Innisfree
"peace comes dropping slow"
Tap to reveal technique →
Innisfree
Personification + Sibilance + Onomatopoeia
Peace is a liquid thing descending slowly. The sibilance and long vowels slow the reader physically — the line enacts the peace it describes. One of Yeats's most musically precise moments.
Innisfree
"bee-loud glade"
Tap to reveal technique →
Innisfree
Compound Adjective + Auditory Imagery
Yeats invents "bee-loud" — a hyphenated compound capturing a whole atmosphere. Sound defines the place: it hums with life and productivity. Three words contain an entire world.
Innisfree
"on the pavements grey"
Tap to reveal technique →
Innisfree
Colour Symbolism + Contrast
After midnight's "glimmer" and noon's "purple glow," the single word "grey" lands like a verdict. It is the colour of everything Innisfree is not: uniform, lifeless, urban. The contrast is devastating in its simplicity.
Innisfree
"I hear it in the deep heart's core"
Tap to reveal technique →
Innisfree
Metaphor + Alliteration + Final Line Power
The poem's most important line. Innisfree is not a plan but a permanent inner state — home is carried within. Alliteration of "deep/heart/core" drives it home. The imagination transcends geography.
Innisfree
"midnight's all a glimmer, and noon a purple glow"
Tap to reveal technique →
Innisfree
Dreamlike Colour Imagery + Full Day Structure
Not realistic — the colours are magical, mythological. Midnight "glimmers"; noon glows purple. Innisfree is described as memory and desire paint it: luminous, heightened, supernatural. The island is perfect at every hour.

🦢 The Wild Swans at Coole

Wild Swans
"nine-and-fifty swans"
Tap to reveal technique →
Wild Swans
Symbolism + Number
Swans mate for life — 59 means one is alone without a partner. Directly mirrors Yeats's own loneliness. The archaism "nine-and-fifty" (not "fifty-nine") slows the reading, giving the number weight and a fairy-tale quality.
Wild Swans
"The nineteenth autumn has come upon me"
Tap to reveal technique →
Wild Swans
Personification of Time + Verb Choice
"Come upon" — not "passed" — makes time an ambush, a predator. Nineteen years have crept up and overwhelmed him. The phrasing removes his agency: time happened to him. This is one of Yeats's most honest admissions of vulnerability.
Wild Swans
"All's changed since I... Trod with a lighter tread"
Tap to reveal technique →
Wild Swans
Contrast + Compression + Link to Easter 1916
"All's changed" — two words carrying nineteen years of loss. Compare to "All changed, changed utterly" in Easter 1916. The "lighter tread" of the younger Yeats is gone: he was unburdened by disappointment, age, and unrequited love.
Wild Swans
"Their hearts have not grown old"
Tap to reveal technique →
Wild Swans
Juxtaposition + Symbolism
Direct counterpoint to "my heart is sore." The swans are immortal in spirit — always paired, always passionate. Yeats's heart has aged and grown heavy. The swans represent everything he desires and cannot attain.
Wild Swans
"To find they have flown away?"
Tap to reveal technique →
Wild Swans
Rhetorical Question + Ambiguity
The poem ends on a question — no resolution. The swans will leave; someone else will see them. "Awake" may carry a spiritual charge: if he wakes from death, the earthly beauties will be gone. Yeats refuses to answer — the uncertainty is the honesty.
Wild Swans
"the bell-beat of their wings above my head"
Tap to reveal technique →
Wild Swans
Auditory Imagery + Compound Noun
"Bell-beat" — the wings are musical, rhythmic, almost sacred. Like the ringing of a church bell. This was the sound of Yeats's youth and hope. Now he hears it differently — as a reminder of what he has lost. The memory is beautiful and painful simultaneously.

✊ September 1913

Sept 1913
"fumble in a greasy till"
Tap to reveal technique →
Sept 1913
Visceral Imagery + Verb Choice
"Fumble" = desperate, obsessive. "Greasy till" = physically revolting — moral contamination by commerce. Yeats makes us feel physical disgust at money-obsession. One of his most effective single images.
Sept 1913
"prayer to shivering prayer"
Tap to reveal technique →
Sept 1913
Sibilance + Repetition + Irony
"Shivering" reveals these prayers are born of cold fear, not warm faith. Parallel to "halfpence to the pence" — both work and prayer are equally mechanical, futile, joyless. Religion becomes no different from commerce.
Sept 1913
"Romantic Ireland's dead and gone, / It's with O'Leary in the grave"
Tap to reveal technique →
Sept 1913
Refrain + Elegiac Tone + Rhetoric
The funeral cry of the poem — repeated after each stanza like a verdict. Each repetition is a hammer-blow. The rhythm is almost chant-like, ensuring the words stick. Lament AND accusation simultaneously.
Sept 1913
"All that delirium of the brave"
Tap to reveal technique →
Sept 1913
Oxymoron + Admiration
"Delirium" = madness, irrationality — yet it is "of the brave." Yeats admires the very irrationality of the heroes' sacrifice. The rational middle class cannot produce this magnificent, irrational excess. The phrase captures exactly what has been lost.
Sept 1913
"Was it for this the wild geese spread / The grey wing upon every tide"
Tap to reveal technique →
Sept 1913
Rhetorical Question + Wild Geese Symbol
The Wild Geese = Irish exiles who fought on every front. "Every tide" = ceaseless, selfless movement. The rhetorical question is withering sarcasm — all that sacrifice, for THIS? The image connects to Wild Swans: free birds vs earthbound humans.
Sept 1913
"They weighed so lightly what they gave"
Tap to reveal technique →
Sept 1913
Weight Metaphor + Contrast
The middle class weighs every halfpenny. The heroes weighed their lives so lightly they gave them without calculation. The contrast in weighing is the contrast in values. Self-sacrifice vs. self-interest. Passion vs. calculation.

🌹 Easter 1916

Easter 1916
"A terrible beauty is born"
Tap to reveal technique →
Easter 1916
Oxymoron + Refrain + Paradox
The most famous line in Irish poetry. "Terrible" = violent, costly, destructive. "Beautiful" = heroic, idealistic, transforming. The oxymoron refuses to let us see only one side. Repeated 4 times, growing more complex each time. The poem's irresolvable heart.
Easter 1916
"All changed, changed utterly"
Tap to reveal technique →
Easter 1916
Repetition + Link to Wild Swans
Double repetition of "changed" enacts the overwhelming, undeniable transformation. Compare to "All's changed" in Wild Swans — Yeats uses this formula for total, irreversible change. Here it is national; in Swans it is personal.
Easter 1916
"Enchanted to a stone / To trouble the living stream"
Tap to reveal technique →
Easter 1916
Extended Metaphor + Ambiguity
The stone = the revolutionary: fixed, purposeful, unchanging. The stream = life in all its beautiful variation. "Enchanted" = magic AND petrification. Is total dedication heroism or a loss of humanity? Yeats deliberately refuses to judge.
Easter 1916
"Too long a sacrifice / Can make a stone of the heart"
Tap to reveal technique →
Easter 1916
Metaphor + Warning against Fanaticism
Sustained sacrifice petrifies the heart — removes tenderness and humanity. A gentle warning about fanaticism. Echoes his thoughts on Maude Gonne, whose politics made her "shrill." The revolutionary gives up their humanity to become a cause.
Easter 1916
"Was it needless death after all?"
Tap to reveal technique →
Easter 1916
Rhetorical Question + Intellectual Courage
The bravest line in the poem. No nationalist propagandist would ask this. Home Rule had been promised — perhaps it was achievable without violence. Yeats does not answer. His refusal to simplify is what makes the poem great rather than merely political.
Easter 1916
"polite meaningless words"
Tap to reveal technique →
Easter 1916
Confession + Colloquial Language
Yeats confesses his own blindness and contempt. He dismissed the future martyrs as circus performers, mocked them at the club. The colloquial language makes the confession personal and specific. His guilt is the emotional starting point of the entire poem.

🏛️ Sailing to Byzantium

Byzantium
"A tattered coat upon a stick"
Tap to reveal technique →
Byzantium
Dehumanising Metaphor + Body/Soul
The body of an old man reduced to a scarecrow — no life, no soul, no use. "Tattered" = literally falling apart. But this is followed immediately by "unless / Soul clap its hands and sing" — the metaphor sets up the argument for art as transcendence.
Byzantium
"Soul clap its hands and sing, and louder sing"
Tap to reveal technique →
Byzantium
Personification + Repetition + Defiance
The soul claps its hands — exuberant, joyful, physical. It must sing "louder" the more the body decays. The worse the physical decline, the more urgent the creative response. Physical decay drives spiritual intensity. Art compensates for — and transcends — mortality.
Byzantium
"fastened to a dying animal"
Tap to reveal technique →
Byzantium
Metaphor + Entrapment + Body as Prison
"Fastened" = chained, imprisoned. The body is not a home but a dying prison. "Dying animal" is brutally graphic — bodies die messily, slowly. The soul is attached to this process. Yeats's most visceral expression of the desire to escape physical existence.
Byzantium
"the artifice of eternity"
Tap to reveal technique →
Byzantium
Paradox + Art as Immortality
"Artifice" = made, artificial. "Eternity" = uncreated, infinite. Together: art is the human-made equivalent of divine permanence. Natural things die; made things endure. The poem itself is an "artifice of eternity" — it outlives Yeats's body. It has.
Byzantium
"Of what is past, or passing, or to come"
Tap to reveal technique →
Byzantium
Tricolon + Prophecy + Final Line
The golden bird sings of past, present, and future simultaneously — transcending time entirely. The poet as prophet, omniscient, eternal. The three-part structure is complete and elegant. If his soul becomes this bird, his poetry outlasts his body — and it has.
Byzantium
"Those dying generations"
Tap to reveal technique →
Byzantium
Irony + Contrast
Ireland is vibrant — young lovers, birds in trees, salmon-falls. Yet everything in this living world is dying. The irony is devastating: the most alive-seeming things are the most mortal. This sets up the entire argument for seeking eternity in Byzantium.

🦅 An Acre of Grass

Acre of Grass
"Where nothing stirs but a mouse"
Tap to reveal technique →
Acre of Grass
Bathetic Imagery + Setting
Almost comic in its quiet desolation — the only vitality in his world is a mouse. The bathos is deliberate: this is what Yeats is trying to escape. It sets up the urgency of the prayer that follows. The reader immediately understands why frenzy is needed.
Acre of Grass
"My temptation is quiet"
Tap to reveal technique →
Acre of Grass
Honest Self-Revelation + Tone
Yeats is not tempted by vice — he is tempted by quiet. Part of him wants to give in, to rest, to accept. He recognises this as the enemy of creativity. The poem is a battle against this very temptation. The most honest admission in all his late work.
Acre of Grass
"Grant me an old man's frenzy"
Tap to reveal technique →
Acre of Grass
Prayer + Tone Shift + Passion
"Grant me" = formal prayer. "Frenzy" = not a normal prayer request. Yeats prays for something beyond rational control — passionate intensity that the quiet mind cannot generate. The prayer is the poem's emotional pivot from diagnosis to defiance.
Acre of Grass
"Myself must I remake"
Tap to reveal technique →
Acre of Grass
Inverted Syntax + Alliteration + Theme
Inverted syntax ("Myself must I" not "I must remake myself") gives the line archaic, forceful authority. To create great art at 71, he cannot accept who he is — he must transform himself. Art is not just expression; it is self-transformation. One of his most demanding self-declarations.
Acre of Grass
"Blake / Who beat upon the wall / Till Truth obeyed his call"
Tap to reveal technique →
Acre of Grass
Allusion + Physical Violence Metaphor
Blake as model of relentless, passionate creativity in old age. "Beat upon the wall" is almost violent — truth must be beaten out of the universe; it does not come willingly. The rhyme "wall/call" drives home the determination. Art requires this intensity.
Acre of Grass
"An old man's eagle mind"
Tap to reveal technique →
Acre of Grass
Symbol + Resolution + Defiance
The eagle: sharp, far-seeing, powerful, a predator. "Old man's eagle mind" argues that the body ages but the mind — if fed by frenzy — can still soar. Age is in the body; transcendence is in the mind. The poem ends not in surrender but in defiant assertion.
