Na Véarsaí Ceann ar Cheann

Each of the four verses of An tEarrach Thiar paints a distinct scene from spring life on Inis Mór. Here we break down each verse image by image, sound by sound, with key Irish phrases for your exam answer.

1

Véarsa 1 — The Farmer Digging

Fear ag glanadh cré
De ghimseán spáide
Sa gciúnas séimh,
Binn an fhuaim,
San Earrach Thiar.
A man cleaning clay / From the heel of a spade / In the gentle quiet, / Sweet is the sound, / In the Western Spring.

🖼️ The Central Image

The poet opens the poem with a single, still image: a lone farmer cleaning clay from the heel of his spade. This is a snapshot of traditional agricultural life on the island — obair traidisiúnta (traditional work) that the poet witnessed as a young man. The image is deceptively simple but carries enormous emotional weight for someone looking back from exile in the city.

Tuigimid ón aidiacht (we understand from the adjective) "séimh" (gentle/soft) go dtaitníonn ciúnas an atmaisféar sa pháirc go mór leis an bhfile — the calmness of the scene clearly delights the poet. The farmer is working at his own pace, saor ó bhuairt (free from worry).

🔊 Sound Imagery — Binn an Fhuaim

The line "Binn an fhuaim" (sweet is the sound) is one of the most important lines in the poem. It tells us the poet does not just see this scene — he hears it too. The scraping sound of the spade on clay, which might seem mundane, is described as binn (sweet, melodious). This shows the depth of the poet's love: even the everyday sounds of island work are precious to him.

🎵
Sound vs. Sight

Verse 1 is unique in combining both hearing and sight — "sa gciúnas séimh" (in the gentle quiet) evokes atmosphere, while "binn an fhuaim" highlights a specific sound. As a reader, you feel you can almost hear the scene yourself. Mention this duality in your exam answer for extra marks.

🌱 Traditional Work / Obair Thraidisiúnta

Digging and tending land was the foundation of survival on Inis Mór. The rocky limestone soil of the Aran Islands required enormous effort to cultivate — small plots called "garraí" were worked by hand, with seaweed used as fertiliser. When the poet recalls this image, he is recalling not just a moment, but an entire way of life that stood in sharp contrast to his office job in Dublin city.

Is léir gur bhain an fear taitneamh as an obair agus go raibh sé ar a shuaimhneasIt is clear the man enjoyed the work and was at peace
Tá íomhá den gciúnas agus den tsíocháin le feiceáil sa véarsa seoAn image of quietness and peace is visible in this verse

Mar léitheoir, táim beagnach ábalta fuaimeanna na hoibre sin a chloisteáilAs a reader, I am almost able to hear the sounds of that work
2

Véarsa 2 — The Red Seaweed Shining

Fear ag caitheamh
Cliabh dhá dhroim,
Is an fheamainn dhearg
Ag lonrú
Ar dhuirling bháin,
Niamhrach an radharc,
San Earrach Thiar.
A man throwing / A basket from his back, / And the red seaweed / Shining / On a white rocky shore, / Wondrous the sight, / In the Western Spring.

🎨 Colour Imagery — Dathanna

Verse 2 is the most visually rich verse in the poem. The poet contrasts two vibrant colours: the red of the seaweed (an fheamainn dhearg) against the white of the rocky shore (duirling bháin). This colour contrast creates a vivid, almost painterly image of the island landscape. Baineann an file sárúsáid as dathanna sa véarsa seo — the poet makes superb use of colours in this verse.

✨ Niamhrach — Wondrous, Radiant

The word "niamhrach" (wondrous, radiant, dazzling) is one of the most powerful adjectives in the entire poem. It describes the sight of the red seaweed shining on the white shore. This single word tells us that for the poet, the island is not just beautiful — it is almost otherworldly, magical, a place of wonder. The use of "niamhrach" elevates the description above mere observation into genuine awe.

Creideann an file féin go bhfuil an radharc seo go hálainn — the poet himself believes this sight is beautiful. The word "niamhrach" carries a note of wonder and reverence.

⚓ Seaweed Gathering — Obair an Chladaigh

The cliabh (basket/creel) was used to carry seaweed from the shore up to the fields, where it was spread as fertiliser on the rocky limestone soil. This was essential, back-breaking work on the Aran Islands. The image of a man lowering this heavy basket from his back is also a metaphor: de réir mar a íslíonn an fear an cliabh dá dhroim, scaoileann sé leis an ualach meafarach — as the man lowers the basket, he releases a metaphorical burden. The rest and beauty of the island work as restorative.

💡
Exam Tip — Colour in Verse 2

When writing about imagery in this poem, always mention the colour contrast in Verse 2 specifically: red seaweed against a white shore, under the golden sun. The word "lonrú" (shining) adds light to this image, making it even more vivid. A high-scoring answer mentions all three elements: colour, light, and the adjective "niamhrach".

✍️ Phrases to Use in Your Answer

Sa dara véarsa, tá cuimhní an fhile ag baint leis an bhfeamainn dhearg ag lonrú ar dhuirling bháinIn the second verse, the poet's memories are associated with the red seaweed shining on a white rocky shore
Baintear úsáid as an aidiacht "niamhrach" chun cur síos a dhéanamh ar an íomha seoThe adjective "wondrous/radiant" is used to describe this image
Baineann an file sárúsáid as dathanna sa véarsa seoThe poet makes superb use of colours in this verse
Is léir go bhfuil radharcanna álainn ar an oileánIt is clear that there are beautiful views on the island
Táim ábalta samhlú cé chomh geal is atá an fheamainn dhearg ar an tráI can picture just how vibrant the red seaweed is on the beach
3

Véarsa 3 — Women at the Shore

Mná i locháin
In íochtar diaidh-thrá,
A gcótaí craptha,
Scáileanna fúthu
Ag rince ar ghainimh,
San Earrach Thiar.
Women in pools / At the ebb of the tide, / Their skirts tucked up, / Shadows beneath them / Dancing on the sand, / In the Western Spring.

👩 Community Spirit — Spiorad Pobail

For the first time in the poem, we move from a lone individual to a group. The women working together at the shore embodies the spiorad pobail (community spirit) that was central to life on the island. The poet shows that island life was not merely individual labour but a shared, communal endeavour.

Léiríonn an file an spiorad pobail a bhain le saol an oileáin sa tríú véarsa — the poet illustrates the community spirit associated with island life in the third verse.

💃 Scáileanna ag Rince — Shadows Dancing

The most beautiful detail in this verse is the image of the women's shadows dancing on the sand beneath them: "scáileanna fúthu ag rince ar ghainimh". This transforms an ordinary work scene into something almost magical. The shadows appear to dance — an involuntary, unself-conscious beauty in ordinary labour. This is a moment of unexpected poetry hidden in everyday life on the island.

Éiríonn le Máirtín ciúine agus sáimhe a chur sa phictiúr de na mná ag obair san fharraige — Máirtín succeeds in placing quietness and calmness into the picture of the women working in the sea.

🌊 The Tide — An Taoide

The phrase "in íochtar diaidh-thrá" (at the ebb of the tide) sets the time precisely: the tide has gone out, revealing the rock pools where shellfish and seaweed could be gathered. This kind of careful, tide-dependent work required knowledge of the sea — it was passed down through generations. The "gcótaí craptha" (skirts tucked up) gives us a vivid, realistic detail of the women at work in the water.

✨
The Dancing Shadows — A Key Poetic Detail

The shadows dancing on the sand is the most cited image from Verse 3 in exam answers. It transforms work into art — the women are not conscious of their dancing shadows, but the poet notices them and finds beauty in them. This reflects his deep love for even the smallest details of island life. Always quote "scáileanna fúthu ag rince ar ghainimh" in your answer on imagery.

✍️ Phrases to Use in Your Answer

Sa tríú véarsa, tá na mná ag baint feamainne ar an trá nuair atá an taoide imithe amachIn the third verse, the women are collecting seaweed on the beach when the tide has gone out
Tá íomhá ann de na mná san uisce le cótaí crapthaThere is an image of the women in the water with skirts tucked up
Léiríonn an file an spiorad pobail a bhain le saol an oileáinThe poet illustrates the community spirit associated with island life
Cruthaíonn an file atmaisféar socair agus suaimhneach ar an oileánThe poet creates a calm and peaceful atmosphere on the island
Is é "scáileanna fúthu ag rince ar ghainimh" an t-íomhá is deise sa véarsa seo"Shadows beneath them dancing on the sand" is the most beautiful image in this verse

Véarsa 4 — The Fishermen Return

Currach lán éisc
Ag teacht chun cladaigh
Ar órmhuir mhall
I ndeireadh lae,
San Earrach Thiar.
A currach full of fish / Coming to shore / On a slow, golden sea / At the end of day, / In the Western Spring.

🌅 Ór-Mhuir Mhall — The Golden Sea at Day's End

The final verse is many students' favourite, and for good reason. The phrase "ar órmhuir mhall" (on a slow, golden sea) is one of the most beautiful in the entire poem. Ór means gold — the sea reflects the setting sun. Mall means slow — the sea is calm, gentle. Together they create an image of profound peace and beauty as the day draws to a close.

Is aoibheann liom an tsíocháin a bhaineann le dul faoi na gréine órga ag dathú na spéire — I love the peace associated with the golden sun going down and colouring the sky.

🚣 The Currach — Symbol of Island Life

The currach (traditional Aran canoe made of canvas stretched over a wooden frame) is one of the most iconic symbols of life on the Aran Islands. The image of a currach lán éisc (currach full of fish) coming home at dusk represents a successful day's work — not just survival, but abundance. Cuairtear saibhreas sa bhreis leis an véarsa seo le híomhá den fhlúirse éisc ar bord na curaí — great wealth is added to this verse with an image of an abundance of fish on board the currach.

🕊️ Peace and Completion — Síocháin agus Críoch

The final verse brings the poem to a gentle close. The day is ending, the fishermen are returning home, the sea is golden and calm. The repetition of "San Earrach Thiar" here carries its full emotional weight — this is the western spring the poet has been longing for throughout his years in the city. The verse is both beautiful and melancholy: it is a memory of something lost, a world the poet can no longer inhabit.

Tugtar an léargas dúinn go bhfuil saol an oileáin síochánta, cé go mbíonn muintir an oileáin an-ghnóthach ag obair — we are given the insight that island life is serene, even though the islanders are very busy working.

🎓
Exam Favourite

The fourth verse is the one exam candidates most often choose as their personal favourite. The "órmhuir mhall" (golden slow sea) image is vivid and memorable. You can write: "Is é an ceathrú véarsa is mó a thaitníonn liom" (The fourth verse is the one I like most) and then explain why — the beauty of the sunset, the abundance of the catch, and the peace of the end of day all combine to create an unforgettable final image.

Tá an fharraige "órmhuir mhall" — cur síos álainn ar an bhfarraige ag lonrú mar gheall ar luí na gréineThe sea is "a slow golden sea" — a beautiful description of the shining sea because of the sunset
