🔗 Thematic Links

How the six poems connect across Yeats's recurring tensions — time & ageing, nationalism, art & immortality, love & loss, nature, body vs soul. Essential for comparative exam answers.

Six poems — cross-poem connections

⏳ Time, Ageing & Mortality

The most consistent theme across Yeats's work — the passing of time, the decay of the body, the loss of youth and love. It appears in different registers: elegy, defiance, transcendence, and acceptance.

Wild Swans Elegy for Lost Youth

The most plaintive expression of ageing. Nineteen years have "come upon" Yeats. His heart is sore; the swans' hearts have "not grown old." Time is an ambush — passive, devastating.

"The nineteenth autumn has come upon me"

Byzantium Transcendence Through Art

Rather than lamenting age, Yeats fights it through art. The body is "a tattered coat upon a stick" — but the soul that sings will become the golden bird, eternal, timeless.

"fastened to a dying animal"

Acre of Grass Defiant Creative Frenzy

The most combative response to age. No elegy, no transcendence — just the raw prayer for passion: "Grant me an old man's frenzy." The body is diminished; the eagle mind refuses to be.

"An old man's eagle mind"

Innisfree Longing Preserved in Memory

An earlier, gentler response — time is not yet an enemy. But the poem shows Yeats already using the inner life (memory, imagination) as a refuge from the present. The pattern begins here.

"I hear it in the deep heart's core"
🎓
Exam Comparison Sentence

Yeats's treatment of ageing ranges from the elegiac helplessness of The Wild Swans at Coole — where time has "come upon" him and left his heart "sore" — to the fierce defiance of An Acre of Grass, where he prays for "an old man's frenzy" and claims an "eagle mind" that age cannot diminish. Between these extremes, Sailing to Byzantium offers transcendence through art as a third response.

🚢 Escapism — Three Different Destinations

Three poems are explicitly poems of escape — but the place they escape to, and the reason for escaping, are radically different in each case. This is one of the most productive comparisons in the entire set.

Poem Escaping FROM Escaping TO Key Quote
Innisfree Grey London pavements, urban grind, homesickness Simple nature — beans, bees, lake water — pastoral peace "I hear it in the deep heart's core"
Byzantium Mortal body, "dying animal," the sensual world that forgets intellect Eternal art, the golden city, the "artifice of eternity" "gather me / Into the artifice of eternity"
Acre of Grass Quiet, stillness, the "temptation" of passive old age Not escape at all — defiant earthly engagement, frenzy, creativity "Grant me an old man's frenzy"
🎓
The Key Contrast

In Innisfree, Yeats longs for the peace of natural solitude — yet in An Acre of Grass, that same peace has arrived and he finds it intolerable: "My temptation is quiet." This reversal is one of the most striking developments across his career. He got what he wished for, and it was not enough.

🌿 Nature — Healing, Mirror, and Indifferent

Nature plays very different roles across the poems — from the longed-for paradise of Innisfree to the unsympathetic swans who represent everything Yeats is not.

Innisfree Nature as Paradise

Nature is healing, complete, perfect. The bee-loud glade, the evening full of linnet's wings — it offers everything the city cannot. Nature is entirely positive here.

"bee-loud glade"

Wild Swans Nature as Painful Mirror

The swans are beautiful — but their beauty highlights what Yeats lacks. They have hearts that "have not grown old." Nature is indifferent to his suffering and more alive than he is.

"Their hearts have not grown old"

Sept 1913 Nature as Heroic Symbol

The wild geese spread their wings "upon every tide" — nature's freedom symbolises the selfless, ceaseless sacrifice of Ireland's heroes, contrasted with the earthbound middle class.

"the wild geese spread / The grey wing upon every tide"

Easter 1916 Nature as Living Stream

The stream changes "minute by minute" — its natural vitality and variety contrasts with the revolutionary stone that is fixed and unchanging. Nature = free life; stone = dedicated sacrifice.

"Minute by minute they live: / The stone's in the midst of all"

🇮🇪 Nationalism, Ireland & Political Change

Three poems deal directly with Irish politics, and they trace an extraordinary arc — from furious disillusionment (1913) to conflicted admiration (1916) to playful dismissal (Politics, not in this set but referenced). Together, September 1913 and Easter 1916 show Yeats's growth as a political thinker.

September 1913 Furious Disillusionment

The middle class is greedy, church-fearing, culture-hating. The heroes of Romantic Ireland died for nothing. The refrain is a funeral cry. Yeats is certain, contemptuous, bitter.

"Romantic Ireland's dead and gone"

Easter 1916 Conflicted Admiration

The middle class Yeats vilified in 1913 are the very people who rose in 1916 and died for Ireland. His view shifts — but he remains conflicted: "Was it needless death after all?"

"A terrible beauty is born"
Element September 1913 Easter 1916
View of middle class Contemptible, greedy, cultureless Capable of heroism and sacrifice — his view reversed
The refrain "Romantic Ireland's dead and gone" — despair "A terrible beauty is born" — paradox, ambivalence
Tone Certain, furious, contemptuous Conflicted, questioning, humble
Form Rigid 4×8 stanzas — ordered argument Varied stanza length — expanding, evolving thought
Key heroes O'Leary, Emmet, Fitzgerald — dead historical figures Pearse, MacBride, Connolly — contemporaries, newly martyred

The Most Important Link in the Set

The shift from September 1913 to Easter 1916 is one of the most dramatic reversals in modern Irish poetry. The very people Yeats dismissed as shopkeepers — "fumbling in a greasy till" — became the martyrs he would immortalise in verse. Always discuss these two poems together. The shift reveals Yeats's intellectual honesty and willingness to change his mind.

🎨 Art, Soul & Immortality

A core Yeatsian tension: the body dies, but can the soul — through art — achieve something lasting? This question runs most explicitly through Byzantium and An Acre of Grass, but touches every poem.

Byzantium Art as Literal Immortality

The most explicit argument. The golden bird sings of "what is past, or passing, or to come" — it transcends time. The poem itself is an "artifice of eternity." Yeats wants to become his own greatest poem.

"the artifice of eternity"

Acre of Grass Art as Survival in the Now

Less philosophical than Byzantium — more urgent, earthly. He does not want to transcend time; he wants to create fiercely while still alive. Art is not eternal gold; it is the "frenzy" of the eagle mind still working.

"Myself must I remake"

Wild Swans Art Hinted at Through Symbols

The swans may represent Yeats's creative energy — when they leave, will his inspiration leave too? The poem's question is not just about love but about whether creativity and passion will survive his ageing.

"Mysterious, beautiful"

Easter 1916 The Poet's Duty to Name

"I write it out in a verse" — Yeats performs his role as national poet, using art to preserve the martyrs' memory. Poetry is commemoration, monument-building, the form of immortality available to ordinary heroes.

"I write it out in a verse"

💔 Love, Loneliness & Unrequited Desire

Maud Gonne haunts almost every poem, even when she is not named. Yeats spent thirty years proposing to her, being rejected, loving her. The loneliness this created is the emotional underpinning of his greatest work.

Wild Swans Most Directly About Loneliness

The 59 swans — one alone without a partner. The "lighter tread" of youth when love was possible. The heart "sore." This is Yeats's most plaintive expression of what unrequited love does over nineteen years.

"my heart is sore"

Easter 1916 Maud Gonne's Shadow

Countess Markievicz stands partly for Maud Gonne — a beautiful woman whose sweetness was ruined by politics. "Her voice grew shrill." The stone metaphor may reference Maude Gonne directly — sacrificing her humanity to a cause.

"Her voice grew shrill"

Byzantium Love as Burden on the Soul

The heart is "sick with desire" — still longing, still aching. Yeats wants the holy fire to burn away his desires. Love itself has become a burden, something he wants to be freed from rather than fulfilled in.

"sick with desire"

Innisfree Solitude by Choice

No other person is mentioned in Innisfree — Yeats wants to be alone with nature. This may already encode the isolation of someone who has not found partnership. The poem is about peace, but it is a solitary peace.

"live alone in the bee-loud glade"

⚡ Yeats's Core Tensions — An Overview

Yeats saw the world as composed of conflicting opposites. These tensions are not resolved in the poems — they are held, explored, and left for the reader. The examiner rewards students who can name and discuss these tensions.

Tension Poems where it appears The conflict
Youth / Age SwansByzAcre The young are vibrant but dying; the old are experienced but diminished. Neither state is complete.
Body / Soul ByzAcre The body decays and imprisons. The soul can sing, transcend, endure — but only if it is active and passionate.
Nature / Art InnisfreeByzSwans Nature is beautiful but dying. Art is artificial but eternal. Innisfree chooses nature; Byzantium chooses art.
Personal / Public SeptEasterSwans The private grief of unrequited love; the public drama of nationalism. They meet in Easter 1916 — personal dislike of MacBride overridden by public admiration for his sacrifice.
Romantic Past / Degraded Present Sept 1913Easter 1916 In 1913, Romantic Ireland is dead. In 1916, the present generates its own heroism. Yeats's relationship with the present changes entirely between the two poems.
Action / Contemplation Easter 1916Acre The revolutionaries acted; Yeats watched and wrote. In An Acre of Grass, he insists that the creative act is also a form of passionate action — "Shake the dead in their shrouds."
🎓
The Exam Formula

When comparing poems, always name the tension first, then show how each poem approaches it differently. For example: "Both Sailing to Byzantium and An Acre of Grass address the tension between the ageing body and the creative spirit — but where Byzantium seeks transcendence and eternal art, An Acre of Grass insists on earthly, passionate engagement with life while it lasts."
