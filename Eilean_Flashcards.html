🔪 Street

Street
"dangling a knife"
Key image · Click to flip
Street
Verb Choice · Power · Symbol
"Dangling" suggests complete ease with a weapon — she is totally at home with it. The knife is also a phallic symbol: by giving the woman the knife, Ní Ch. gives her the power society usually assigns to men. The man who watches is symbolically powerless.
Street
"dark shining drops in the paving-stones"
Visual imagery · Click to flip
Street
Paradox · Gothic Imagery
Paradoxical: "dark" and "shining" seem to contradict each other, yet combine to create a vivid, visceral image of blood catching the light on stone. Beautiful and horrifying at once — this is Gothic at its finest. We never know if the blood is animal or human.
Street
"The shambles"
Setting · Click to flip
Street
Symbolism · The Woman's Domain
A "shambles" is a place of carnage and slaughter. Yet the butcher's daughter is completely at home here. This is her natural world — she is not frightened by it, she inhabits it. The setting reinforces her power and the man's vulnerability as an outsider.
Street
"red crescent marks... fading to faintest"
Closing image · Click to flip
Street
Imagery · Lunar Symbolism · Mystery
The blood on her feet forms crescent (half-moon) shapes as she climbs the stairs. "Fading to faintest" creates a film-like dissolve — she disappears into another realm, becoming mythical. Combined with Lucina (Moon Goddess), women in Ní Ch. are often connected to lunar imagery.
Street
"half open door"
Threshold image · Click to flip
Street
Symbolism · Ambiguity
The half-open door is perfectly symbolic of the poem's refusal to reveal everything. An invitation and a barrier simultaneously. We can see in, but not fully. The man — and the reader — gets a glimpse, but the full picture is withheld. This is Ní Ch.'s method: respect the reader's imagination.
Street
The ambiguous ending: no resolution
Structure · Click to flip
Street
Open Ending · Mystery
Ní Chuilleanáin refuses to tell us what happens next. Does the man follow? Is she dangerous? This ambiguity is deliberate — the woman remains mysterious and unknowable. It preserves her power. The reader is left to decide: a rare act of trust in the audience.

🌙 Lucina Schynning in Silence of the Night

Lucina
"Without roast meat or music"
Historical allusion · Click to flip
Lucina
Allusion · Dual Meaning · Deprivation
Works on two levels: historically, the Cromwellian suppression of Irish culture (music banned, food destroyed); personally, the absence of joy and sustenance. "Roast meat" = physical nourishment; "music" = spiritual nourishment. Both stripped away in one simple, devastating line.
Lucina
"plague... plague"
Repetition · Click to flip
Lucina
Repetition · Psychological Disturbance
The repetition of "plague" suggests overwhelming, inescapable suffering — not a single event but a condition that will not release her. Its biblical connotations (plagues of Egypt) add a sense of divine test. Yet the ruined house provides respite: the place of destruction is also the place of survival.
Lucina
"sour candle" and "crazed window"
Unsettling imagery · Click to flip
Lucina
Imagery · Strange Adjectives · Unease
"Sour" applied to a candle is unexpected — candles give warmth, but this one has gone rancid, wrong. "Crazed" means cracked/fractured (as in ceramics) but also carries connotations of madness. Together: a world that has broken down, where even objects of comfort have become wrong.
Lucina
"sky growing through the hole in the roof"
Paradox · Click to flip
Lucina
Paradox · Symbolism · Transcendence
The "hole in the roof" is a sign of ruin — yet through it, the vast, infinite sky "grows." The very place of destruction becomes the opening through which something transcendent enters. Ruin as gateway: transformation comes through suffering, not despite it. Central to the poem's message.
Lucina
"the resilient stream running"
Closing image · Click to flip
Lucina
Water Symbolism · Resilience
The stream keeps flowing regardless — it is unstoppable. Water is a traditional symbol of cleansing and rebirth, and here its "resilience" carries the whole poem's argument: life, like water, continues. Despite plague, destruction, and suffering, the stream runs on. Alliteration of "resilient/running" adds quiet confidence.
Lucina
Lucina = Moon Goddess (Diana)
Title · Click to flip
Lucina
Classical Allusion · Archaic Diction · Setting
The title uses archaic Scottish dialect ("schynning" = shining) to create an ancient, timeless quality. Lucina is Diana, the Moon Goddess — powerful, female, associated with transformation. The Moon watches over the suffering woman, a divine feminine presence that cleanses and transforms her.

⛪ Translation

Translation
"where steam danced / around stones and giggled and slipped"
Contrast · Click to flip
Translation
Personification · Devastating Contrast
The steam is personified as joyful — it "dances," "giggles," "slips." But the women cannot do any of these things. They are forbidden to laugh, play or communicate freely. The cruelest irony: the very steam of their labour is more free than they are. Enjambment mimics the endless, continuous work.
Translation
"ridges under the veil"
Visual imagery · Click to flip
Translation
Imagery · Multiple Meanings
The "ridges" may be the bones visible through the thin fabric covering exhumed remains, or it may evoke the ridges of a forehead under a nun's veil — suggesting the institution that imprisoned them. The veil evokes both a wedding veil (joy denied) and the secrecy that hid these women from society.
Translation
"sharp edges of words"
Language · Click to flip
Translation
Metaphor · Language as Weapon
The metaphor makes language physically dangerous. Words like "fallen," "sinful," "unclean" cut and wound. The same sharp words branded these women, stripped their humanity and, likely, commanded their secret burial. Ní Ch. uses the poem to blunt those sharp edges — to reclaim language for these women.
Translation
"rising above the shuffle and hum"
Voice · Click to flip
Translation
Auditory Imagery · The Rising Voice
The "shuffle and hum" is the relentless sound of the laundry — feet shuffling, machinery humming, prayers droning. "Rising above" captures both the difficulty and the aspiration. The voice strains upward, against the weight of noise and routine. The poem itself is this rising voice.
Translation
"washed clean of idiom"
Final stanza · Click to flip
Translation
Metaphor · Irony · Liberation
To be "washed clean of idiom" = freed from the institutional language that branded women as "fallen." The irony is devastating: the laundry used washing as punishment; here washing represents liberation. The same action that oppressed becomes the action that frees. The poem performs what the title promises.
Translation
"the bunched keys I bore"
The nun speaker · Click to flip
Translation
Symbol · Power · Guilt
Keys symbolise control — the power to lock and unlock. In the nun reading, the speaker acknowledges that she "bore" these keys — she was the one who locked the women in. The ceremony at which this poem was read is the moment those keys "slacken and fall." Power is finally, literally, relinquished.

✈️ Death and Engines

Death & Engines
"Soon you will need wings of your own"
Direct address · Click to flip
Death & Engines
Extended Metaphor · Direct Address
Fuses the poem's two subjects: the plane and her dying father. When the plane fails, the passenger must fly on without it. "You" makes this universal — she speaks to her father, to us, to all humans. "Soon" adds urgency without cruelty. The extended plane metaphor runs throughout the poem.
Death & Engines
"Time and life like a knife and fork cross"
Simile · Click to flip
Death & Engines
Domestic Simile · Inevitability
By using the image of a knife and fork, Ní Ch. brings death into the ordinary domestic world. A knife and fork will always cross at some point — inevitable, unremarkable. This is her argument about death: not a special event but a natural crossing. The assonance of "life/like/knife" accelerates the line.
Death & Engines
"images of relief... will fail you some time"
Central message · Click to flip
Death & Engines
Tone · Inevitability · Honesty
The "images of relief" are all our near-misses — the accidents we walked away from, the illnesses we survived. Ní Ch. says: these cannot protect you forever. At some point, relief will not come. The bluntest statement in the poem. The honesty is a form of respect for the dying person.
Death & Engines
"you know how light your death is"
Tone shift · Click to flip
Death & Engines
Tone Shift · Tenderness · Comfort
After the bleakness, this line arrives as a tender surprise. "Light" contrasts with the heaviness of everything else. The moment of death will be gentle, swift. This is a gift: an assurance that the moment itself will not be heavy or terrible. "Know" is not a question or hope — it is certainty.
Death & Engines
"scattered like a wreckage... lodge in the hearts"
Closing image · Click to flip
Death & Engines
Simile · Memory · Consolation
The plane metaphor returns: death scatters us like wreckage. Yet the pieces don't disappear — they "lodge in the hearts / Of all who love you." Wreckage usually means waste; here it becomes continuing presence. "Lodge" suggests permanence. The poem ends with love, not loss.
Death & Engines
Change of tense: past → present
Structure · Click to flip
Death & Engines
Tense Shift · Drama · Immediacy
The poem moves from past tense (describing the plane wreck) to present tense (addressing death directly). A dramatic, deliberate shift. Death is not historical — it is happening now. The present tense creates an uncomfortable immediacy that the reader cannot escape.

🌿 The Bend in the Road

Bend in Road
"tree like a cat's tail"
Simile · Click to flip
Bend in Road
Simile · Animating Nature
Surprising and slightly playful: a cat's tail is flexible, self-willed, alive. The tree has grown as it chooses, without human direction, arching over the road. By giving the tree personality, Ní Ch. suggests that nature is active, not passive — it absorbs and holds the lives of those who pass by it.
Bend in Road
"green creeper"
Sound · Click to flip
Bend in Road
Assonance · Time Passing
The repeated "ee" sounds create a slow, insidious quality — mimicking the plant's quiet, persistent growth: unobserved, unannounced. A metaphor for time: it accumulates while we're not watching. When we look back, everything has changed.
Bend in Road
"as silent as it ever was"
Tone · Click to flip
Bend in Road
Comparison · Permanence vs. Change
The bend is exactly as it has always been — unchanged by years, by deaths, by the child's illness. This permanence is contrasted with the radical changes in the speaker's human world. The bend does not mourn; it simply persists. The world carries on regardless of our suffering.
Bend in Road
"in the tree, in the air"
Memory · Click to flip
Bend in Road
Imagery · Death in Nature · Consolation
Repetition of "in" places the dead physically inside the natural world — not above it or beyond it but within it. "In the tree" = within living wood. "In the air" = in every breath. The people we have lost are woven into the places we share with them. Loss is not erasure.
Bend in Road
"wrapped and sealed"
Death imagery · Click to flip
Bend in Road
Precision · Care
Almost commercial in its precision — yet it carries a sense of care: they have been carefully preserved. The phrase is both clinical (the reality of burial) and tender (the care taken). The poem refuses melodrama: the dead are treated with the same quiet attention as everything else in the poem.
Bend in Road
"piled weight of sleep"
Death metaphor · Click to flip
Bend in Road
Metaphor · Weight and Softness
"Piled weight" suggests heaviness, accumulation, pressing down. Yet "sleep" softens this — death as rest, not destruction. The "piling" suggests layers of tiredness accumulating over time: death comes not as a single blow but as a slow gathering. Tender and unusual.

🌟 To Niall Woods and Xenya Ostrovskaia

Niall Woods
"You see the same star"
Opening line · Click to flip
Niall Woods
Symbolism · Fate
From different countries, different cultures, both Niall and Xenya look up and see the same star. The star is a symbol of fate and destiny — something universal, shared without knowing, connecting them across distance. The marriage is cosmically ordained. The simplicity of the image gives it power.
Niall Woods
"leave behind... leave behind"
Repetition · Click to flip
Niall Woods
Repetition · Necessary Loss
The repetition insists on the necessary losses of marriage: old places, old ways, old identities. What is left behind is not abandoned — it is carried forward in memory and culture. The repetition makes this act of leaving feel significant, deliberate, not casual.
Niall Woods
"the cat speaks Irish and Russian"
Cultural identity · Click to flip
Niall Woods
Symbolism · Cultural Identity · Humour
Gently funny — a bilingual cat — but seriously meaningful. Culture lives in the ordinary domestic world. Their languages and traditions don't disappear when they become one household; they coexist. The cat — always independent, never fully domesticated — represents the living, untamed spirit of their cultures.
Niall Woods
"half a loaf and a mother's blessing"
Sacrifice · Click to flip
Niall Woods
Folk Saying · Sacrifice · Blessing
A folk saying: the mother gives up material support ("half a loaf") to give the most valuable gift: her blessing. She lets her son go so she can give him everything. Physical sacrifice becomes spiritual gift. The simplicity of "bread and blessing" grounds an enormous emotional act in the everyday.
Niall Woods
"You will... you will... you will"
Anaphora · Click to flip
Niall Woods
Anaphora · Prophecy · Authority
"You will" is the language of a prophet or fairy godmother — not a hope or wish, but a certainty. Repeated throughout, it gives the speaker the authority of someone who knows how the story ends. She is the storyteller who controls the narrative. Her certainty is the poem's greatest gift to the couple.
Niall Woods
"lived happily ever after."
Final isolated line · Click to flip
Niall Woods
Allusion · Isolated Line · Blessing
By isolating this line, Ní Ch. gives it the weight of a genuine blessing, not a cliché. In the context of all the allusions — the Firebird, Ruth, the King of Ireland's Son — this ending has been earned. The storyteller-mother is not offering a platitude but a real conclusion. The full stop is definitive.
