Fiacha na Toitíní

Twelve-year-old Maidhc, addicted to cigarettes, collecting tobacco from cigarette butts, stealing eggs to fund the habit, and a dead-chick disaster that brings Dainín's fury — mischief with real consequences.

Toitíní / Cigarettes Eddie Hutch Cleasaíocht / Trickery Bochtanas / Poverty Athair / Father

📋 Background & Overview

Maidhc is twelve years old in this chapter. At the bottom of a bog, the local boys gathered every evening to smoke. Smoking publicly was seen as a rite of passage — you were considered grown-up enough when you were sixteen. But Maidhc and his friends couldn't wait that long.

His friend Eddie Hutch always had cigarettes from his uncle. Maidhc visited Eddie's house regularly to get them, and the chapter tells the story of his growing addiction and increasingly desperate schemes to fund his habit — including collecting tobacco from discarded cigarette butts in the Parish Hall, cutting hair from horses' tails to sell, and eventually stealing eggs from a hen. The egg theft backfires spectacularly when the creamery manager arrives at the house to report that the eggs contained dead chicks.

🎯 Core Themes in this Chapter

  • The Outlook of Youth (Dearcadh na hÓige): Smoking at twelve, stealing eggs, and hiding tobacco — all show the boldness and recklessness of youth.
  • Friends' Influence (Tionchar Cairde): Eddie Hutch is directly responsible for Maidhc's addiction to cigarettes — a clear example of how friends influenced Maidhc's life.
  • Poverty (An Bochtanas): The lengths Maidhc goes to in order to get money for cigarettes — collecting butts, selling horse hair, stealing — all reflect a world where children had almost no money.
  • Consequences of Mischief: Unlike earlier chapters, the mischief here has real consequences — Dainín is furious, Maidhc has to flee the house.
  • Humour (An Greann): The egg/dead chicks story is brilliantly funny — Maidhc's scheme collapsing in the most unexpected way.
🎓
Exam Approach

Chapter 5 is one of the most frequently used chapters in exam answers. It is excellent for: friends' influence (Eddie Hutch directly causing the addiction), humour (the dead chicks disaster), the outlook of youth (smoking at twelve, the cigarette but collecting), and mischief and its consequences. It also shows the poverty of the time clearly.

Key Scenes — Analysis + Irish Phrases to Use
Scene 1 — Eddie Hutch and the Cigarette Addiction
Bhíodh Maidhc ag fáil toitíní ó uncail Eddie Hutch. Fuair andúil ag Maidhc le toitíní agus sin mar thoradh ar an gcairdeas a bhí aige le hEddie.
Maidhc used to get cigarettes from Eddie Hutch's uncle. Maidhc got addicted to cigarettes and that is a result of the friendship he had with Eddie.

Analysis (English)

This is the most direct example in the entire book of a friend having a negative influence on Maidhc's life. Eddie Hutch's uncle is the source of the cigarettes, but it is the friendship with Eddie that puts Maidhc in the position to access them. The word andúil (addiction) is striking — Maidhc frames this as a genuine addiction, not just casual experimentation. What starts as an exciting, adult-feeling activity leads to sickness, theft, and his father's fury. Eddie's influence is pivotal to everything that follows in this chapter.

Scene 2 — Maidhc Gets Sick from Smoking
Bhraith sé an-aosta leis na toitíní ach d'éirigh sé breoite. D'imigh sé abhaile ach ag boithrín bheag chaith sé gach rud suas. Cheap sé go raibh sé chun bás a fháil.
He felt very grown-up with the cigarettes but he became ill. He went home but at a small lane he vomited everything up. He thought he was going to die.

Analysis (English)

This is a wonderfully comic and relatable scene — the twelve-year-old trying to feel grown-up, only for his body to immediately and dramatically refuse. The contrast between how he felt (an-aosta — very mature) and what actually happened (vomiting on a lane, convinced he was dying) is perfectly funny. It is also a very human moment — almost every reader can relate to the experience of trying something that was supposed to make you seem older and immediately regretting it. Maidhc tells the story against himself with characteristic honesty and humour.

Scene 3 — Collecting Butts in the Parish Hall
Gach Luan rachadh Maidhc agus Eddie go dtí Halla an Pharóiste chun an tobac as na buiteanna a bhailiú agus a chur i bhfolach i mbosca stain sa chlaí.
Every Monday Maidhc and Eddie used to go to the Parish Hall to collect the tobacco from the cigarette butts and hide it in a tin box in the fence.

Analysis (English)

This detail perfectly captures the desperation and ingenuity of the boys' situation. They have no money to buy cigarettes, so they collect the leftover tobacco from discarded butts in the parish hall — carefully hiding their stash in a tin box in a fence. The fact that this happens every Monday shows it was a regular, organised operation, not a one-off event. It reveals both the poverty of the time and the extraordinary lengths that boys will go to for something they are obsessed with.

Scene 4 — Stealing Eggs and the Dead Chicks Disaster
Bhí Maidhc i ngort lá áirithe nuair a tháinig cearc amach as sceach. Ghoid sé na huibheacha agus dhíol sé iad leis an uachtarlann. Cúpla lá ina dhiaidh sin, thug bainisteoir na huachtarlainne cuairt ar tigh Mhaidhc — sicíní a bhí sna huibheacha agus bhíodar go léir marbh.
Maidhc was in a field one day when a hen came out of a hedge. He stole the eggs and sold them to the creamery. A few days later, the creamery manager visited Maidhc's house — the eggs were fertilised and all the chicks were dead.

Analysis (English)

This is the comic climax of the chapter and one of the funniest stories in the book. Maidhc's scheme — stealing eggs to sell for cigarette money — seems clever enough. What he doesn't know is that the eggs were fertilised. When the creamery manager arrives at the door to report that all the "eggs" turned out to be dead chicks, Maidhc's elaborate scheme collapses in the most absurd way possible. The scene is funny because of the sheer unexpectedness of the consequence — who could have anticipated that particular outcome? Dainín is furious, and Maidhc is forced to flee the house.

Key Quotes — with Analysis and Irish Phrases
Quote 1 — The Cigarette Debt (Fiacha na Toitíní)
Ba mhinic a ghearr siad an ghruaig ó eireaball na gcapall nó ghoid siad uibheacha chun díol le haghaidh airgead.
It was often that they cut the hair from horses' tails or stole eggs to sell for money.

Analysis (English)

This list of schemes — cutting horse-tail hair, stealing eggs — captures the inventive desperation of twelve-year-olds with no pocket money. Each scheme is more outlandish than the last, and together they build a picture of boys who are genuinely creative in their mischief. The list also implicitly shows the poverty of the time: if the boys had any money, none of this would be necessary. The detail about horse-tail hair is particularly memorable and specific — it is the kind of vivid, unexpected detail that makes Maidhc's storytelling so distinctive.

Themes in Chapter 5

👬 Friends' Influence

Eddie Hutch is the single most important influence in this chapter — without him, Maidhc would never have had access to cigarettes or developed his addiction. The chapter is a clear, sustained example of how a friend can lead you astray. Tá an-tionchar ag cairde Mhaidhc ar a shaol.

🧒 Outlook of Youth

Trying to feel grown-up by smoking; hiding tobacco in a tin box; thinking you're dying after your first cigarette — all of this is perfectly captured adolescent behaviour. The gap between how mature Maidhc wants to feel and how his body actually reacts is the heart of the chapter's humour.

💸 Poverty

Every scheme in this chapter is driven by the same root cause: no money. Collecting butts, selling horse hair, stealing eggs — these are the actions of children with absolutely no financial resources. The poverty of the era is never more clearly shown than through the cigarette debt schemes.

😂 Humour with Consequences

Unlike earlier chapters where mischief is punished at school, here the consequences come from Dainín at home. The dead chicks disaster is brilliantly funny precisely because the consequence was so unpredictable — and Maidhc's flight from the house to avoid his father's fury is a perfect comic ending.

Which Essay Questions Suit Chapter 5?
  • "Bhí tionchar ag cairde Mhaidhc ar a shaol" — Eddie Hutch and the cigarette addiction is the strongest example in the book. Use it with direct Irish quotes.
  • "Tá spraoi agus dearcadh na hóige léirithe" — Smoking at twelve, the sickness, the schemes — all show the reckless boldness of youth.
  • "Tá greann agus brón araon le feiceáil" — Greann: the dead chicks; the sickness on the lane. Brón: poverty forcing the boys to steal; Dainín's fury.
  • "Cuntas taitneamhach ar chuid scolaíochta agus a chuid cleasaíochta" — This chapter is the richest in cleasaíocht (trickery) — use all four schemes.

📝 Model Paragraph — Friends' Influence (Eddie Hutch)

Ceann eile de chairde Mhaidhc ná Eddie Hutch. (Another of Maidhc's friends is Eddie Hutch.) Bhíodh Maidhc agus Eddie ag fáil toitíní ó uncail Eddie. (Maidhc and Eddie used to get cigarettes from Eddie's uncle.) Fuair andúil ag Maidhc le toitíní agus sin mar thoradh ar an gcairdeas a bhí aige le hEddie. (Maidhc got an addiction to cigarettes and that is a result of his friendship with Eddie.) Oíche amháin, chaith sé toitín agus tháinig tinneas air — chaith sé suas agus cheap sé go raibh sé ag fáil bháis. (One night he smoked a cigarette and became sick — he threw up and thought he was dying.) Chomh maith leis sin, gach Luan rachadh Maidhc agus Eddie go dtí Halla an Pharóiste chun an tobac as na buiteanna a bhailiú. (As well as that, every Monday Maidhc and Eddie would go to the Parish Hall to collect the tobacco from the butts.) Mar fhocail scoir, is léir go raibh tionchar mór ag Eddie Hutch ar shaol Mhaidhc. (To conclude, it is clear that Eddie Hutch had a big influence on Maidhc's life.)

Top Exam Tip — Chapter 5

Chapter 5 contains the most Irish phrases worth learning in the whole book because so many questions can be answered using it. Learn the Eddie Hutch paragraph above by heart — it covers friendship influence, the outlook of youth, humour, and poverty all in one go. The dead chicks story is your stand-out comic anecdote for any humour question.

😢
The Sadness in Chapter 5

Behind the comedy, poverty forces twelve-year-old children to steal and to collect tobacco from the ground. Maidhc flees his own home in fear of his father. For a modern reader, the image of a child hiding from a parent's anger is quietly sad, even if Maidhc presents it with humour. Don't forget to acknowledge this in your answer for a balanced response.
