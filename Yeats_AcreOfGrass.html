Poem 6 — Last Poems — 1936

An Acre of Grass

At 71, in his last home, Yeats rejects quiet decline and prays for the passionate frenzy that great art demands — even in old age.

Old AgeCreative FrenzyRejection of StillnessEagle MindAllusions

📋 Background & Overview

Written in 1936 when Yeats was 71, set in Riversdale — his final home in Rathfarnham, Dublin. Published posthumously in Last Poems. He was an old man in a quiet house, knowing this would be his last residence. The poem takes stock and then explodes against what it finds.

Instead of accepting the gentle decline his circumstances suggest, Yeats prays for "frenzy" — passionate, even mad, creative intensity. He invokes Blake, Michelangelo, Lear, and Timon as models of old men who raged magnificently and created powerfully right to the end.

🎯 Core Themes

  • Rejection of Passive Old Age: The quiet still life of stanza 1 is not contentment — it is death-in-life. Yeats refuses it.
  • The Need for Frenzy: Creativity requires passion, intensity, even a little madness. "Frenzy" is essential, not frightening.
  • Art as Remaking the Self: "Myself must I remake" — to create is simultaneously to transform oneself.
  • The Eagle Mind: The body is diminished; the mind can still be eagle-sharp — far-seeing, powerful, defiant.
  • Heroic Old Age: Blake, Michelangelo, Lear, Timon — figures of magnificent, passionate old age that Yeats aspires to join.
🎓
Key Contrast to Know

This poem is a fascinating reversal of Innisfree. As a young man Yeats longed for peace and quiet solitude. Now that quiet has arrived, it is intolerable: "My temptation is quiet." He got what he wished for — and it is not enough. This reversal across a lifetime is one of the most striking developments in his work.

📚 The Four Allusions

  • William Blake (1757–1827): Poet, artist, mystic — created radical, visionary work until death at 70. "Beat upon the wall / Till Truth obeyed his call."
  • Michelangelo (1475–1564): Painted Sistine Chapel ceiling in his 30s; designed St Peter's Basilica in his 80s. Worked with ferocious energy to nearly 90.
  • King Lear: Shakespeare's old king who raged magnificently — "Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks!" — before descending into glorious madness.
  • Timon of Athens: Shakespeare's noble who became a passionate misanthrope, raging against ingratitude. Passionate even in bitterness.
Stanza 1
Picture and book remain,
An acre of green grass
For air and exercise,
Now strength of body goes;
Midnight, an old house
Where nothing stirs but a mouse.
Stanza 2
My temptation is quiet.
Here at life's end
Neither loose imagination,
Nor the mill of the mind
Consuming its rag and bone,
Can make the truth known.
Stanza 3
Grant me an old man's frenzy,
Myself must I remake
Till I am Timon and Lear
Or that William Blake
Who beat upon the wall
Till Truth obeyed his call;
Stanza 4
A mind Michael Angelo knew
That can pierce the clouds,
Or inspired by frenzy
Shake the dead in their shrouds;
Forgotten else by mankind,
An old man's eagle mind.
Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis
"Midnight, an old house / Where nothing stirs but a mouse."

A bare inventory: one picture, one book, one acre of grass. Everything singular, everything shrinking. "Now strength of body goes" — stated plainly, without self-pity. The closing image is almost comic in its desolation: midnight, an old house, and the only movement is a mouse. This bathetic image establishes the urgency of what follows. This is what Yeats is fighting against.

Crucially, Yeats is aware that this is what he once longed for — Innisfree promised "nine bean-rows" and solitude in nature. Now he has the solitude and finds it intolerable. The reversal is the poem's secret irony.

"Neither loose imagination, / Nor the mill of the mind / Consuming its rag and bone, / Can make the truth known."

"My temptation is quiet" — an extraordinarily honest line. Yeats is tempted not by vice but by surrender, by rest, by letting go. He recognises this as the enemy of creation.

He then diagnoses two inadequate mental states. "Loose imagination" is unfocused, drifting — it generates daydreams, not truth. The "mill of the mind" is the other extreme: grinding, obsessive, consuming its own "rag and bone" — the disordered scraps of thought and emotion — without producing anything of value. Neither passivity nor anxious churning will create great work. What is needed is something else: frenzy.

"Grant me an old man's frenzy, / Myself must I remake / Till I am Timon and Lear / Or that William Blake / Who beat upon the wall / Till Truth obeyed his call"

"Grant me" — a formal prayer. Yeats asks for what he cannot generate alone: passionate intensity, the gift of frenzy. "Frenzy" is not a negative word here — it is what great art demands.

"Myself must I remake" — the inverted syntax gives this archaic force. Creating great poetry at 71 requires first transforming oneself — refusing the quiet old man he currently is. The models he invokes are extraordinary: Lear and Timon both raged magnificently against injustice. Blake "beat upon the wall / Till Truth obeyed his call" — truth does not come easily; it must be beaten out of the universe. The rhyme "wall/call" drives home the relentlessness required.

"Shake the dead in their shrouds; / Forgotten else by mankind, / An old man's eagle mind."

The final stanza reaches the poem's resolution. A mind like Michelangelo's can "pierce the clouds" — transcend ordinary limitation. Inspired by frenzy, art so powerful it can "Shake the dead in their shrouds" — disturb even the dead with its power. This is the highest possible ambition for a poem.

"Forgotten else by mankind" — without this frenzy, he will simply be forgotten. He is in his last home with his mouse. But with "an old man's eagle mind" — the resolution arrives in four words. The eagle: sharp, far-seeing, powerful, predatory. Age cannot take the eagle's sight or its power to soar. The body is old; the mind can still be eagle. The poem ends not in surrender but in absolute defiance.

Key Quotes
BathosSettingContrast
Technique: Bathetic image — the urgent need for frenzy made visible
"Midnight, an old house / Where nothing stirs but a mouse."

Almost comic in its quiet desolation. This single image — a mouse as the only movement — establishes everything. The reader immediately understands why frenzy is needed. The bathos is entirely deliberate: Yeats is staring at the abyss of insignificance and refusing it.

Honest ConfessionToneSelf-Awareness
Technique: Startling self-revelation — naming the real enemy
"My temptation is quiet."

Not tempted by vice or excess — tempted by quiet itself. This is the most honest line in the late poems. Part of him wants to give in, to rest, to stop. He names this temptation precisely because he refuses to yield to it. The poem is the act of resistance against this very temptation.

PrayerTone ShiftDefiance
Technique: Formal prayer — the emotional pivot of the poem
"Grant me an old man's frenzy"

"Grant me" is a formal petitionary prayer — humble yet urgent. "Frenzy" is not a word normally associated with prayer, yet Yeats prays for it because rational, controlled thinking cannot produce great art. The poem's tone shifts entirely at this line: from quiet inventory to passionate demand.

Inverted SyntaxAlliterationDetermination
Technique: Inverted syntax giving archaic, forceful authority
"Myself must I remake"

Inverted syntax — "Myself must I" not "I must remake myself" — gives the line an Old Testament force, as if it is an ancient command rather than a personal resolution. Alliteration of "m" hammers it home. To create great art, Yeats must first transform the person he currently is. Creation is self-transformation.

AllusionPhysical MetaphorRhyme
Technique: Violent physical metaphor for creative relentlessness
"that William Blake / Who beat upon the wall / Till Truth obeyed his call"

Blake "beats" — a violent, physical verb for creative work. Truth does not come willingly; it must be hammered out of the universe through relentless effort. The rhyme of "wall/call" drives home the determination. This is Yeats's model: not gentle inspiration but furious, physical insistence on truth.

SymbolResolutionDefiance
Technique: Eagle as symbol of power that age cannot diminish
"An old man's eagle mind."

The poem's resolution in four words. Eagles: sharp, far-seeing, powerful, predatory, soaring. "Old man's" acknowledges the body's limitations — but "eagle mind" insists the mind can transcend them. Age is in the body; transcendence is in the mind. The poem ends not in acceptance but in absolute defiance — the eagle does not apologise for its age.

Poetic Techniques

📏 Form: Six-line Stanzas

Four stanzas of six lines each, rhyming approximately abcbdd. The compact, structured form reflects the building argument — from diagnosis to resolution. The closing couplet drives home each stage. Slant rhyme in stanza 1 mirrors the unsatisfactory situation; full rhyme develops as his resolution hardens.

💪 Active Physical Verbs

In the second half: "beat," "shake," "pierce." These are the verbs of passion and action, contrasting with the stillness of the opening. Yeats uses verbs to enact the frenzy he is praying for — the language becomes what it describes.

📚 Four Allusions

Blake, Michelangelo, Lear, Timon — all models of passionate, raging old age. The allusions elevate the poem from personal complaint to a universal statement about the dignity of the creative spirit. They also show Yeats as deeply learned, placing himself in a great tradition.

🔊 Alliteration as Chant

"Myself must I remake," "Blake / Who beat," "Shake the dead in their shrouds" — the alliteration creates a hammer-beat rhythm, almost like a chant or incantation. The sound enacts the frenzy the words describe. The poem performs its own argument.

🦅 Eagle Symbol

Eagles: power, vision, the bird of Zeus. Associated with divine authority, far sight, predatory power. An eagle remains an eagle even when old. "Eagle mind" makes the crucial argument: intellectual and creative power are not subject to physical decline. The mind can still soar.

⚡ Contrast: Stanza 1 vs Stanza 4

The mouse of stanza 1 versus the eagle of stanza 4 — the poem's entire arc in two images. From the most diminished creature of domestic stillness to the most powerful symbol of soaring vision. The journey between these two images is the journey of the poem.

Context & Exam Preparation
🎓
Key Exam Arguments

Two main angles: (1) Contrast with earlier poems: Yeats once longed for Innisfree's quiet. Now quiet is the enemy. This shows his development across a lifetime. (2) Defiance of age: Where Wild Swans laments, An Acre of Grass fights. Always use the eagle image as the poem's resolution and contrast it with the mouse of stanza 1. The poem's entire argument lives in those two images.

Links to Other Yeats Poems

  • The Lake Isle of Innisfree: The reversal. Young Yeats longed for peace and quiet solitude. Old Yeats finds that quiet intolerable — "My temptation is quiet." He got what he wished for, and it was not enough.
  • Sailing to Byzantium: Both are late poems about old age and art. Byzantium seeks transcendence and eternal art; An Acre of Grass insists on fierce earthly creative passion. One looks to eternity; the other insists on now.
  • The Wild Swans at Coole: Both are poems of old age. Swans laments what cannot be changed; Acre of Grass refuses to accept diminishment and fights back. Two different emotional responses to the same predicament.
💡
The Essential Quote Pair

Open with "My temptation is quiet" — the poem's most honest admission. Close with "an old man's eagle mind" — its resolution and triumph. The entire argument of the poem lives in the distance between these two phrases: from temptation to acceptance of quiet, to the defiant assertion that the mind can still soar like an eagle.
