Poem 5 · The Tower · 1928

Sailing to Byzantium

An aged man sails away from mortal Ireland toward the eternal holy city — a visionary meditation on art, soul, immortality, and the decaying body.

Art & Immortality Body vs Soul Ageing Escapism Mysticism

📋 Background & Overview

This is the opening poem of The Tower (1928), widely regarded as Yeats's greatest collection. He was over 60 when he wrote it, married, with children, but acutely conscious of his ageing body and mortality. The poem projects his fears onto a persona — an "aged man" — who sails on an imaginary voyage to Byzantium, the ancient city he saw as the holy centre of Western civilisation.

Yeats believed Byzantium was the meeting point of East and West, and that its art — golden mosaics, intricate craftsmanship — represented the closest humans had come to creating something eternal. His voyage is spiritual, not literal: he seeks transcendence through art.

🎯 Core Themes

  • Body vs Soul: The central conflict. The body decays, ages, becomes a "tattered coat upon a stick." But the soul is immortal and can be strengthened through art and intellect.
  • Art as Immortality: Great art outlasts human bodies. The golden mosaics of Byzantium will exist long after their creators are dead. Yeats wants to become — metaphorically — a work of art.
  • Escapism: Like Innisfree but far more extreme. This time, Yeats is not escaping to nature but to eternity itself — to a realm beyond mortal decay.
  • Young/Old, Time/Eternity: The mortal country of the first stanza is full of "the young in one another's arms" — vibrant but dying. Byzantium represents the timeless, the eternal, the intellectual.
  • The Singing School: Art, creativity, and the soul's expression are what give the aged body value. "Sing" and "singing" run through the poem as a thread connecting soul to immortality.

🏛️ What was Byzantium?

The Byzantine Empire lasted from roughly 330 to 1453 AD — a continuation of the Roman Empire in its eastern provinces. Its capital Constantinople (now Istanbul) was the meeting point of European and Asian culture. Byzantine art was famous for its extraordinary gold mosaics, intricate religious imagery, and timeless quality — art that seemed to transcend individual human creation. Yeats believed that Celtic art (including the Book of Kells) was influenced by Byzantine art, making the journey from Ireland to Byzantium a cultural homecoming as well as a spiritual voyage.

Key Exam Approach

There are three main exam angles for this poem: (1) escapism — the desire to leave the mortal world behind; (2) art and immortality — the poem argues that great art conquers death; (3) the relationship between body and soul. You can discuss all three, but choose the one you know best and develop it with close quotation.

Stanza 1 — The Country (Ireland)
That is no country for old men. The young
In one another's arms, birds in the trees,
— Those dying generations — at their song,
The salmon-falls, the mackerel-crowded seas,
Fish, flesh, or fowl, commend all summer long
Whatever is begotten, born, and dies.
Caught in that sensual music all neglect
Monuments of unageing intellect.
Stanza 2 — The Old Man
An aged man is but a paltry thing,
A tattered coat upon a stick, unless
Soul clap its hands and sing, and louder sing
For every tatter in its mortal dress,
Nor is there singing school but studying
Monuments of its own magnificence;
And therefore I have sailed the seas and come
To the holy city of Byzantium.
Stanza 3 — Prayer to the Sages
O sages standing in God's holy fire
As in the gold mosaic of a wall,
Come from the holy fire, perne in a gyre,
And be the singing-masters of my soul.
Consume my heart away; sick with desire
And fastened to a dying animal
It knows not what it is; and gather me
Into the artifice of eternity.
Stanza 4 — The Golden Bird
Once out of nature I shall never take
My bodily form from any natural thing,
But such a form as Grecian goldsmiths make
Of hammered gold and gold enamelling
To keep a drowsy Emperor awake;
Or set upon a golden bough to sing
To lords and ladies of Byzantium
Of what is past, or passing, or to come.
Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis
"That is no country for old men. The young / In one another's arms, birds in the trees, / — Those dying generations — at their song"

The poem opens mid-journey — Yeats has already left, already sailing. "That" looks back at the country he has departed. Ireland is beautiful — "salmon-falls," "mackerel-crowded seas," the young, the birds, the abundant sensory life. But it is a country of the body, of the flesh, and bodies die. "Those dying generations" — every creature in this vibrant world is mortal. The irony is devastating: the most alive-seeming things are the most dying.

The young are "caught in that sensual music" — they are so absorbed in the pleasures of the body that they neglect "Monuments of unageing intellect." This is Yeats's argument: sensual life is beautiful but temporary. Art and intellect endure. The young cannot see this — but the old man sees it clearly and painfully.

"An aged man is but a paltry thing, / A tattered coat upon a stick, unless / Soul clap its hands and sing"

This is the emotional nadir of the poem — the moment of maximum vulnerability. An old man stripped of the "sensual music" of the first stanza is a scarecrow: "a tattered coat upon a stick." The body is literally falling apart. "Paltry" — worth nothing, pitifully small. This is Yeats confronting his own physical decay with absolute honesty.

But the turn is immediate and urgent: "unless / Soul clap its hands and sing." If the soul is active, creative, singing — then the decaying body is irrelevant. And crucially, the soul must sing "louder sing / For every tatter in its mortal dress" — the more the body decays, the louder the soul must respond. Physical decline drives spiritual and creative intensity. This is the poem's central argument: art compensates for, and ultimately transcends, physical mortality.

"Consume my heart away; sick with desire / And fastened to a dying animal / It knows not what it is; and gather me / Into the artifice of eternity."

In the great Byzantine mosaics, holy figures — sages — stand in golden fire. Yeats addresses them as if they are alive and present, asking them to be the "singing-masters of my soul." This is prayer — urgent, desperate, sincere. He wants guidance for his soul's creative work.

"Consume my heart away" — a shocking request. He wants the holy fire to burn away his human heart, with all its desires and emotions. His heart is "sick with desire" — still longing, still aching (for Maude Gonne, for youth, for what he cannot have). The heart is "fastened to a dying animal" — the body is literally a dying animal dragging the soul down with it. He wants to be free of this. "Gather me / Into the artifice of eternity" — he wants to become part of something eternal and crafted, like the mosaics themselves. "Artifice" is key: not natural, but made. Art is superior to nature because it does not decay.

"Such a form as Grecian goldsmiths make / Of hammered gold and gold enamelling... / Of what is past, or passing, or to come."

In the final stanza, Yeats describes what he would become "once out of nature" — once free of his mortal body. He would take the form of a golden bird, made by craftsmen, set "upon a golden bough to sing." This bird is artificial, eternal, indestructible — the opposite of the "dying animal" his soul is currently fastened to.

The golden bird sings "of what is past, or passing, or to come" — the past, present, and future simultaneously. This is prophecy, omniscience — the bird/poet has transcended time entirely, seeing all of it at once. The repetition of "gold" throughout the stanza is almost overwhelming — golden goldsmiths, hammered gold, gold enamelling, golden bough. This is Yeats's imagination fully unleashed, perhaps even satirising its own excess. Is this too much? Or is this what eternity looks like? Both readings are valid.

Key Quotes
MetaphorVulnerabilityBody
Technique: Dehumanising metaphor — the body as useless scarecrow
"A tattered coat upon a stick"

The most devastating metaphor for old age in all of Yeats. A scarecrow has no life, no soul, no use except to scare away birds. The tattered coat is literally falling apart. The stick is just a stick. This is what age does to the body — reduces it to a scarecrow, an empty shell. But the power is in what comes next: unless the soul sings, the body is nothing. The metaphor sets up the argument.

PersonificationJoySoul
Technique: Physical action of soul — making the intangible tangible
"Soul clap its hands and sing, and louder sing"

The soul is given physical action — it "claps its hands." This is exuberant, joyful, defiant. The soul does not quietly accept the decay of the body; it celebrates, it makes noise, it sings louder as the body falls further apart. The repetition of "sing" — "and louder sing" — suggests escalation, urgency. The worse the body gets, the more urgent the soul's creative response.

MetaphorBody/SoulEntrapment
Technique: Dehumanising metaphor — the body as dying animal
"fastened to a dying animal"

"Fastened" suggests imprisonment, being chained. The soul is chained to the body — the body is not a home but a prison. Worse, it is a "dying animal" — the most graphic possible image of the body's mortality. Animals die messily, slowly, painfully. The soul is attached to this process, unable to free itself. This is Yeats's most visceral expression of the desire to escape physical existence.

ParadoxArtImmortality
Technique: Paradoxical phrase — the made thing as more lasting than the natural
"the artifice of eternity"

A profound paradox. "Artifice" means something made — artificial, crafted, not natural. "Eternity" is the opposite of anything made — it is infinite, uncreated. Yet Yeats puts them together. He argues that art — the made thing — is the closest humans can come to eternity. Natural things die; made things (the golden bird, the mosaic, the poem) endure. Art is the artifice of eternity — the human-made equivalent of the divine permanence Yeats seeks.

TricolonProphecyImmortality
Technique: Tricolon covering all of time — the poet as prophet
"Of what is past, or passing, or to come."

The golden bird sings about the past, the present, and the future — simultaneously. It has transcended time entirely. This is the artist as prophet: not trapped in a single moment, but perceiving and singing the whole of time at once. The three-part structure (past/passing/to come) is elegant and complete. If Yeats's soul becomes this golden bird, his poetry will outlast his body — and it has.

Poetic Techniques

📏 Form: ottava rima

Eight-line stanzas rhyming abababcc — the Italian form used by Ariosto and others. The tight interlocking rhymes reflect the intricate craftsmanship of Byzantine art: the poem is itself a mosaic of sounds and patterns. The closing couplet (cc) in each stanza ties everything together neatly, like a golden clasp.

🎵 Repetition of "Sing"

"Sing," "singing-masters," "soul clap its hands and sing, and louder sing," "set upon a golden bough to sing" — the word runs through the poem as a thread. It connects the soul's creative expression with immortality. Song is what the soul does; song is what the golden bird does; song is what Yeats's poem itself is. The repetition argues that art = soul = immortality.

🔀 Contrast: Young/Old, Body/Soul

Every contrast in the poem is between mortal and immortal: the young and sensual vs the aged and intellectual; the natural and dying vs the artificial and eternal; the "salmon-falls" of the mortal country vs the golden mosaics of Byzantium. Yeats structures the entire poem around this opposition.

🟡 Gold Imagery

The final stanza is saturated with gold: "hammered gold and gold enamelling," "golden bough," "Grecian goldsmiths." Gold is eternal, incorruptible, precious, artificial. The accumulation of gold imagery is almost overwhelming — deliberately so. Eternity is this bright, this artificial, this overwhelming.

⟶ Enjambment

Used to carry energy through lines, particularly in stanza 3 where the prayer is urgent and unstoppable: "Consume my heart away; sick with desire / And fastened to a dying animal / It knows not what it is." The enjambment creates the feeling of desperation, of thoughts tumbling over themselves in urgency.

🔊 Alliteration

"Monuments of its own magnificence" — the "m" sounds create a musical, self-regarding quality appropriate to great art. Harsh "t" sounds emphasise physical pain: "tattered coat upon a stick." Sound always mirrors meaning in Yeats.

Context & Exam Preparation
🎓
Three Exam Angles

1. Escapism: The speaker wants to leave mortal Ireland for eternal Byzantium — escaping the "sensual music" of dying flesh. Compare to Innisfree (simpler escape) and An Acre of Grass (refusal to escape into quiet). 2. Art & Immortality: The poem argues that art outlasts the body. The golden bird is the poem itself — it will sing "of what is past, or passing, or to come" long after Yeats dies. 3. Body/Soul: The central tension. "Fastened to a dying animal" vs "Soul clap its hands and sing" — the body decays but the soul can be liberated through art.

Links to Other Yeats Poems

  • The Wild Swans at Coole: Both deal with ageing. But in Swans, Yeats laments; in Byzantium, he fights back through art. Compare as two responses to the same problem — one elegiac, one defiant.
  • An Acre of Grass: Both are late poems about old age, but Byzantium seeks transcendence through eternity while Acre of Grass seeks "frenzy" and earthly creative passion. Two very different solutions.
  • The Lake Isle of Innisfree: Both are poems of escape. Innisfree escapes to simple nature; Byzantium escapes to art and immortality. One is pastoral, one is metaphysical.
💡
The Key Quote Pair

Use "fastened to a dying animal" alongside "the artifice of eternity" — one is the problem (the body imprisons the soul), the other is the solution (art creates something that outlasts the body). Together they summarise the poem's entire argument.
