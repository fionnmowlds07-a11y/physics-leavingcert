Who Is Macbeth?

🛡️ His Starting Point

We never actually see the noble Macbeth — we only hear about him. The Captain calls him "brave Macbeth" and Duncan calls him his "valiant cousin, worthy gentleman." This matters: Shakespeare builds his greatness through other characters' admiration so that his fall feels all the more devastating.

He is a talented general with a conscience. When the witches give him their prophecy, he is immediately tempted — but he is also immediately horrified by his own temptation. He knows what ambition could lead him to, and for a while, he resists.

⚡ The Central Conflict

Macbeth's tragedy is not that he is a simple villain. It is that he is a moral person who makes an immoral choice — and keeps making it. His great soliloquy in Act 1.7 shows him reasoning through every argument against murdering Duncan and concluding he has "no spur" to do it. Then Lady Macbeth walks in, and he does it anyway.

This is Shakespeare's devastating insight: people can fully understand that something is wrong and still be swept along by desire. Macbeth's tragedy is a tragedy of free will, not fate.

📉 The Deterioration

What makes Macbeth uniquely compelling is the speed and completeness of his moral collapse. By Act 3, he is ordering murders without the anguished deliberation of Act 1. By Act 5, he has reached nihilism — life "signifies nothing." Shakespeare shows us not just a man who commits evil but a man destroyed by it from the inside out.

💡
Leaving Cert Angle

The key argument about Macbeth: he is responsible for his own downfall. The witches give him a prophecy, Lady Macbeth manipulates him — but Banquo receives the same prophecy and resists. Macbeth chooses. This is the most important thing to establish in any exam answer.

7 Key Quotes — Short, Learnable, Deeply Analysed
1
Stars, hide your fires
"Stars, hide your fires; / Let not light see my black and deep desires."
Act 1, Scene 4 — Macbeth, aside, after Duncan names Malcolm as heir
📍 Context — What's happening

Duncan has just announced his son Malcolm will inherit the throne. Macbeth had expected to be next in line. In this one aside, all his secret ambition surfaces.

🔍 Technique — What Shakespeare does

Personification and apostrophe — Macbeth addresses the stars directly. Darkness and light are used throughout the play to represent evil and goodness.

💡 Key Insight — What this really means

This quote proves that Macbeth's ambition exists before the witches, before Lady Macbeth. He calls them his own "black and deep desires" — possessive, personal. He is already thinking about murder. But he wants darkness to hide what he is about to feel. His conscience is still alive; he is ashamed of his own desires. This is the last moment we see him trying to suppress what he has become.

🎭 Effect on Audience

The aside creates dramatic irony — we know what Macbeth is thinking while Duncan stands beside him. We become unwilling witnesses to murder being planned.

📝 Use In Exam For

Ambition (his own, not externally caused), Free will vs fate, Appearance vs Reality (he hides his thoughts from Duncan).

2
Vaulting ambition
"I have no spur / To prick the sides of my intent, but only / Vaulting ambition."
Act 1, Scene 7 — Macbeth's soliloquy, just before the murder
📍 Context — What's happening

Macbeth is alone before the royal banquet. He spends the whole soliloquy arguing himself out of killing Duncan — Duncan is his king, kinsman, and guest. He finally concludes he has no justified reason to act.

🔍 Technique — What Shakespeare does

Extended metaphor from horse-riding: a rider who vaults too high overshoots the saddle and falls. The image predicts Macbeth's entire arc — reach too far, fall hard.

💡 Key Insight — What this really means

This is the most important speech in the play for the theme of free will. Macbeth goes through careful moral reasoning and arrives at the right answer — "I'll proceed no further in this business." Then Lady Macbeth enters, manipulates him, and he reverses his decision. Shakespeare is showing that he could have chosen differently. The fact that he arrives at "I should not do this" and then does it anyway makes his guilt entirely self-chosen. He is not a victim of fate or manipulation — he is a man who knows better and acts anyway.

🎭 Effect on Audience

The soliloquy is deeply uncomfortable. We see his full moral awareness and then watch it collapse. It makes him simultaneously more understandable and more culpable.

📝 Use In Exam For

Ambition, Free will vs fate, Macbeth's complexity, Tragic hero — his awareness of his own flaw makes him tragic, not simply villainous.

3
Macbeth does murder sleep
"Methought I heard a voice cry, 'Sleep no more! / Macbeth does murder sleep.'"
Act 2, Scene 2 — Macbeth immediately after killing Duncan
📍 Context — What's happening

Macbeth has just returned from murdering the sleeping king. He is shaking, still holding the daggers. He tells Lady Macbeth he heard a voice — real or imagined — proclaiming his guilt.

🔍 Technique — What Shakespeare does

Personification and hallucination. Sleep is given human qualities — it is "innocent," it "knits up the ravell'd sleeve of care." The hallucination of the voice is Macbeth's conscience speaking aloud.

💡 Key Insight — What this really means

Sleep is one of the play's most powerful symbols. It represents innocence, peace of conscience, and the natural order of life. Duncan was literally asleep when murdered — defenceless in the most innocent human state. By killing him, Macbeth has destroyed his own capacity for peace. This becomes a prophecy: Lady Macbeth later walks in her sleep compulsively washing her hands; Macbeth sees ghosts. Neither sleeps peacefully again. The murder of sleep is not just poetic — it is literally what happens to both of them.

🎭 Effect on Audience

Macbeth's horror here is genuine and immediate. The guilt has arrived faster and more powerfully than he expected. This is not the cold killer he thought he could be.

📝 Use In Exam For

Guilt (immediate, overwhelming), Consequences of ambition, Sleep symbolism, Imagery and its dramatic effect.

4
Full of scorpions is my mind
"O, full of scorpions is my mind, dear wife!"
Act 3, Scene 2 — Macbeth to Lady Macbeth, before ordering Banquo's murder
📍 Context — What's happening

Macbeth is king but miserable. He knows Banquo suspects him and knows the witches' prophecy says Banquo's sons will be kings — making his own crown pointless. He is planning Banquo's murder.

🔍 Technique — What Shakespeare does

Visceral metaphor. Scorpions are creatures of pain and poison — not one torment but a swarm, multiplying, each capable of fatal sting. This is a mind of constant, swarming anxiety.

💡 Key Insight — What this really means

Notice that what fills his mind is not guilt but fear and paranoia. In Act 2, he felt horror and remorse after Duncan's murder. Now in Act 3, the dominant emotion is calculation and anxiety about threats. This shows the deterioration of his character: the moral anguish of a guilty conscience has been replaced by the cold paranoia of a tyrant. He is also now keeping Lady Macbeth in the dark ("Be innocent of the knowledge, dearest chuck") — he has outgrown his need for her and moved beyond her morally.

🎭 Effect on Audience

We see that achieving his ambition has made Macbeth more miserable than before. The crown has brought him only more fear. This is Shakespeare showing that ambition can never be satisfied.

📝 Use In Exam For

Consequences of ambition (kingship doesn't bring happiness), Moral deterioration, The changing relationship with Lady Macbeth.

5
I am in blood stepped in so far
"I am in blood / Stepped in so far that, should I wade no more."
Act 3, Scene 4 — Macbeth after seeing Banquo's ghost at the banquet
📍 Context — What's happening

Banquo's ghost has just disrupted the royal banquet. Macbeth has raged and hallucinated in front of all his nobles. After the guests leave, he settles into cold calculation — and decides to press forward.

🔍 Technique — What Shakespeare does

Extended metaphor — wading through blood. The image of blood as a deep river captures the relentless, self-sustaining momentum of his crimes. Blood is both literal (his murders) and symbolic (guilt).

💡 Key Insight — What this really means

This is the point of moral no-return. The most telling word is "tedious" — not agonising, not impossible, just inconvenient. Macbeth has reached a stage where returning to virtue feels like too much effort. He has fully crossed from tragic hero into tyrant. This is also the moment he chooses to visit the witches again — not to be tempted by them, but to demand answers. He now treats them as servants, not as mysterious powers. The man who feared a dagger vision in Act 2 now actively seeks out dark magic.

🎭 Effect on Audience

We feel the horror of watching a person choose to stop choosing. He is no longer fighting his ambition — he has surrendered to it completely. The audience may feel pity, disgust, or both.

📝 Use In Exam For

Moral deterioration, Consequences of ambition, Blood imagery, Macbeth's responsibility for his own downfall — he could turn back. He chooses not to.

6
Tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow
"Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, / Creeps in this petty pace from day to day."
Act 5, Scene 5 — Macbeth on hearing of Lady Macbeth's death
📍 Context — What's happening

A servant tells Macbeth that the Queen is dead. Rather than grieve, he reflects that she would have died at some point anyway — then delivers this meditation on the meaninglessness of life.

🔍 Technique — What Shakespeare does

Anaphora (repetition) — the triple "tomorrow" mimics the monotonous, endless passage of time. The word "creeps" gives time a physical quality: slow, exhausting, joyless.

💡 Key Insight — What this really means

This speech is the final, devastating cost of Macbeth's ambition. He gained everything he wanted — the crown — and found it to be a "tale / Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, / Signifying nothing." Nihilism: life itself has become meaningless to him. He cannot even grieve for his wife. Notice that he refers to her death as something that "should have been" later — he is not even surprised. The man who murdered for power has arrived at the conclusion that power, and all of life, means nothing. The ambition that drove him has burned away everything that made life worth living. This is the complete portrait of ambition's cost.

🎭 Effect on Audience

Even at this point, the speech generates pathos. We feel pity for a man so emptied by his own choices. Shakespeare never allows us to simply hate Macbeth — we also mourn what he could have been.

📝 Use In Exam For

Ambition — its ultimate cost (gains everything, finds it worthless), Guilt — he cannot feel grief, Leadership — a king who finds life meaningless has failed Scotland, Tragic hero — the final collapse.

7
Yet I will try the last
"Yet I will try the last. Before my body / I throw my warlike shield."
Act 5, Scene 8 — Macbeth's final words before the fight with Macduff
📍 Context — What's happening

Macduff reveals he was "from his mother's womb / Untimely ripped" — the prophecy is broken. Macbeth knows he will die. He chooses to fight anyway, not surrender.

🔍 Technique — What Shakespeare does

Defiant declarative. The monosyllabic "Yet I will try" is simple, resolute, final. The image of throwing a warlike shield before him is a soldier's act — he dies as he began, a warrior.

💡 Key Insight — What this really means

This quote is crucial for the tragic hero debate. Macduff at the end calls him a "dead butcher" — but is that the whole truth? At the last moment, Macbeth rejects surrender and fights. There is something in him — pride, courage, the warrior he was at the start — that refuses to yield. Shakespeare doesn't let him simply die as a coward. This ambiguity is essential: the audience is invited to weigh "dead butcher" against the complex, suffering, self-aware man we have followed for five acts. The tragic hero is redeemed not morally but dramatically — he meets death with the same defiance that defined him at his best.

📝 Use In Exam For

Tragic hero (does he retain any nobility?), Ambition vs courage — he was always a warrior, Complexity of character — not simply a "butcher."

Quote Strategy for the Exam

You don't need all 7. Pick 4–5 that fit your question. Quote 2 (vaulting ambition) and Quote 6 (tomorrow) are your anchors for almost every theme. Quote 3 (murder sleep) is your guilt quote. Quote 5 (stepped in blood) is your moral deterioration quote. Quote 1 (stars hide) proves ambition is self-generated, not caused by the witches.

Act 2
👹 Paranoid Tyrant

💀 Nihilistic Ruin
Act 5

📈 Stage 1 — The Noble Warrior (Act 1, opening)

We never see this Macbeth — only hear about him. The Captain: "brave Macbeth — well he deserves that name." Duncan: "valiant cousin, worthy gentleman." This is deliberate. Shakespeare establishes what he was so the fall is more devastating. He is introduced through other people's admiration, which means the audience has to hold two Macbeths in their head simultaneously throughout the play.

📈 Stage 2 — Temptation Activated (Act 1, after witches)

The witches don't create his ambition — they find it already there ("black and deep desires"). His moral struggle in Act 1.7 is his last genuine chance to choose otherwise. He reasons himself out of the murder and then lets Lady Macbeth reason him back in. This is free choice, not fate.

📈 Stage 3 — Guilt-Ridden Murderer (Act 2)

Immediately after the murder: shaking, hearing voices, carrying the daggers back by accident, unable to say "Amen," horrified by his own bloodied hands. "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" Lady Macbeth is still in control practically, but Macbeth's inner world has already begun to crack. Guilt arrives faster and harder than he expected.

📈 Stage 4 — Paranoid Tyrant (Acts 3–4)

He orders Banquo's murder without the moral anguish of Act 1 — quickly, secretly, alone. He massacres Macduff's entire family when Macduff is not even there. The anguished man of Act 2 has become the cold tyrant. He now visits the witches to demand answers, not to be led by them. His relationship with Lady Macbeth is severed — he no longer needs or confides in her.

📈 Stage 5 — Nihilistic Ruin (Act 5)

By Act 5, he has lost allies, purpose, wife, and meaning. The "tomorrow" speech is the complete portrait of ambition's cost. Yet his final act is defiant — he fights and dies as a warrior. Malcolm calls him a "dead butcher," but the audience has witnessed something far more complex. Shakespeare's final gift to Macbeth is this ambiguity.

Past Exam Questions & Strategies
📝
2026 Mock — Guilt Cannot Be Suppressed

Show guilt operating at every stage: Q3 (murder sleep — immediate guilt after the act), Q4 (scorpions — paranoia replacing guilt), Q6 (tomorrow — nihilism as the end point of guilt). Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking is your strongest supporting evidence. Guilt destroys both characters, just at different speeds.

📝
2026 Mock — Macbeth's Internal Conflict with Ambition and Guilt

This question is asking you to trace the tension between wanting power and knowing it is wrong. Use Q2 (he reasons against it, then acts), Q3 (guilt arrives immediately), Q5 (he decides to stop reasoning and just continue). Show how the tension resolves: guilt loses, ambition wins, and then both are burned away by the time of Q6.

📝
2023 — Unstable and Tragic Identity

Structure your answer around the arc: he is not one thing but five. Noble warrior → tempted schemer → guilt-ridden murderer → paranoid tyrant → nihilistic ruin. The "complexity" comes from Q2 — he is never unaware of his own evil. He knows, and acts anyway. That is what makes him tragic rather than simply villainous.

📝
2013 — Responsible for His Own Downfall

The key: Banquo receives the same prophecy. Banquo resists. Macbeth chooses to act. Q2 (vaulting ambition) is the proof — he arrives at the right conclusion himself and reverses it. Use Q1 (stars hide) to show the desire is his own. Use Q5 (stepped in blood) to show he chooses to continue at every opportunity. Free will, not fate.

The Examiner's Formula — Never Forget This

Name the technique → Give the quote → Explain the effect on the audience → Link to the theme. Every paragraph. Four steps. A student who does all four consistently will score very high marks even with imperfect quotes.

Cross-Character Links (Always Include These)
  • Macbeth vs Banquo: Same witches' prophecy, different choices. Banquo proves free will. Macbeth's choice is self-made.
  • Macbeth & Lady Macbeth: She begins stronger; he surpasses her in ruthlessness by Act 3. Both destroyed, differently — he by paranoia, she by guilt.
  • Macbeth vs Duncan: Everything Duncan represents as a king — trust, generosity, natural order — Macbeth destroys. Duncan's virtue makes Macbeth's crime worse.
