The Magdalene Sermon · 1989

Translation

A devastating tribute to the women of the Magdalene laundries — read at their reburial ceremony. Ní Chuilleanáin translates decades of enforced silence into powerful, political poetry.

Political / ElegiacTercets + Sestet Women's RightsIrish History

The Simple Version

In the early 1990s, remains of over 150 women were found at Dublin religious institutions during excavations. The bones were cremated and reburied at Glasnevin Cemetery. This poem was written and read at the reburial ceremony. Ní Ch. uses the poem to "translate" the silence of these women's lives into words — to give them a voice they were denied in life. The word "translation" refers to: translating silence into speech; translating these women from their hidden graves into public memory; and the Catholic tradition of moving the remains of saints to a worthy resting place.

The Title: "Translation" — Four Meanings

  • Literal: "Translate" originally means to move from one place to another — the remains were literally moved from secret graves to Glasnevin.
  • Linguistic: Ní Ch. translates the silence of these women's lives into the language of poetry.
  • Religious: In Catholic tradition, the "translation of relics" means moving the remains of saints to a worthy resting place — she treats these women as equally worthy.
  • Cultural: Moving these women from the margins of history to its centre.

The Magdalene Laundries — What You Need to Know

⛪ What Were the Magdalene Laundries?

Magdalene Laundries were Church-run institutions in Ireland where "fallen women" — including unmarried mothers, women deemed promiscuous, girls who were too pretty, girls in poverty — were sent to do hard physical labour. They washed, scrubbed and ironed for up to 18 hours a day. They were forbidden from speaking. They prayed in enforced silence. They were stripped of their names and identities. Many were buried without proper graves when they died. The last laundry closed in 1996. An estimated 10,000 women passed through these institutions.

📝
Why This Context Matters

The poem was written and read at an actual reburial ceremony. Every line connects to the lived reality of these women — the steam is the steam of the laundry; the washing is the punishment they endured; the "sharp edges of words" are the labels used against them.

Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis

Stanzas 1 & 2 — The Burial and the Labour
Women from every county... where steam danced / around stones and giggled and slipped across water...

The steam "danced" and "giggled" — devastating contrast

The steam is personified as joyful and playful — it "dances," "giggles" and "slips." But the women doing the work cannot do any of these things. They are forbidden to laugh, forbidden to play, forbidden to communicate freely. The cruelest irony: the very water and steam of their labour is more free than they are. The enjambment mimics the continuous, endless nature of the work.

Stanzas 3–5 — "Assist them now" / The Prayer
Assist them now... ridges under the veil... sharp edges of words... rising above the shuffle and hum...

Three stanzas of prayer / direct address

"Assist them now" — we are called to act on behalf of these women. "Ridges under the veil" — possibly bones in the dirt; "veil" evokes both a wedding veil (joy, innocence) and secrecy (hidden away). "Sharp edges of words" — the metaphor makes language physically dangerous. The same sharp words ("fallen," "sinful," "unclean") branded these women and commanded their burial.

"Rising above the shuffle and hum"

The "shuffle and hum" is the relentless sound of the laundry — feet shuffling, machinery humming, prayers droning. "Rising above" suggests both the difficulty (it almost cannot be done) and the aspiration — the voice strains upward. The poem itself is this rising voice.

Stanza 6 — The Change of Voice (Three Readings)
I am washed clean of idiom... the bunched keys I bore... the baked crust of words...

The shift to "I" and "my" — three possible speakers

Reading 1 — The Poet herself: Ní Ch. says she is "washed clean of idiom" — the language of the laundry ("fallen women," "sinners") no longer holds. The "baked crust of words" that oppressed all women has been lifted.

Reading 2 — A Nun: She acknowledges her guilt. She was defined by "the bunched keys I bore" (her power over the women). The ceremony cleanses her from "that spell" — the institutional Church that controlled her.

Reading 3 — A Magdalene speaking from the grave: The voice "washed clean" of the laundry's language. Only in death is the "spell" lifted. The cloud that rises is both steam and the shadow of shame over Ireland.

Keys symbolise control — the power to lock and unlock. In the nun reading, the speaker acknowledges that she "bore" these keys — she was the one who locked the women in. The keys "slacken and fall" at the ceremony — an image of relinquishing power, of the institution finally losing its grip.

Poetic Techniques

📐 Form: Tercets and Sestet

The poem is structured in tercets (3-line stanzas) with a final sestet (6 lines). There is no regular rhyme — there is nothing comfortable here; it is meant to be fragmented. The broken rhythm and slant rhyme add to the discomfort.

🔇 Sibilance

Hushing "s" sounds throughout evoke the silence of the laundries — the enforced quiet. May also represent the sound of washing, the steam, the shuffling of feet. The sibilance is both threatening and mournful.

⚖️ Contrast: Youth and Pain

The poem repeatedly contrasts the youth and innocence that should have been these women's lives with the brutal reality they experienced. The steam giggles while the women cannot. The veil suggests weddings while the reality is burial.

☁️ The Steam / Cloud Metaphor

The rising steam at the end is both literal (the steam of the laundry) and metaphorical — the shadow and shame hovering over Ireland's history. As it rises, it both acknowledges the wrong and suggests that shame is beginning to lift.

🗣️ Change of Voice in Stanza 6

The shift from third person to first person ("I," "my") in the final stanza is dramatically powerful. It connects past to present, links the poet to the women, and creates multiple possible speakers (poet, nun, Magdalene).

🔁 Irony Throughout

The laundry used washing as punishment; the poem ends with washing as liberation. Words were used to silence; the poem is words that give voice. The ceremony meant to bury is what finally speaks for the dead.

Context & Exam Preparation

🎓
Exam Tip

This is Ní Ch.'s most explicitly political poem. Always mention: (1) the specific historical context of the Magdalene laundries, (2) the multiple readings of the final stanza, (3) the title's layered meanings, (4) how she gives voice to the silenced.

Links to Other Poems

  • Lucina Schynning: Both deal with women in confined, difficult situations. Both deal with Irish history. Both move from suffering toward some form of acknowledgement.
  • Street: Both feature powerful women, but in very different circumstances. In Translation the women are victimised; in Street the woman holds all the power.
  • Death and Engines: Both deal with the finality of death and the need to commemorate those who have died.
💡
Key Argument

In "Translation," Ní Chuilleanáin performs the very act the title describes: she translates decades of institutional silence into public speech, giving the Magdalene women the voice they were denied in life. By ending with an ambiguous first-person voice that could belong to any woman — poet, nun, Magdalene — she insists that this history belongs to all of Ireland.
