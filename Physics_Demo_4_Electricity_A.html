⚡ Electricity Demonstrations A (1–15)

1Forces Between Charges
  • Two lightweight conducting balls on nylon threads
  • Van de Graaff generator or charged rods (glass + ebonite)
  • Silk and wool cloths
  1. Suspend two metallised balls near each other on nylon threads.
  2. Charge both balls the same type (both + via Van de Graaff).
  3. Observe: balls repel, hanging apart.
  4. Now give one ball + and one − charge (opposite charges).
  5. Observe: balls attract.
  • Same charges: balls repel, hanging apart.
  • Opposite charges: balls attract, moving towards each other.
  • Confirms: like charges repel; unlike charges attract (Coulomb's Law).

Like magnets — two north poles push apart, north and south snap together.

F ∝ q₁q₂/r² (Coulomb's Law). Like charges repel; unlike attract. State this exactly.

2Charging by Induction
  • Metal conductor on insulating stand
  • Charged plastic rod
  • Earth connection (wire)
  • Gold-leaf electroscope
  1. Bring positively charged rod near (NOT touching) the conductor.
  2. While rod is near, briefly earth the far end of the conductor.
  3. Remove earth first, then remove charged rod.
  4. Test conductor with electroscope.
  • Conductor is negatively charged (opposite to rod) without contact.
  • Gold-leaf opens on testing.
  • Inducing rod retains its charge — induction not conduction.

Like a magnet near a nail — the nail becomes magnetised without touching.

Sequence matters: (1) bring rod near, (2) earth conductor, (3) remove earth, (4) remove rod. Charge = opposite sign to inducing charge.

3Demonstration of Point Discharge
  • Pointed conductor connected to Van de Graaff generator
  • Lighted candle (~10 cm from point)
  • Smoke strips as alternative indicators
  1. Charge the pointed conductor to high potential.
  2. Hold lit candle approximately 5–10 cm from the point.
  3. Switch on generator.
  4. Observe the candle flame.
  • Candle flame is blown away from the point — 'electric wind'.
  • Strong field at the point ionises air; ions repelled create an ion wind.
  • Rounded conductor shows no effect — sharpness is key.

Like a water hose nozzle — sharpest point concentrates the electric field just as narrow nozzle concentrates flow.

Field is greatest at sharp points (high charge density). Ionised air ions repelled = electric wind. This is why lightning conductors have sharp points.

4Charge on Outside of Hollow Conductor
  • Hollow metal can (Faraday's ice pail) on insulating stand
  • Van de Graaff generator
  • Proof plane (metal disc on insulating handle)
  • Gold-leaf electroscope
  1. Charge the hollow can via Van de Graaff. Disconnect generator.
  2. Sample charge from INSIDE with proof plane → test on electroscope.
  3. Discharge electroscope. Sample from OUTSIDE with proof plane → test.
  4. Compare leaf deflections.
  • Inside: negligible leaf deflection — no charge inside.
  • Outside: significant leaf deflection — charge present.
  • All excess charge resides on the outer surface — Faraday cage effect.

A car during lightning: charge stays outside — the metal body shields the interior. Same principle.

State: 'For a hollow conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field inside is zero. All excess charge resides on the outer surface.' This is the Faraday cage principle.

5Electric Field Pattern
  • Two metal plate electrodes or point electrodes
  • Castor oil in a shallow dish
  • Semolina grains scattered in oil
  • EHT supply (~5–10 kV)
  1. Place dish of oil on non-conducting surface. Scatter semolina evenly.
  2. Insert electrodes. Connect to EHT. Switch on.
  3. Observe alignment of semolina grains.
  • Parallel plates: semolina aligns perpendicular to plates — uniform field.
  • Point charges: semolina radiates outward — radial field.
  • At sharp points: grains closer together — stronger field.

Like iron filings in a magnetic field — semolina rotates to align with the electric field.

Parallel plates = uniform field (equal spacing). Point charge = radial field (lines outward from +). Field lines go from + to −, never cross.

6Capacitance of Parallel Plate Capacitor
  • Large parallel plate capacitor on insulating supports
  • Electrometer or high-impedance voltmeter
  • EHT to charge capacitor
  • Dielectric sheet (glass/perspex)
  • Ruler
  1. Charge capacitor to fixed voltage. Disconnect supply (constant Q).
  2. Measure voltage V.
  3. Increase plate separation d — observe V change.
  4. Return to original. Insert dielectric — observe V change.
  5. Decrease plate area — observe V change.
  • Increasing d: voltage rises (C decreases). C ∝ 1/d.
  • Inserting dielectric: voltage falls (C increases).
  • Decreasing area: voltage rises (C decreases). C ∝ A.
  • Verifies C = ε₀εᵣA/d.

A capacitor is like a sponge — larger area, smaller gap, better material = more charge stored.

C = ε₀εᵣA/d. Three factors: plate area (↑A → ↑C), separation (↑d → ↓C), dielectric (↑εᵣ → ↑C).

7Capacitor Stores Energy
  • Large capacitor (1000–4700 μF electrolytic)
  • 9V battery
  • LED + 470 Ω resistor
  • Switch
  • Connecting wires
  1. Connect capacitor to 9V battery for 5–10 seconds (charge it).
  2. Disconnect battery.
  3. Connect LED + resistor across capacitor.
  4. Close switch — observe LED.
  • LED flashes briefly and brightly — capacitor releases stored energy.
  • Flash fades quickly as capacitor discharges.
  • Demonstrates energy stored = ½CV².

A capacitor is like a bucket of water — the battery fills it, remove the tap, connect a pipe and the water empties quickly.

Energy = ½QV = ½CV² = Q²/2C. Show calculation if C and V given.

8Capacitors Conduct AC but Not DC
  • Capacitor (10–100 μF)
  • LED
  • Signal generator (AC) and battery (DC)
  • Connecting wires
  1. DC test: connect capacitor in series with LED and DC battery. Observe.
  2. AC test: replace battery with signal generator at 50–100 Hz. Observe.
  • DC: LED flickers briefly (charging flash) then goes out — capacitor blocks steady DC.
  • AC: LED glows continuously — capacitor repeatedly charges/discharges each cycle.
  • Capacitor passes AC, blocks DC (in steady state).

Like a revolving door — AC keeps spinning through; DC stands still and gets blocked once charged.

State: 'Capacitor blocks steady DC (current stops when fully charged) but allows AC (charges/discharges each cycle).' Basis of capacitive coupling in electronics.

9Conductor/Insulator Test with Ammeter
  • Battery (6–9V)
  • Ammeter (milliammeter)
  • Connecting wires with crocodile clips
  • Test materials: copper, aluminium, graphite, steel, rubber, plastic, wood, glass
  1. Build series circuit: battery → ammeter → two crocodile clip leads.
  2. Bridge gap with each test material. Record ammeter deflection.
  3. Group materials as conductors (deflection) or insulators (none).
  • Metals (copper, aluminium, steel): large deflection — good conductors.
  • Graphite: small deflection — conductor but not as good.
  • Rubber, plastic, glass, dry wood: no deflection — insulators.

A conductor is a motorway for charge; an insulator is a wall; a semiconductor is a country road.

Conductor: many free electrons. Insulator: tightly bound electrons, no free carriers. Examples: conductor = Cu, Al; insulator = rubber, glass.

10Electroplating by Electrolysis
  • Two copper electrodes
  • Copper sulfate (CuSO₄) solution
  • Beaker
  • Battery (6–9V)
  • Ammeter
  • Balance
  1. Clean and weigh both electrodes. Label anode (+) and cathode (−).
  2. Connect anode to positive terminal, cathode to negative.
  3. Run current for 10–15 minutes. Record ammeter.
  4. Remove, dry carefully, re-weigh both electrodes.
  • Cathode gains mass — copper deposited on it.
  • Anode loses mass — copper dissolves into solution.
  • Mass gained at cathode ≈ mass lost at anode.
  • Verifies Faraday's First Law of Electrolysis.

Like a 3D printer using electricity — copper atoms are 'printed' one by one onto the cathode from the solution.

Faraday's First Law: 'Mass deposited ∝ charge passed.' m = ZIt, Z = electrochemical equivalent. Cathode always gains; anode always loses (if copper anode in CuSO₄).

11Magnetic Effect of Current
  • Straight wire through horizontal card
  • Iron filings or compass needles
  • Power supply (DC, 3–6V)
  • Switch
  1. Pass wire vertically through card with iron filings. Connect to power.
  2. Switch on current. Gently tap card.
  3. Observe iron filings. Place compass needles around wire.
  4. Reverse current — observe deflection reversal.
  • Iron filings form concentric circles around wire.
  • Compass needles deflect tangentially.
  • Reversing current reverses field direction.
  • Closer to wire = stronger field (tighter rings).

Like ripples in a pond — except the 'ripples' (magnetic field) are circles around the wire rather than expanding outward.

Right-Hand Grip Rule: thumb = current direction, fingers = field direction. State: 'A current-carrying conductor produces a circular magnetic field.'

12Heating Effect of Current
  • Thin nichrome resistance wire (28 SWG, ~20 cm)
  • Thick connecting wires
  • Battery (6V) or power supply
  • Variable resistor (rheostat)
  • Ammeter
  • Switch
  1. Connect thin wire in series with rheostat and ammeter.
  2. Start at maximum resistance (minimum current). Switch on.
  3. Gradually decrease resistance to increase current.
  4. Observe thin wire at different currents.
  • At low current: wire feels warm.
  • At higher current: wire glows red then white.
  • Thin wire heats most (highest R = ρl/A; smaller A → higher R).
  • Thick wires stay cool — low resistance.
  • Demonstrates Joule's Law: P ∝ I².

Like a toaster — thin high-resistance element gets very hot while thick wires to the plug stay cool.

Joule's Law: 'Rate of heat ∝ I². P = I²R.' H = I²Rt. Doubling I quadruples power output.

13Rheostat as Potentiometer
  • Rheostat (variable resistor, 50 Ω)
  • Battery (6V)
  • Voltmeter (0–6V)
  • Connecting wires
  • Switch
  1. Connect both end terminals of rheostat to battery.
  2. Connect voltmeter between sliding contact and one end.
  3. Switch on. Move slider from one end to other.
  4. Observe voltmeter reading.
  • Slider moved end to end: voltmeter varies continuously from 0V to full battery voltage.
  • Voltage output proportional to fraction of resistance used.
  • Demonstrates rheostat as potential divider.

Like a volume knob — you can set any volume level between silent (0V) and maximum (battery V).

V_out = V_in × R₂/(R₁+R₂). A potentiometer gives variable voltage from 0 to Vmax. Different from simple variable resistor (which limits current but output stays connected to supply).

14Ohm's Law
  • Known resistor (10 Ω wire-wound)
  • Rheostat
  • Battery (6V)
  • Ammeter (0–1A)
  • Voltmeter (0–6V)
  • Switch
  • Connecting wires
  1. Connect R in series with rheostat, ammeter, battery, switch.
  2. Connect voltmeter in parallel across R.
  3. Start at max resistance (min current). Record V and I.
  4. Decrease rheostat in steps — take at least 5 readings.
  5. Plot V (y-axis) vs I (x-axis).
  • V increases proportionally with I.
  • Graph: straight line through origin — confirms V ∝ I.
  • Slope = R = ΔV/ΔI, agrees with marked value.
  • Verifies Ohm's Law: V = IR at constant temperature.

Like a garden hose — harder pressure (higher V) → faster flow (higher I), ratio always the same (constant R).

Must state 'at constant temperature.' V vs I graph: straight line through origin, slope = R. 'Current is directly proportional to potential difference.'

15Current Flow Across P-N Junction
  • P-N junction diode (or LED)
  • Resistor (470 Ω)
  • Battery (9V)
  • Ammeter (milliammeter)
  • Switch
  • Connecting wires
  1. Forward bias: connect + terminal to P-type (anode), − to N-type (cathode). Include ammeter and resistor.
  2. Switch on. Observe ammeter.
  3. Reverse bias: swap battery connections. Observe ammeter.
  • Forward bias: clear current flows — junction conducts.
  • Reverse bias: ammeter reads zero — junction does not conduct.
  • LED: emits light only in forward bias.
  • P-N junction is a one-way valve for current.

Like a one-way turnstile — correct direction: sail through; wrong way: blocked.

Forward bias: + to P, − to N. Depletion layer narrows, current flows. Reverse bias: depletion layer widens, no current. Applications: rectification (AC→DC), LED.
