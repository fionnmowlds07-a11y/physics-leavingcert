Plé & Anailís

A verse-by-verse breakdown of Mo Ghrá-sa (idir lúibíní) — understanding what the poem says, how the brackets work, and what Ní Dhomhnaill is really doing with this unusual love poem.

Aoir · Satire Lúibíní · Brackets Trí Véarsa Casadh sa Deireadh

📋 Cad é an Dán Seo?

Is dán grá neamhghnáthach é seo. (This is an unusual love poem.) At first glance, it reads like an insult — the poet describes her partner's eyes as too close together, his hair like barbed wire, and his appearance so bad that if he is related to daisies, they'll grow from his ears. But the final twist changes everything.

The poem is structured as a deliberate inversion of the old Irish love-song tradition (na hamhráin grá), where women were compared to flowers, swans, and moonlight. Ní Dhomhnaill turns this tradition on its head, using the same nature images — but to mock rather than praise.

  • Véarsa 1–2: What her love is NOT — the denial of traditional beauty comparisons
  • Véarsa 3: Specific, comic physical flaws — eyes too close, hair like wire
  • An casadh (the turn): "Ach is cuma sin" — but none of that matters
  • An deireadh: He gives her apples and grapes — simple kindness is everything
🔑
The Core Message

Is é bun agus barr an dáin ná go bhfuil sí i ngrá le gnáthfhear le pearsantacht chineálta. (The top and bottom of the poem is that she is in love with a normal man with a kind personality.) Physical beauty is irrelevant — what matters is who a person is inside, what they do, and how they make you feel.

💡
Key Phrase for Your Exam

"Is cinnte gur dán grámhar é seo, cé nach gceapfá é sin ar an gcéad léamh."
(It is certain that this is a love poem, even though you would not think that on the first read.) — Use this to open any answer about the poem's theme.

Na Lúibíní — The Role of the Brackets

( ) Cén fáth a bhfuil lúibíní sa teideal?

The brackets appear in the very title — Mo Ghrá-sa (idir lúibíní) — which immediately signals to the reader that this is not a conventional love poem. The phrase "idir lúibíní" literally means "in brackets" or "between brackets," but also carries the idiomatic sense of "with reservations" or "not quite." This title is a pun — her love is literally written in brackets.

📤 Text Outside the Brackets

Contains the traditional, poetic statements you would expect in an old-style love poem — comparisons with flowers, swans, the moon. But here they are all negative: "Níl mo ghrá-sa mar bhláth na n-airní..." (My love is not like the flower of the blackthorn...)

📥 Text Inside the Brackets

Contains the real, unfiltered, often comic truth. The brackets deliver the "fírinne lom" (bare truth) — both the insults about his appearance AND, at the end, the genuine praise for his kindness. The brackets are where reality lives.

🔑 Trí Fheidhm na Lúibíní — Three Functions of the Brackets
1. Scigmhagadh ar na sean-dánta grá
Mocking the old love poems
The brackets interrupt the "flowery" language and undercut it with reality. Every grand poetic statement is immediately qualified or undermined by what comes next in brackets.
2. Fiosracht an léitheora a mhúscailt
Awakening the reader's curiosity
The brackets create a kind of whispered aside — like the poet is pulling you close to tell you the real story. They create intimacy, humour, and suspense throughout the poem.
3. An fhírinne dheireanach a nochtadh
Revealing the final truth
The most important bracket of all: "(is nuair a bhíonn sé i ndea-ghiúmar / caora finiúna)" — the grapes appear inside a bracket, making the most loving moment of the poem technically parenthetical. And yet it is the heart of everything.

Véarsa ar Véarsa — Verse by Verse
📖 Véarsaí 1 & 2 — The Negative Comparisons
Níl mo ghrá-sa mar bhláth na n-airní
(atá ag fás ar an gclai)
ná mar eala uaigneach,
(ag seoladh ar an loch.)

Níl a ghnúis mar ghrian an mheán lae
(is é ag taitneamh ar an gcoill,)
ná mar ghealaigh ag éirí
(thar chnoic na tíre seo.)

These opening verses are a direct parody of the traditional Irish love-song (amhrán grá). In the old tradition, a woman's skin was compared to flowers, her movement to a swan, her face to the sun and moon. Here, Ní Dhomhnaill uses the exact same comparisons — but negates every single one.

"Mar chodarsnacht iomlán, tosaíonn Ní Dhomhnaill ag rá nach bhfuil a leannán 'mar bhláth na n-airní'." (As a total contrast, Ní Dhomhnaill begins by saying her lover is not like the blackthorn flower.)

The brackets in these verses contain beautiful natural images — the hedgerow, the lonely swan on the lake, the midday sun, the moon rising over hills. By placing beauty inside brackets while negating it in the main text, she shows that traditional poetic beauty is a kind of afterthought — in parentheses — to real love.

📖 Véarsa 3 — The Comic Physical Description
Tá a shúile ró-chóngarach dá chéile
(is é féin a dúirt liom é,)
is tá a chuid gruaige
(mar a bheadh sreang dheilgneach ann.)

Is má tá aon ghaol aige le nóiníní,
(is as a chluasa a fhásfaidh siad.)

This is the comic heart of the poem. The description becomes increasingly absurd — his eyes are too close together (and he admitted it himself, she points out in brackets), his hair is like barbed wire, and if he has any connection to daisies, they'll grow from his ears.

The bracket "(is é féin a dúirt liom é)" — "he himself told me" — is brilliantly comic. The poet isn't even being cruel — she's just repeating what he said about himself. This detail humanises the man and shows he has self-awareness and humour.

"D'fhéadfá a rá gur íomha chumhachtach í seo mar gur féidir linn a chuma gránna a shamhlú." (One could say this is a powerful image because we can imagine his horrible appearance.)

✍️ Phrases for Véarsa 3

Ag an bpointe seo, éiríonn go géal leis an bhfile fiosracht an léitheora a mhúscailtAt this point, the poet successfully evokes the curiosity of the reader
Déantar cur síos greannmhar agus saghas maslach ar a stóirínA funny and somewhat insulting description is made of her dear
📖 An Casadh — "Ach is cuma sin" (The Turn)
Ach is cuma sin;
tugann sé dom
úlla
(is nuair a bhíonn sé i ndea-ghiúmar)
caora finiúna.

"Ach is cuma sin" — three words that flip the entire poem. "But none of that matters." After two and a half verses of comic mockery, this single line pivots everything. The poet acknowledges all his flaws — and dismisses them completely.

What does matter? He gives her apples. And when he is in good humour — (is nuair a bhíonn sé i ndea-ghiúmar) — grapes. These are not grand romantic gestures. They are small, everyday acts of generosity and care.

"Is meafar iad na húlla agus na caora finiúna don ghrá agus don chineáltacht." (The apples and the grapes are a metaphor for love and kindness.) The apples represent the necessities of life he provides; the grapes represent the small luxuries — given when he is in good spirits, which makes them feel more genuine and spontaneous than any forced grand gesture.

"D'aithin sí an seanfhocal 'ní bhíonn saoi gan locht'." (She recognises the proverb "nobody is perfect.")

An Aoir — The Satire

🎭 Cad é an t-aoir? What is a Satire?

An aoir (satire) is a piece of writing that mocks or criticises something through humour, irony, and exaggeration. Ní Dhomhnaill is satirising the tradition of na hamhráin grá — the love-song tradition that was dominant in Ireland in the 17th century. These songs described the beloved in impossibly perfect, idealised terms, using nature imagery (flowers, swans, moonlight) to create an image of flawless beauty.

🎭 The Old Tradition vs. This Poem

In the old songs, you would expect to find:

Súile chomh soléir le sruthán cnoic
Eyes as clear as a mountain stream
Traditional comparison — clear, beautiful, natural
Gruaig chomh buí le heorna
Hair as golden as barley
Traditional comparison — golden, natural beauty
Leicne chomh dearg leis an rós
Cheeks as red as the rose
Traditional comparison — rosy, perfect complexion

Ní Dhomhnaill uses the same structure — comparisons with nature — but delivers the opposite results:

Tá a shúile ró-chóngarach dá chéile
His eyes are too close together
Anti-traditional — specific, comic, unflattering
Tá a chuid gruaige mar a bheadh sreang dheilgneach ann
His hair is like barbed wire
Anti-traditional — rough, harsh, ugly

✍️ Key Satire Phrases

Is léir gur aoir é an dán seo ar na seandánta gráIt is clear that this poem is a satire of the old love poems
D'aithin sí nach bhfuil na seandánta seo réalaíochShe recognises that these old poems are not realistic
Ceapann an file go gcuirtear an iomarca béime ar áilleacht sheachtrach sa lá atá inniuThe poet thinks too much emphasis is put on external beauty today
Déanann an file tagairt le Shakespeare agus a shoinéad 130The poet makes a reference to Shakespeare and his Sonnet 130
🎓
Shakespeare Connection — Bonus Point!

Sonnet 130 by Shakespeare follows the exact same structure: "My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun..." — Shakespeare also mocks the tradition of idealising women through nature comparisons, concluding that real love doesn't need false flattery. Ní Dhomhnaill and Shakespeare both celebrate genuine, imperfect human love over poetic fantasy. Mentioning this in your exam answer shows sophisticated knowledge.

Freagra Samplach — Model Answer
🎓 Question: Discuss the main theme of this poem

Tús / Introduction:

Is cinnte gur dán grámhar é seo, cé nach gceapfá é sin ar an gcéad léamh. Tá sé suntasach gurb é buntéama an dáin seo ná an tábhacht a bhaineann le cineáltacht agus le tuiscint sa chaidreamh a bhíonn idir leannáin.
It is certain that this is a love poem, even though you would not think that on the first read. It is significant that the main theme of this poem is the importance of kindness and understanding in the relationship between lovers.

Forbairt / Development:

Osclaítear an dán agus is amhlaidh gur aoir é ar na seandánta grá ina gcuirtear béim mhór ar áilleacht fhisiciúil an duine. Mar chodarsnacht iomlán, tosaíonn Ní Dhomhnaill ag rá nach bhfuil a leannán "mar bhláth na n-airní."
The poem opens and it is clear that it is a satire of old love poems in which a large emphasis was placed on the physical beauty of the person. As a complete contrast, Ní Dhomhnaill starts by saying her lover is not "like the blackthorn flower."

An casadh / The turn:

Pé scéal é, sa véarsa deireanach athraíonn atmaisféar an dáin ó cheann a bheith aibhéalach go ceann a bheith grámhar. Is é an chloch is mó ar a paidrín ná pearsantacht an fhir. Tugann sé di úlla agus "nuair a bhíonn sé i ndea-ghiúmar, caora finiúna." Is meafar iad na húlla agus na caora finiúna don ghrá agus don chineáltacht.
However, in the last verse, the atmosphere of the poem changes from sarcastic to loving. The most important thing to her is the personality of the man. He gives her apples and "when he is in good humour, grapes." The apples and the grapes are a metaphor for love and kindness.

Conclúid / Conclusion:

Mar fhocail scoir, ní féidir a shéanadh gurb é príomhthéama an dáin seo ná an tábhacht a bhaineann le cineáltacht agus le tuiscint sa chaidreamh. Déanann Ní Dhomhnaill ceiliúradh ar lochtanna a stóirín. Tá an grá dall.
Finally, there is no denying that the main theme of this poem is the importance of kindness and understanding in the relationship. Ní Dhomhnaill celebrates her dear's faults. Love is blind.
