🐟 The Fish

Survival, observation, and an unexpected epiphany. Bishop catches an extraordinary fish and — through close, loving attention — discovers a survivor deserving of freedom.

76 lines · Free Verse No Rhyme Scheme Narrative / Conversational Epiphany at Line 75
🤔
The Simple Version — What's Happening

Bishop goes fishing on her own and catches one remarkable fish. Instead of feeling triumphant, she becomes fascinated by it. She holds it up and looks at it closely — its old, worn skin, its enormous eyes, and most importantly: five old hooks and broken fishing lines stuck in its lip. This fish has been caught five times before and escaped every single time. She has a sudden realisation — this battered, ancient creature is a survivor. Joy floods the boat like a rainbow. She lets it go.

📋 Snapshot

🗓️ Background

  • Written in Florida, c. 1940. Published in her first collection North & South (1946)
  • Based on a real fishing trip — Bishop was a keen fisherwoman from her time in Nova Scotia
  • She called it "that damn fish" because it was reprinted so often
  • She described herself in 1947 as "like a fish out of water" — the fish may be partly self-portrait
  • She once wrote to Robert Lowell: "I was the loneliest person who ever lived" — the poem is about finding kinship in unexpected places

🎯 At a Glance

  • Theme: Surviving adversity; second chances; respect for life
  • Tone: Curious → admiring → celebratory
  • Form: Free verse, long descriptive narrative — like a story being told aloud
  • Mood: Celebratory, elation at the end
  • Effect: Allegorical — teaches a lesson about resilience and letting go
  • Key moment: Line 75 — "And I let the fish go."

🌊 The Central Ideas

Why Does She Let It Go?

The fish hasn't done anything extraordinary in this moment — it just hangs there. But the speaker's close observation reveals a hidden story: five previous captures, five escapes. Each broken hook is a battle scar, a medal. She realises she is looking at a fellow survivor. This is Bishop's core belief: if you look closely enough at anything, you will find something profound.

The fish is also a metaphor for Bishop herself — battered, living with the marks of past struggles (alcoholism, depression, loss), but still here, still surviving. Letting the fish go is an act of recognition and respect. It has earned its freedom.

📝
Exam Tip — What to Argue

If asked about Bishop's use of language and imagery: argue that she uses close observation of the natural world as a pathway to emotional and philosophical insight. The fish starts as a caught object and becomes a symbol of survival. Her detailed, domestic similes (wallpaper, roses, medals) make the extraordinary feel familiar, which is exactly how her epiphanies work — they sneak up on you through ordinary detail.

Lines 1–8 — The Catch
I caught a tremendous fish
and held him beside the boat
half out of water, with my hook
fast in a corner of his mouth.
He didn't fight.
He hadn't fought at all.

What's Happening

The poem opens in medias res — we're already in the middle of the action. The fish is described as "tremendous" immediately, giving it status. The crucial detail: it didn't fight. This is the first mystery. Why won't it fight? This passivity is what draws her in and starts the poem's journey of observation.

Repetition "He didn't fight. He hadn't fought at all." — the repetition emphasises the strangeness. It also immediately humanises the fish: "he," not "it."

🧠
Remember

The fish's stillness is the catalyst for the whole poem. If it had fought back, she would have simply landed it. Its dignity creates the space for her to look, and looking changes everything.

Lines 9–22 — The Skin and Body
He hung a grunting weight,
battered and venerable
and homely. Here and there
his brown skin hung in strips
like ancient wallpaper,
and its pattern of darker brown
was like wallpaper:
shapes like full-blown roses
stained and lost through age...

What's Happening

Bishop describes the fish in extraordinary detail — not beautiful in any conventional sense, but deeply interesting. "Battered and venerable and homely" — these three adjectives (a triad) capture it perfectly: worn, deserving of respect, and ordinary-looking all at once.

Simile "like ancient wallpaper" — a deliberately domestic comparison. Wallpaper is found inside homes; choosing it to describe fish skin makes the unfamiliar (fish) feel familiar (home). "Ancient" gives it age, dignity, history.

Imagery "shapes like full-blown roses" — even in decay, there is beauty. She refuses to see the fish as just ugly or damaged. She finds floral shapes in its worn skin. This teaches us to look for beauty in overlooked, aging things.

Lines 33–44 — The Eyes
I looked into his eyes
which were far larger than mine
but shallower, and yellowed,
the irises backed and packed
with tarnished tinfoil
seen through the lenses
of old scratched isinglass.
They shifted a little, but not
to return my stare.

What's Happening

This is the first real attempt at connection. She looks into the fish's eyes — but it doesn't look back. This refusal to engage is significant: the fish is not performing for her. It simply exists, on its own terms. She can't quite reach it.

Perspective Shift "far larger than mine" — she is comparing their eyes. She is now seeing the fish as her equal, a fellow creature with its own perception of the world.

The eyes are described as tarnished, scratched, old — but still there, still open. This is another layer of the fish's age and survival.

Lines 45–64 — The Five Hooks (Key Passage)
and then I saw
that from his lower lip
— if you could call it a lip —
grim, wet, and weaponlike,
hung five old pieces of fish-line,
or four and a wire leader
with the swivel still attached,
with all their five big hooks
grown firmly in his mouth...
Like medals with their ribbons
frayed and wavering,
a five-haired beard of wisdom
trailing from his aching jaw.

What's Happening — THE TURNING POINT

This is the emotional climax of the poem. The broken fishing lines embedded in the fish's lip tell its whole story: it has been caught five times before and escaped every single time. These are its battle scars — its proof of survival.

Metaphor "Like medals with their ribbons" — the hooks become military medals. The fish is now a war veteran who has survived multiple battles. This is the moment the poem transforms entirely.

Personification "beard of wisdom" — the fish is now a wise old man. "Wisdom" comes from surviving experience. This fish has earned its place in the world.

🎖️
Think of it like this

Imagine meeting someone very old covered in scars. Instead of pity, you realise each scar is a story of survival — they got knocked down five times and got back up six. That's the fish. The scars aren't weakness; they're proof of resilience. Bishop sees herself in this creature.

Lines 65–76 — Victory and Release (The Epiphany)
I stared and stared
and victory filled up
the little rented boat,
from the pool of bilge
where oil had spread a rainbow
around the rusted engine
to the bailer rusted orange,
the sun-cracked thwarts,
the oarlocks on their strings,
the gunnels — until everything
was rainbow, rainbow, rainbow!
And I let the fish go.

What's Happening — THE EPIPHANY

Colour Imagery The poem begins in dull browns and ends in an explosion of colour. Oil on bilge water creates a rainbow — beauty found in the dirtiest, most functional part of the boat.

Repetition "rainbow, rainbow, rainbow!" — the triad and exclamation mark signal the epiphany: a burst of joy, wonder, and realisation flooding through her. Three repetitions = completeness, fullness, overflow.

The final line — "And I let the fish go." — is five words after 75 lines of build-up. The simplicity is the power. She gives the fish its freedom because it has earned it. Perhaps she finds a kind of freedom herself in letting go.

📝
Exam Tip — The Ending

The contrast between the setting (dirty, oily, rusted boat) and the rainbow perfectly embodies Bishop's philosophy: beauty exists in unexpected, overlooked places. If asked about a moment of insight or how she uses language to create effect, this ending is always your best example.

"He didn't fight. He hadn't fought at all."
Lines 5–6 — The very first description

Repetition The repetition of "fight" with the addition of "at all" deepens the emphasis. This is unusual behaviour for a caught fish — its stillness is dignified, almost otherworldly.

Already calling it "he" (not "it") signals that Bishop is granting the fish personhood from the opening lines. This small pronoun choice sets up everything that follows.

Why it matters: The fish's passivity is what makes her look. If it fought back she'd land it and move on. Its stillness creates the space for close observation — which is the whole point of Bishop's poetry.

"his brown skin hung in strips / like ancient wallpaper"
Lines 10–11

Simile Domestic Imagery "Ancient wallpaper" is deliberately homely — something from indoors, something old, worn but once decorative. It makes the exotic (old fish) feel familiar and even affectionate.

"Ancient" is key — it gives the fish age, history, and dignity. Not "old" or "damaged" — "ancient," like a monument or an artefact.

Immediately followed by beauty: "shapes like full-blown roses / stained and lost through age." She refuses to stop at the unpleasant — she finds the aesthetic even in decay.

"Like medals with their ribbons / frayed and wavering, / a five-haired beard of wisdom / trailing from his aching jaw."
Lines 61–64 — The emotional turning point

Extended Metaphor Personification The hooks become military medals — the fish is a veteran. "Frayed and wavering" — the ribbons (lines) are old and worn, just like real battle medals.

"Beard of wisdom" — the fish is now a wise elder, its experience encoded in the very hooks still in its jaw. Wisdom comes from surviving. The fish has survived five captures. That is extraordinary.

Link to Bishop's life: She too had survived enormous hardships. This is the moment she sees herself in the fish — a fellow survivor carrying the marks of past struggles.

"until everything / was rainbow, rainbow, rainbow! / And I let the fish go."
Lines 74–76 — The epiphany and ending

Repetition / Triad Three "rainbows" — Bishop's signature technique for marking a moment of intense feeling. The excess of the word mirrors the excess of joy and wonder flooding through her.

Colour Imagery The poem moves from dull browns (Line 1) to this explosion of colour. The rainbow is formed by oil on bilge water — beauty born from the ugliest part of the scene. This is Bishop's philosophy in miniature.

"And I let the fish go." — understated, five words, enormous weight. The simplicity is the power. Everything has built to this moment of recognition and release.

"He hung a grunting weight, / battered and venerable / and homely."
Lines 7–9

Triad Three adjectives that perfectly capture the fish: battered (damaged by life), venerable (deserving deep respect), homely (ordinary-looking, plain). These three together say everything about how Bishop sees the fish and, by extension, how she sees resilience and worth.

"Venerable" is an extraordinary word choice for a fish — it is typically reserved for religious figures, ancient institutions, elders. Using it here immediately elevates the fish to a figure of moral weight.

What it is: Sound devices that speed up or slow down the poem based on the speaker's excitement or the subject matter.

Examples:

  • Sibilance: "shiny entrails," "sea-lice," "stared and stared" — the soft 's' sounds slow the poem down, creating a sense of quiet, close observation
  • Alliteration: "battered and venerable" — the repeated 'v' creates emphasis
  • Assonance: The 'o' sounds in "coarse," "bones," "blown" create a flowing, musical quality

When discussing sound, always explain the effect — don't just name the device. "The sibilance in 'stared and stared' slows the line, creating a sense of the speaker's absorbed, reverent attention."

What it is: Lines that run into the next without a pause at the end — no full stop or comma at the line break.

Effect in The Fish: The poem flows almost like prose being spoken aloud — like someone excitedly describing what they saw. The enjambment mirrors the speaker's breathless, building observation as she discovers more and more detail about the fish.

Example: "and then I saw / that from his lower lip / — if you could call it a lip —" — the enjambment here keeps pulling us forward, mimicking the speaker's momentum as she makes her discovery.

What it is: Repeating words or phrases for emphasis; triads are groups of three words/phrases.

Examples:

  • "He didn't fight. He hadn't fought at all." — repetition of "fight" with intensification
  • "battered and venerable and homely" — triad of adjectives
  • "rainbow, rainbow, rainbow!" — the epiphany. Three repetitions = completeness and overflow of feeling
  • "I stared and stared" — repetition creates a sense of absorbed wonder

Domestic similes:

  • "like ancient wallpaper" — inside the home, old, worn but once decorative
  • "packed in like feathers" (the flesh) — familiar, soft, domestic
  • "like a big peony" (the swim bladder) — like a garden flower

More striking similes:

  • "Like medals with their ribbons" — the hooks as war medals
  • "like the tipping of an object toward the light" — the shifting of the eyes

Effect: The domestic similes make the unfamiliar fish feel familiar and even affectionate. They show Bishop's observational style — using what she knows (home, everyday objects) to describe what she sees.

Your notes say: "Gives the fish human qualities — allowing us to understand it and empathise with it."

Examples:

  • "He" throughout — not "it"
  • "sullen face, the mechanism of his jaw" — human facial anatomy
  • "if you could call it a lip" — she is reaching for human vocabulary to describe the fish, half-aware of the stretch she's making
  • "beard of wisdom" — the fish as a wise old man
  • "his aching jaw" — attributing pain and physical feeling to the fish

Your notes say: "Specialised language specific to boating — 'Wire leader,' 'swivel,' 'bilge,' 'bailer,' 'gunnels' — makes her account legitimate and authentic."

Effect: Using the correct technical vocabulary for fishing and boating establishes Bishop as a real fisherwoman who knows what she's talking about. This gives the poem authority and credibility — we trust her description because she clearly has the knowledge to back it up.

It also grounds the poem in a very specific, real world. This is not a symbolic or imaginary fish in a symbolic ocean — it is a real fish, in a real rented boat, with real fishing equipment. The specificity makes the emotional journey more powerful.

Your notes say: "No rhyming scheme, very formal, written as a narrative with conversational tone. As if telling a story. 'From his lower lip — if you could call it a lip.'"

Effect: The absence of a rhyme scheme makes the poem feel like natural speech — like Bishop is telling us what happened, exactly as it happened, in real time. This intimacy is crucial: we are placed beside her in the boat, sharing the experience.

The conversational asides ("if you could call it a lip," "I think") reinforce this — she is thinking out loud, inviting us into her mind.

Your notes say: "Colour: rot and old, to hope-filled and bright."

The poem's colour arc perfectly traces its emotional arc:

  • Opening: "brown," "darker brown," "tarnished tinfoil" — dull, earthy, aged
  • Middle: "dramatic reds and blacks," "pink swim-bladder like a big peony" — richer colour, more life
  • End: "orange," and then the explosion — "rainbow, rainbow, rainbow!" — full spectrum of colour, hope, joy

Crucially, the rainbow is made from oil on dirty bilge water — beauty found in the most unglamorous, functional part of the boat. This is Bishop's philosophy: extraordinary beauty hides in ordinary, even ugly, places.

🧭 Biographical Context — Why Bishop Wrote This

Bishop was a keen fisherwoman, learned during her happy years living with her grandparents in Nova Scotia as a child. Fishing was one of the few sources of joy and stability in an otherwise turbulent childhood.

She wrote this poem in Florida, c.1940, during a period of relative calm in her life. But she was already dealing with alcoholism, depression, and a deep sense of loneliness. In 1948, she wrote to Robert Lowell: "When you write my epitaph you must say I was the loneliest person who ever lived."

The fish, battered but surviving, carrying the marks of five previous escapes "like medals" — this is Bishop's own resilience reflected back at her. She identifies with the fish. The act of letting it go is an act of recognition: this creature has earned its freedom, just as she might hope to earn hers.

📖 The Biblical Undertone

Fish have enormous symbolic weight in Christian tradition — they represent Christ, faith, transformation. Five is also a significant number (the five wounds of Christ). The "five old pieces of fish-line" could carry a quiet religious resonance, though Bishop never makes it explicit.

More likely, the number five simply reinforces the fish's extraordinary resilience — five captures, five escapes. The accumulation of the number makes the survival feel almost miraculous.
