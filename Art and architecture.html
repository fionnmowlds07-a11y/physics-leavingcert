Topic 1

🛣️ Roads

The arteries of the Roman Empire — without roads Rome could not have controlled, traded with, or communicated across its vast territories.

🌍
Why Roads Were Essential
The Purpose of Roman RoadsKEY
▼
Roads allowed for quick communication throughout the empire, trade and easy passage for the military. Roads greatly helped the process of Romanization, especially in Gaul.

Think of Roman roads like a motorway network — they connected every corner of the Empire and kept it running. Three core uses: military movement, trade, and communication.

⚔️ Military Use

  • Armies moved rapidly to trouble spots
  • No need to hack through forests
  • Crucial for maintaining control of distant provinces
  • Troops could march 25 miles per day on good roads

📦 Trade & Communication

  • Goods moved quickly between cities
  • Official messages reached emperors fast
  • Postal relay system (cursus publicus) used roads
  • Helped Romanize conquered peoples (especially Gaul)
🧠 Analogy: The Roman road network was like the internet — it connected everything and allowed rapid movement of information (messages), goods (trade), and "updates" (military force). Without it, the Empire would have fragmented into isolated pieces.
⚠️ Exam Quote to Know: "Without roads the Empire would not have been so great." Use this in any essay on Roman roads.
The Via Appia (Appian Way)EXAM EXAMPLE
▼
The Via Appia is one of the most famous roads in ancient Italy. It was built by Appius Claudius. It connected Rome with Capua and secured the hold of Rome on Campania. It was later extended as far as Brundisium and Tarentum.

📍 Key Facts

  • Built by Appius Claudius
  • Connected: Rome → Capua
  • Extended to: Brundisium & Tarentum
  • Secured Roman control of Campania

🗺️ Strategic Importance

  • Brundisium = main port to Greece & the East
  • Key military & trade artery to south Italy
  • Template for all future Roman roads
  • Still partially intact today
🔮 Memory Trick: "Appius built the Appian Way" — the road literally named itself after its builder. Appius → Appia. You cannot forget this!
Milestones (Milliaria)DEFINITION
▼
Milestones were markers on the road. The usual milestone was a cylindrical pillar from 3–9 feet tall. It was inscribed with the date, name and titles of the emperor responsible for the road. It gave the distance to the next town in miles.

Milestones served two purposes simultaneously: practical (navigation — how far to the next town) and political (propaganda — the emperor's name reminded everyone who was in charge).

🔩 Physical Description

  • Shape: cylindrical pillar
  • Height: 3–9 feet tall
  • Material: stone
  • Placed at regular intervals along road

📝 What Was Inscribed

  • Date (when road built/repaired)
  • Name and titles of responsible emperor
  • Distance to the next town in miles
🧠 Analogy: Like a modern road sign that says "Dublin 45km — maintained by Transport Infrastructure Ireland" — except the Romans replaced "TII" with the emperor's full name and titles. Pure genius and pure ego, at the same time!
Roads under Augustus — Agrippa's SurveyEXAM DETAIL
▼
Roads were greatly developed under Augustus who had his lieutenant Agrippa survey the entire empire. 50,000 miles of paved roads.

Augustus understood: roads = control. He commissioned his greatest general Agrippa to survey the whole empire and create a comprehensive road system.

📌 Scale of Achievement: 50,000 miles of paved road — roughly equivalent to circling the Earth twice. An unprecedented engineering achievement for the ancient world. Roads were built by slaves and local townspeople.
⚙️
Road Construction — How They Were Built
The Layers of a Roman RoadKEY — KNOW IN DETAIL
▼
Roads were wide enough for 2 carts to pass. Their gradients were often severe, and they were straight for the most part. The foundations of the road were often 2 feet deep. A layer of heavy stone was placed, then two or more layers of coarse lime mortar (cement). The top surface which was sloped to allow run-off water was made of the hardest material available in the region.
LayerMaterialPurpose
Foundation (~2 ft deep)Heavy stone slabsStability — prevents sinking
Middle layers (2+)Coarse lime mortar / cementBinding and load distribution
Top surfaceHardest local stone, slightly domed/slopedDurability + water drainage (rain runs off)

📏 Dimensions & Design

  • Wide enough for 2 carts to pass
  • Foundations: 2 feet deep
  • Straight for most of their length
  • Gradients often severe but managed cleverly

💡 Clever Engineering Details

  • Top surface sloped/domed → rain water drains off sides
  • If slope >10°: road zigzagged rather than going straight up
  • Tunnels only built if absolutely essential
  • Used hardest available local material — very practical
🧠 Analogy: A Roman road is like a modern tarmac road — concrete base → binding layers → smooth driving surface on top. The Romans invented this multi-layer approach 2,000 years ago. Their roads were so well-built that sections still exist today!
⚠️ Exam Phrase: "This showed how practical the Romans were." Use this when describing road construction — combining function (drainage slope, foundations) with efficiency (hardest local material, built by slaves).

🏛️ The Column

Borrowed from the Greeks, perfected by the Romans. Columns combined structural strength with breathtaking beauty — and there are 4 types you MUST know.

🏛️
What is a Column?
Columns — Origins, Purpose & StructureKEY CONCEPT
▼
A notable feature of Greek architecture. Adapted by the Romans. Used both for purpose and style (both practical and beauty). 4 types of columns.
Columns compromised of a base which rested on a foundation called a stylobate. Main trunk of the column was the shaft, on top of which rested the capital. Used to transmit the weight of the structure above down. Usually contained a decorative element, combining beauty with practicality. Constructed from a single piece of stone. Their design incorporates a reduction in diameter from base to capital. Made them look very tall. Some columns were incorporated for only design — this is called pseudoperipteral. Columns quickly became a feature of classical architecture.

🪨 Stylobate

The foundation/platform the column rests on. The base on which everything above stands.

🏺 Shaft

The tall main trunk/body of the column. Constructed from a single piece of stone. Tapers slightly from bottom to top.

👑 Capital

The decorative top piece that sits above the shaft. The capital type determines which of the 4 orders the column belongs to.

🧠 Analogy: Think of a column like a table leg: it has a foot (stylobate), a body (shaft), and a head (capital). The genius is the gentle taper — wider at the bottom, narrower at the top — which makes it look incredibly tall and elegant while actually being structurally stronger at the base where weight is greatest.
⚠️ KEY TERM — Pseudoperipteral: When columns are used purely for decoration (built into/attached to the wall) and do NOT structurally support anything. They look load-bearing but aren't. Example: the Maison Carrée — only the front columns are real; the side "columns" are half-columns attached to the wall.
📊

Doric → Tuscan → Ionic → Corinthian = D-T-I-C
From OLDEST/STRONGEST → to NEWEST/MOST DECORATIVE

Corinthian at the top has the least weight to bear, so the most decorative style goes there. Romans weren't just beautiful — they were logical.

🔵 The Arch

One of the most revolutionary architectural ideas in history. The Romans didn't invent it, but they mastered it — and it changed everything.

🔵
The Roman Arch — How It Works
What is the Roman Arch?KEY CONCEPT
▼
It was said to have been the Etruscans who showed the Romans the arch, but of course it was refined and adapted by the Romans. The Romans saw that the arch had great potential in building. It composed of bricks or stone blocks wedged together to prevent slipping. The Romans covered the arch in stones to conceal the impression of weight, therefore combining beauty with practicality.
Arches allowed the Romans to enclose much larger spaces. The stones in the arch pressed against one another, therefore transmitting weight from the supporting structure above to the ground below. The advantage of the arch and later variations like the dome, is that the Romans could have large open spaces without any columns.
🧠 Analogy: Imagine a stack of playing cards leaning into each other in an arch shape. Each card pushes on the one beside it. Remove any one card and the whole thing collapses — but together they're incredibly strong. That's the arch: the keystone at the very top is the last piece inserted and locks everything in place, redirecting weight outward and downward along the curve to the ground.

✅ Why Arches Were Revolutionary

  • Could span far larger spaces than flat stone beams
  • No need for columns in middle of open spaces
  • Weight transmitted efficiently down to the ground
  • Opened the door to domes, bridges, aqueducts and vaults
  • Bricks/stone wedged together to prevent slipping

🎨 Beauty + Practicality

  • Romans covered arch in decorative stones
  • Concealed the engineering mechanics
  • Surface appeared purely decorative
  • In simplest form: rectangular masonry with single archway between 2 columns
  • Developed into great bridges and aqueducts
⚠️ Key Exam Phrase: "The advantage of the arch and later variations like the dome, is that the Romans could have large open spaces without any columns." — Use this exact idea in any essay. It explains WHY the arch was so important.
Triumphal ArchesEXAM EXAMPLE
▼
There was also stand alone arches called triumphal arches used to commemorate military victories, e.g. arch of Titus.

Triumphal arches were freestanding monuments — not part of a building — built purely to celebrate military victories. They were propaganda in stone, visible to all.

🏛️ Structure of a Triumphal Arch

  • Single arch between huge piers
  • Other columns standing on a podium on each side
  • Inscribed with a dedication
  • Often topped with a chariot group (quadriga = 4-horse chariot)
  • Interior: decorated with relief sculpture

📌 Famous Examples

  • Arch of Titus: 81 AD. Oldest triumphal arch still standing in Rome. Cross between Corinthian and Ionic styles.
  • Arch of Septimius Severus: in the Roman Forum
  • In the reign of Augustus: arches erected even in the most remote provinces
🔮 Memory: Arch of Titus = 81 AD. Think: "Titus built his arch in 81 — he took Jerusalem in 70 AD, and a decade later (81) got his arch." The reliefs inside show the sack of Jerusalem and the Menorah being carried away.

🌉 Bridges

Once the Romans mastered concrete and arches, bridges were the logical next step — and they helped hold the entire Empire together.

🌉
Roman Bridges — Development & Importance
The Development of Roman BridgesKEY TOPIC
▼
Since the Romans had already mastered concrete and arches, bridges were a fairly easy step to make. Bridges enabled rivers and ravines to be easily crossed. At first, wooden bridges were built, but they were not permanent. The stone bridge was a fairly late development. Most Roman bridges date from the Imperial Era. According to the evidence, the Romans invented arched bridges. Wooden bridges were continued to be built but only for temporary military crossings and other things.

📅 Development Timeline

  • First: wooden bridges — easy but temporary
  • Later: permanent stone bridges (late development)
  • Most stone bridges: Imperial Era
  • Romans credited with inventing arched bridges
  • Wood continued only for temporary military crossings

💡 Why Bridges Mattered

  • Armies could move rapidly across rivers
  • Facilitated trade and movement of goods
  • Incorporating arches made structure lighter
  • "Can be argued bridges contributed to the very existence of Rome"
  • Enabled the easier movement of goods and people
🧠 Analogy: Wooden bridges were like temporary tents — quick to set up, fine for an army passing through, but no good for permanent infrastructure. Stone arched bridges were like permanent motorways — built to last centuries, enabling commerce, armies, and growth.
⚠️ Key Phrase for Exam: "It can be argued bridges contributed to the very existence of Rome." Strong closing sentence for any bridges essay.

Trajan's Bridge on the Danube: Built between 103 and 105 AD. Designed by Apollodorus of Damascus. Depicted in relief on Trajan's column. 15m wide, 20 stone pillars each 20m high, each 50m apart. High enough for ships to pass beneath.

📏 Exact Dimensions

  • Built: 103–105 AD
  • Designer: Apollodorus of Damascus
  • Width: 15 metres
  • Pillars: 20 stone pillars
  • Pillar height: 20 metres each
  • Spacing: 50 metres apart
  • High enough for ships to pass beneath

🎨 Historical Significance

  • Depicted in relief on Trajan's Column in Rome
  • One of the greatest feats of ancient engineering
  • Crossed the mighty River Danube
  • Enabled Trajan's campaigns in Dacia
  • Apollodorus of Damascus also designed Trajan's Baths

Or: "Fifteen-wide, Twenty pillars, Twenty tall, Fifty apart". Picture 20 massive stone pillars each as tall as a 6-storey building, spaced 50m apart across a wide river!

💧 Aqueducts

The ancient water supply system — a marvel of engineering that used nothing but gravity to carry millions of gallons of fresh water across the Empire.

💧
What is an Aqueduct?
Aqueducts — Definition, Function & EngineeringKEY TOPIC
▼
The arched aqueduct was a short step away from the arched bridge. It carried water in covered channels of brick, stone and concrete. Arches were often incorporated for the sake of aesthetics. 3 famous aqueducts are the Aqua Virgo, Aqua Claudia and Aqua Traiana.
The literal meaning of aqueduct is water channel. They incorporated a one degree gradient invisible to the naked eye so that gravity could push the water. Once water hit the cities it was put into dividing tanks where silt and grit sank, and then piped to baths, fountains, villas and the homes of the wealthy. At first it was piped through lead pipes, but this changed to ceramic when people got lead poisoning.

🔧 How They Worked

  • Literal meaning: water channel (aqua = water, ductus = led)
  • 1 degree gradient — invisible to eye but enough for gravity
  • Water flowed through covered channels of brick, stone, concrete
  • On arrival: settling/dividing tanks — silt and grit sank
  • Clean water piped to end destinations

🚰 Piping & Distribution

  • Initially: lead pipes
  • Changed to: ceramic pipes (after lead poisoning discovered)
  • Distributed to: baths, fountains, villas, homes of the wealthy
  • Arches often added for aesthetics
🧠 Analogy: An aqueduct is like an enormous gently-sloped slide for water. Build it 1° lower at the destination end — just barely tilted — and water flows there by itself, all the way from a mountain spring tens of miles away. No pumps, no engines, just physics. Romans mastered calculating that tiny 1° angle across 50+ miles!
📌 The Three Famous Aqueducts to Name:
1. Aqua Virgo — still supplies water to the Trevi Fountain in Rome today!
2. Aqua Claudia — built by Emperor Claudius
3. Aqua Traiana — built by Trajan

📏 Exact Dimensions

  • Location: Nimes (Nemausus), France
  • Built by: Agrippa
  • Stories: 3
  • Arches per story: 6 / 11 / 35
  • Height: 158 feet
  • Width: 21 feet
  • Length: 900 feet
  • Carried: millions of gallons per day

🌟 Why It's Remarkable

  • Best surviving example of a Roman aqueduct
  • Built purely on gravity — no pumps
  • 3-tier arch system is visually stunning
  • Still stands after 2,000 years
  • Agrippa (Augustus's general) also surveyed the Roman road network

Think: the arch count goes small → medium → big as you go up (6, 11, 35) — like a wedding cake with more decorations at each layer up!

Topic 6

⛩️ Temples

Houses of the gods — two types, circular and rectangular, with famous examples you must know for the exam.

⛩️
Temples — Overview
What is a Roman Temple? Key VocabularyKEY CONCEPT
▼
There were two types of temples in Rome, circular and rectangular. The world templum literally means religious precinct containing an inner building called a fanum or aedes which was the house of the god/goddess whose statue was in it. Public ceremonies occurred outside the fanum, while private prayer occurred within it. Temples were usually near theatres, at crossroads, on country estates or at significant locations. They were also used to store valuables and goods won in war. Temples usually had a portico in front, which was like a porch.

📚 Essential Vocabulary

  • Templum = the whole religious precinct
  • Fanum / Aedes = the inner building, house of the god
  • Portico = the covered porch/entrance at the front
  • Podium = the raised platform the temple stands on

📍 Key Facts

  • Public ceremonies: outside the fanum
  • Private prayer: inside the fanum
  • 2 types: Rectangular and Circular
  • Used to store war booty and valuables
  • Usually had a portico in front
🧠 Analogy: Unlike modern churches where worship is inside, Roman temples had all public activity (processions, sacrifices, ceremonies) OUTSIDE. The inner building (fanum) was essentially the god's private house — the statue lived there. Only priests went inside regularly.
◼️
Rectangular Temples
Rectangular Temples — General FeaturesDEFINITION
▼
Roman rectangular temples were built in the 2:1 ratio like many Greek temples. The frontality of Roman temples was emphasised by them being on a raised platform, accessible by steps. All rectangular temples had a portico.

📐 Design Rules

  • 2:1 ratio (length:width) — same as Greek
  • Built on a raised podium/platform
  • Accessible only by steps at the front (emphasises frontality)
  • All had a portico (front porch)

🏛️ Roman Innovation

  • Frontality = the front/facade is much more important than sides/back
  • Side columns often pseudoperipteral (decorative, attached)
  • High podium = dramatic entrance approach

Circular Temples
Circular Temples — General Features & Temple of VestaKEY
▼
Round temples also very common. The Pantheon and the Temple of Vesta are two examples in Rome. Sometimes built on a podium and sometimes had a surrounding portico. Built with tufa, travertine, concrete and marble. The Temple of Vesta is located in the Roman Forum near the Vestal Virgins. Believed to have been built by Numa Pompilius, a king of Rome. Contained a perpetual fire, kept alive by the Vestal Virgins. Built of marble and composed of 20 Corinthian columns. Situated on a podium 15m in diameter.

  • Built by: Numa Pompilius (king of Rome)
  • Contained: perpetual fire (never allowed to go out)
  • Fire maintained by: Vestal Virgins

  • Podium: 15m diameter
  • Entrance: faced east (connection to sunrise/fire)

🏗️ Circular Temple Features

  • Built with tufa, travertine, concrete, marble
  • Sometimes on a podium
  • Sometimes had a surrounding portico
  • Round shape = symbolic wholeness/eternity
The Pantheon — Full DetailMAJOR EXAM EXAMPLE
▼
The Pantheon is located in the Campus Martius and was built by Agrippa from 27–25BC. It has a portico of Corinthian columns, 8 in the front and 2×4 behind. The portico is 103 feet long and the columns 47 feet high. The inner structure is over 140 feet high, with walls 19 inches thick to support the dome and oculus. Interior decorated with marble to hide the support systems for the dome. Built of marble and composed of 20 Corinthian columns. Situated on a podium 15m in diameter. Entrance faced east to symbolise the connection between the sun rise and the fire.

📏 All Dimensions

  • Built by: Agrippa, 27–25 BC
  • Portico: Corinthian columns — 8 front, 2×4 behind
  • Portico length: 103 feet
  • Column height: 47 feet
  • Inner structure: over 140 feet high
  • Wall thickness: 19 inches
  • Podium: 15m diameter
  • Entrance: faces east (sunrise = fire connection)

🔍 Why the Walls are 19 Inches Thick

  • To support the enormous dome
  • The oculus (circular hole at top) lets in light
  • Oculus also reduces dome's weight — clever!
  • Interior decorated with marble to hide support systems
  • Combined Greek beauty with Roman practicality

🏢 Basilicas

The Roman multi-purpose public hall — courthouse, bank, marketplace and office all in one. The shape that gave birth to the Christian church.

🏢
What is a Basilica?
Basilica — Definition, Layout & UsesKEY TOPIC
▼
The First Basilicas were used as administration halls for Greek kings. The term later applied to a wide range of buildings including multi-purpose halls adjacent to the forum for several purposes like military, judicial, religious and trade. They were usually oblong in shape. The interior was a hall with a main nave and either two or four aisles, divided by pillars. They usually contained an apse which was a semicircular termination at the end of the isle where the officials sat. They were designed to hold large groups of people. The Basilica Julia and Aemilia are two examples.

🏗️ Architecture

  • Shape: oblong
  • Interior: main nave + 2 or 4 aisles (divided by pillars)
  • Apse = semicircular end where officials sat
  • Designed to hold large groups of people
  • Located adjacent to the forum

🏛️ Multiple Uses

  • Military administration
  • Judicial (law courts)
  • Religious gatherings
  • Trade & commerce
  • Government offices
  • Banks and shops
⚠️ KEY VOCAB — APSE: The semicircular recess at the END of the building where the officials/judges sat. This architectural feature was directly adopted by Christian churches. Almost every church ever built has an apse — the Romans invented it!
⚠️ KEY VOCAB — NAVE: The central hall running down the middle of the basilica (and later, every church). Derived from the Latin "navis" = ship (the ceiling looks like an upturned ship's hull).
🧠 Analogy: A basilica is like a modern shopping centre — a long building with a central main corridor (nave), side passages (aisles), and specialist rooms at the end (apse). Romans gathered there for business, law, trade, and government — everything happened in one building!

🏇 Circuses & Chariot Racing

The F1 of the ancient world — violent, thrilling, commercially driven, and watched by hundreds of thousands of cheering Romans.

🏇
Chariot Racing
How Chariot Racing WorkedKEY TOPIC
▼
In the reign of Augustus, 12 chariot races a day, nut increased to 24 under Caligula. Before the race the charioteers paraded before the emperors before taking their positions at the starting boxes (carceres). The presiding official throwing a white napkin signalled for the start. There were 2, 3 and 4 horse races. The drivers (aurigae) were usually slaves. They raced around a central spina, with three posts (meta) at each end. Tracks were very narrow.

🏁 Race Procedure

  • Number of races: 12/day (Augustus), 24/day (Caligula)
  • Pre-race: charioteers paraded before emperor
  • Start signal: official threw a white napkin
  • Starting boxes called: carceres
  • Race around: spina (central barrier)
  • 3 posts (meta) at each end of spina
  • Tracks were very narrow — crashes common!

🐴 Drivers & Races

  • Drivers = aurigae
  • Usually slaves
  • Race types: 2, 3, and 4 horse races
  • Victory could bring freedom or great sums of money
  • A great social occasion for all Romans
🧠 Analogy: Think of chariot racing teams like modern football clubs — Man City, Liverpool, etc. Fans were fiercely loyal, bets were placed, and fights regularly broke out between supporter groups. The difference: losing chariot drivers could be killed in crashes on the extremely narrow tracks!
The 4 Teams & BettingDEFINITION
▼
There were 4 teams at first — white, green, blue and red, but later purple and gold were added. Bets were made on different teams and fights often broke out between supporters. Victorious slaves could earn their freedom or great sums of money.

🎨 The Teams

  • Added later: Purple and Gold

⚔️ Amphitheatres & the Colosseum

Where thousands of lives were sacrificed for entertainment — gladiatorial combat, beast hunts, naval battles, and the most famous arena ever built.

⚔️
Amphitheatres — What & Why
Amphitheatres — Definition, Events & GladiatorsKEY TOPIC
▼
The amphitheatre surpassed the theatre in popularity. In them thousands of lives were sacrificed. Comprised of 2 semi-circular theatres and eventually incorporated into one structure. Elliptical in shape. Used for gladiatorial fights, beast hunts (Venationes), naval battle reinactments (Naumachiae), religious festivals and mock fights. Usually built on the edge of town or outside city walls. Gladiators were recruited from prisoners of war, condemned criminals, slaves or freedmen lured by the rewards if they survived.

🏟️ What Happened Inside

  • Gladiatorial fights
  • Venationes = beast hunts
  • Naumachiae = naval battles (arena flooded!)
  • Religious festivals
  • Mock fights / executions
  • Criminals and Christians thrown to wild beasts

⚔️ Gladiator Origins & Types

  • Recruited from: prisoners of war, condemned criminals, slaves, freedmen
  • Different types armed with different weapons
  • E.g. Retarius (net & trident), Samnite (sword & shield)
  • Usually built on edge of town / outside walls
Before combat gladiators paraded before the emperor. They saluted and said "Hail, emperor, we who are about to die greet you". Different gladiators were armed with different weapons, e.g. retarius, samnite. Victors received gift and after many wins could gain manumission by receiving the rudis, a wooden sword. The emperor decided who died with hand signs. Criminals and Christians were often thrown to the wild beasts. The amphitheatres were filled with water for naval battles called Naumachiae.
⚠️ Key Terms:
Venationes = beast hunts | Naumachiae = naval battles (arena flooded)
Rudis = wooden sword given to freed gladiator (symbol of freedom)
Manumission = the formal act of freeing a slave
🧠 Word Origin — Amphitheatre: "Amphi" = on both sides. An amphitheatre is literally two semicircular theatres merged together to form a continuous oval of seating around a central arena. Think of two Greek theatres pressed face-to-face!
🏟️
The Colosseum
The Colosseum — Full DetailMAJOR EXAM EXAMPLE
▼
The Colosseum was the largest of the Amphitheatres. It was officially called the Flavian Amphitheatre. It took 12 years to build. The name came from a statue of Nero nearby called Colossus. Comprised of thousands of blocks of stone, each weighing 5 tonnes. Its façade was covered in travertine marble. Smart use of arches and vaults prevented the use of 100's of more blocks of stone. Combined beauty with practicality. The Colosseum is typically Roman: Big, practical, ostentatious, hydraulics and sunroofs to protect spectators. Consisted of 4 stories. Bottom story Doric columns, next Ionic, then Corinthian.

📏 Key Facts

  • Official name: Flavian Amphitheatre
  • Time to build: 12 years
  • Name from: statue of Nero nearby = "Colossus"
  • Stone blocks: thousands, each 5 tonnes
  • Facade: travertine marble
  • Stories: 4

🏛️ Architecture & Innovation

  • Ground floor: Doric columns
  • 2nd floor: Ionic columns
  • 3rd floor: Corinthian columns
  • Arches + vaults: saved hundreds of extra stone blocks
  • Had hydraulics (for the Naumachiae)
  • Sunroofs (velarium) to shade spectators
🔮 Memory — Colosseum: The phrase to use: "The Colosseum is typically Roman: Big, practical, ostentatious." This is a perfect exam sentence. Also remember: 12 years to build, Flavian Amphitheatre, 5-tonne stone blocks, Doric-Ionic-Corinthian bottom to top.
⚠️ "Typically Roman" = the exam's favourite phrase. Big (massive scale), practical (arches reducing material needed, sunroofs, hydraulics), ostentatious (showing off wealth and power). Beauty PLUS practicality — this theme runs through all Roman architecture.

🎭 Theatres

From temporary wooden stages to permanent stone theatres that held 40,000 people — Roman theatre had a fascinating history.

🎭
Development & Layout of Roman Theatres
Theatres — History, Layout & CustomsKEY TOPIC
▼
At first they comprised of temporary wooden platforms (pilpitum) with no seats for the audience. The state was against building permanent theatres as they said it would lead to luxurious habits and damage public morals. Once permanent theatres were built, they comprised of three main areas: The Stage, Orchestra (semi-circle in front of stage) and Auditorium (semi-circular rows of concentric seating). Every Roman town had a theatre. Performances were held for religious festivals and therefore always a temple in the vicinity. Romans tried to build theatres into hillsides for practicality. If not available, seating was built on a series of archways, vaults and walls — beauty and practicality.

🏗️ The 3 Parts of a Theatre

  • Stage (Scaena) = where actors performed. Wall behind the stage. Scenes = street with houses.
  • Orchestra = semi-circle in front of the stage. VIPs sat here.
  • Auditorium (Cavea) = semi-circular rows of concentric seating for audience

🎭 Theatre Customs

  • Actors wore masks
  • Many people looked on actors with disfavour (low social status)
  • Plays performed in the open air
  • Stage scenes: represented a street with houses
  • Always a temple in the vicinity (performances for religious festivals)
In extravagant theatres, there was a wall behind the stage. Plays performed outside in the open air. Scenes on stage generally represented a street with some houses. Actors wore masks. Many people looked on actors with disfavour. Box on right for Emperor to sit, box on left for Vestal Virgins and Empress. Women sat at back. The Theatre of Pompey could accommodate 40,000 people. The Theatrum Marcelli was built by Caesar and Augustus. Theatrum Balbi, capacity 11,000.

💺 Seating Arrangements

  • Box on right: Emperor
  • Box on left: Vestal Virgins & Empress
  • Women sat at the back
  • Best seats in the orchestra for VIPs

🏟️ Famous Theatres

  • Theatre of Pompey: 40,000 capacity
  • Theatrum Marcelli: built by Caesar & Augustus
  • Theatrum Balbi: capacity 11,000
⚠️ KEY TERM — PILPITUM: The temporary wooden stage platform used in early Roman theatre before permanent stone theatres were built. The state opposed permanent theatres for fear of "luxurious habits and damage to public morals."
🧠 Analogy: Think of a Roman theatre like a modern concert venue: Stage (where the band plays) → Pit/Orchestra (right in front, VIPs) → Tiered seating (the auditorium). Romans just made theirs semicircular and built into a hillside where possible for natural acoustics and easy construction.

🎨 Mosaics

From simple geometric patterns to 1.5 million tesserae — mosaic art was durable, beautiful, and one of Rome's greatest decorative achievements.

🎨
Mosaics — Purpose, Techniques & History
What are Mosaics? Purpose & DevelopmentKEY TOPIC
▼
The chief purpose of mosaics was to provide a durable form of decoration for walls and pavements. The technique of tessellated mosaics in which pieces of stone or marble were cut into cubes and fitted together in a bed or mortar was invented in the 3rd century BC. At first tesserae were cut irregularly, then with greater precision. By the 2nd century BC, the tiny pieces were as small as 1mm square and in a wide range of colours. In imperial time mosaics begun to be mass produced. They were found particularly in the atrium and triclinium of private homes. The mosaics depicted: Geometric patterns and designs, divine characters and mythological scenes, famous individuals, Historical scenes, aspects of daily lives. Mosaics indicated wealth and opulence.

📅 Historical Development

  • Invented: 3rd century BC (stone/marble cubes in mortar)
  • 2nd century BC: pieces as small as 1mm square, wide range of colours
  • Imperial times: mass produced
  • Popular in: atrium (entrance hall) and triclinium (dining room)
  • Indicated: wealth and opulence

🖼️ What Did They Depict?

  • Geometric patterns and designs
  • Divine characters and mythological scenes
  • Famous individuals
  • Historical scenes
  • Aspects of daily life

🖼️ Paintings, Sculpture & Historical Relief

From frescos locked into walls to 2,500 carved figures on a single column — Roman art was propaganda, beauty, and history combined.

🖼️
Paintings
Roman Paintings — Overview & DevelopmentKEY TOPIC
▼
Originally, Roman art collectors acquired Greek paintings and Greek artists who migrated to Rome to meet demand. By imperial times there was a shift in interest from portable panel paintings to wall paintings (murals). At first used to decorate tombs, wall paintings became increasingly popular in private homes. At Pompey and Herculaneum most residences contained extensive paintings from simple schemes and patterns in smaller rooms to large lavish paintings in larger rooms. Main reason for them was to break the monotony of the blank wall with decoration. Also, an indicator of wealth and prestige. Painters were not considered high in social status. Often slaves or freedmen. Two main types: Murals and Encaustics. Reached maturity under Flavian Dynasty.

📜 Context & Status

  • Started by collecting Greek paintings and artists
  • Shift from panel paintings → wall murals
  • First used for tombs, then private homes
  • Main purpose: break wall monotony + show wealth and prestige
  • Painters: usually slaves or freedmen (low social status)
  • Reached maturity: Flavian Dynasty

🖌️ Two Main Types

  • Murals (Fresco) = painted directly into wet wall plaster
  • Encaustics = painted onto wooden panels using wax
Murals (Fresco Technique) — How It WorksDEFINITION & TECHNIQUE
▼
Murals: Painted directly onto walls using fresco technique. Plaster laid onto wall, smoothed and then kept moist long enough to enable painters to work directly onto it with colour. The chemical colours penetrated below the wet plaster and became locked there. Due to the chemical reaction between plaster and air the paint is preserved. Examples of such murals can be seen in the House of Livia, wife of Augustus on the Palatine hill. These famous Frescos covered the four walls of her underground triclinium. They portray many varieties of flora and fauna in such detail that many can still be identified.
🧠 Analogy: Imagine dyeing fabric vs. painting it. If you paint fabric, the colour sits on top and eventually peels off. If you dye it, the colour penetrates the fibres and becomes permanent. Fresco is dyeing the wall — the pigment is absorbed into the wet plaster while it's still "alive," then the chemical reaction locks it in permanently as the plaster dries. The wall and painting become one!
📌 House of Livia — Famous Fresco Example:
• Wife of Augustus
• Location: Palatine Hill
• Frescos covered all 4 walls of her underground triclinium
• Portray many varieties of flora and fauna in such detail that many can still be identified today
• One of the best preserved examples of Roman fresco painting
⚠️ How Fresco Differs from Encaustic: Fresco = bright, vibrant, permanent (chemical reaction locks colour INTO the wall). Encaustic = smooth glassy finish but NOT as bright as frescos.
Encaustic Painting TechniqueDEFINITION
▼
Encaustics: used in panel and smaller paintings. A surface of wax was applied to a wooden panel, a drawing was prepared and then coloured in with wax. Smooth glassy finish but not as bright as Frescos.

🖌️ Technique

  • Applied to: wooden panel
  • Method: wax surface applied, drawing prepared, coloured with wax
  • Result: smooth, glassy finish
  • Portable (unlike murals)

⚖️ vs Fresco

  • NOT as bright as frescos
  • Better for: smaller, portable paintings
  • More suitable for: portraits
  • Wax preserves and protects paint
Famous Painting — Amphitheatre Riot at PompeiiFAMOUS EXAMPLE
▼
Another famous wall painting was that of the amphitheatre in Pompey. The story tells that a disgraced Senator hoped to impress the locals of Campania by sponsoring gladiator fights. The games ended in disaster when spectators arrived from the neighbouring town, resulting in deaths. The town was not allowed to host games for 10 years. This is depicted on the painting.

This painting is remarkable as historical documentation — it records an actual real event (a riot at Pompeii's amphitheatre) in painted form. It's both art and journalism.

📌 Key points for exam:
• Disgraced senator sponsored gladiator games to impress locals of Campania
• Rival spectators arrived from the neighbouring town
• A riot broke out — deaths occurred
• Pompeii was banned from hosting games for 10 years
• The painting shows fighting both inside and outside the amphitheatre

Sculpture
Roman Sculpture — Origins, Forms & Portrait SculptureKEY TOPIC
▼
Roman sculpture was almost non-existent before the second century BC. After many victories over the Greeks many of their statuary were sent to Rome. Roman craftsmen began producing copies and thus industry thrived. This was due to the Roman middle and upper class wanting to decorate their homes to show wealth and influence. There were many forms of sculpture: Sarcophagus, Equestrian Statues, Portrait Sculpture, and Herms. Portrait head and shoulder busts were what the Romans thrived at. For the Romans visibly depicting a families importance and pedigree through portraits was very important. They were very realistic, showing the good and bad features of a person.

🗿 4 Forms of Sculpture

  • Sarcophagus = tomb elaborately carved with reliefs; minor at first → one of the most prolific later
  • Equestrian Statues = subject on horseback; recognised as one of the most striking forms of state portraiture
  • Portrait Sculpture = busts; first inspired by Etruscan wax death masks
  • Herm = head & shoulders bust on a bronze/marble pillar; usually in the Atrium

👤 Portrait Sculpture — Details

  • Inspired by wax death masks of the Etruscans
  • Also influenced by the Greeks
  • Augustus encouraged Hellenism in Roman art
  • Very realistic — showed good AND bad features
  • Realism indicated nobility (especially in busts of emperors of humble origin)
  • Popularity grew: officials wanted to immortalise themselves in marble
  • Depicting family pedigree = very important
🧠 Portrait Bust Analogy: Roman portrait busts are like LinkedIn profile photos — designed to communicate authority, status, and character. But unlike airbrushed modern photos, Romans deliberately showed every wrinkle to say "I earned my authority through experience." Greeks idealised their subjects; Romans were radically realistic.
📌 Famous Portraits:
• Caesar — serious expression
• Augustus — both serious expressions in official portraits
• Caecilius Iucundus — slight smile (known from Pompeii)
• Suggests realism = indicating nobility and humble origins
⚠️ KEY TERM — HERM: A head and shoulders bust (portrait) mounted on a bronze or marble pillar. Usually displayed in the Atrium (entrance hall) of a Roman home. The Atrium was the most public room — displaying herms there announced family prestige and pedigree to all visitors.

Historical Relief
What is Historical Relief? How It WorksDEFINITION
▼
Historical reliefs were used as decorations in the triumphal arches and columns which in the reign of Augustus were erected even in the most remote provinces. They consisted of a single arch between huge piers, against which were other columns, standing on a podium. They were usually inscribed with a dedication, and on top of the whole structure might be placed a chariot group. Romans were masters of relief sculpture. This is the sculpting of scenes or figures on marble, removing a sufficient amount of the background to allow the figures to stand out. This could be done with high relief, lots of detail, or low relief, moderate degree of depth. The purpose of reliefs was decoration for an otherwise blank marble wall. They also gave historical narrative of a military campaign to a largely illiterate population. They were propaganda.

⚒️ How Relief Works

  • Sculpt scenes/figures onto marble
  • Remove background so figures stand out
  • High relief: lots of detail, figures nearly 3D
  • Low relief: moderate depth, figures flatter
  • Romans were masters of relief sculpture

🎯 4 Purposes of Reliefs

  • Decoration for blank marble walls
  • Historical narrative of military campaigns
  • Accessible to a largely illiterate population
  • Propaganda — glorify the emperor
🧠 Analogy: Think of historical reliefs as ancient graphic novels or comic strips. For a population where most couldn't read, these carved stories on public monuments were the news broadcasts, history books, and propaganda posters all in one. They told you: "Your emperor won this battle, conquered those people, and deserves your loyalty."
