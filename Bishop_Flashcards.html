🐟 The Fish

The Fish
"He didn't fight. He hadn't fought at all."
Lines 5–6 · Click to flip
The Fish
Repetition · Humanisation
The fish's stillness is dignified and mysterious. Calling it "he" (not "it") humanises it from line 1. The repetition of "fight" deepens the emphasis — its passivity invites close observation, which drives the whole poem.
The Fish
"his brown skin hung in strips / like ancient wallpaper"
Lines 10–11 · Click to flip
The Fish
Simile · Domestic Imagery
"Ancient wallpaper" — homely, familiar, aged. Makes the exotic (fish skin) feel like something from home. "Ancient" gives it history and dignity. Immediately she finds beauty in this decay: "shapes like full-blown roses."
The Fish
"battered and venerable / and homely"
Lines 8–9 · Click to flip
The Fish
Triad of Adjectives
Three words that define the fish — and Bishop's view of resilience: damaged by life (battered), deserving deep respect (venerable), plain-looking (homely). "Venerable" is extraordinary — usually reserved for religious figures or elders.
The Fish
"Like medals with their ribbons / frayed and wavering, / a five-haired beard of wisdom"
Lines 61–63 · Click to flip
The Fish
Extended Metaphor · Personification
The old hooks become war medals. The fish is now a veteran survivor. "Beard of wisdom" — a wise old man. This is the poem's emotional turning point. Five captures, five escapes. Bishop sees herself in this survivor.
The Fish
"until everything / was rainbow, rainbow, rainbow! / And I let the fish go."
Lines 74–76 · Click to flip
The Fish
Repetition / Triad · Epiphany · Colour
Three "rainbows" = explosion of joy. Oil on bilge water = beauty in the ugliest part of the boat. Five words end the poem after 75 lines. Close observation leads to recognition — the fish has earned its freedom, just as she finds hers in letting it go.
The Fish
"the frightening gills, / fresh and crisp with blood"
Lines 23–25 · Click to flip
The Fish
Perspective Shift · Imagery
Bishop doesn't look away from harsh reality. The oxygen is "terrible" — from the fish's perspective, air is deadly. This is where she begins to truly empathise with the fish as a living creature in distress, not just an object she has caught.

🐷 The Prodigal

The Prodigal
"The brown enormous odor he lived by"
Line 1 · Click to flip
The Prodigal
Imagery · Colour
"Brown" = dull, earthy, decayed. "Enormous" = the smell has taken over his entire world. Starts with "The" not "A" — we are dropped into a life already at rock bottom. No introduction, no build-up.
The Prodigal
"Light-lashed, self-righteous, above moving snouts"
Line 5 · Click to flip
The Prodigal
Personification · Dark Humour
The pigs are "self-righteous" — morally superior to him. Even the worst pig (the sow that eats her young) appears to judge him. His shame is so extreme it distorts his reality. He feels judged by everything — even animals that have no right to judge.
The Prodigal
"the burning puddles seemed to reassure. / And then he thought he almost might endure"
Lines 12–13 · Click to flip
The Prodigal
Qualified Language · Misplaced Hope
"Seemed" not "did." "Almost might" — triple qualification. This is denial at work: using a beautiful sunrise to justify staying in a terrible situation. The hope is real but the comfort is false. Can hope sometimes be misplaced?
The Prodigal
"But evenings the first star came to warn."
Line 15 · Click to flip
The Prodigal
Structure · Personification · Contrast
"But" — the hinge of the whole poem. Instantly destroys the false hope of Sonnet 1. The star "warns" — nature itself signals danger. Evening brings truth. The shift from morning (denial) to evening (reality) is the poem's central structural movement.
The Prodigal
"safe and companionable as in the Ark."
Line 20 · Click to flip
The Prodigal
Biblical Allusion · Contrast
The animals are inside — warm, together, sheltered. He is outside, alone. "The Ark" suggests biblical safety and salvation — which he does not share. The contrast between their warmth and his isolation is devastating.
The Prodigal
"it took him a long time / finally to make his mind up to go home."
Lines 27–28 · Click to flip
The Prodigal
Understatement · Slant Rhyme
Plain, quiet words after nightmarish imagery — the simplicity is the power. "A long time" = honest truth about addiction recovery. "Home" and "more" don't rhyme (slant rhyme) — recovery is incomplete. The journey has only just begun.

🏠 Sestina

Sestina
"laughing and talking to hide her tears."
Line 6 · Click to flip
Sestina
Contrast · Dramatic Irony
The poem seems cosy — then this final line of Stanza 1 shatters it. "Hide" = both grandmother and child suppress grief through cheerfulness. This is what Bishop herself does with poetry: hiding intense pain behind careful, controlled language.
Sestina
"the teakettle's small hard tears / dance like mad on the hot black stove"
Lines 14–15 · Click to flip
Sestina
Personification · Pathetic Fallacy
"Mad" is crucial — Bishop's mother was committed to a mental institution. The cosy stove becomes "hot black" and threatening. Even domestic objects begin to reflect the hidden emotional turmoil. Grief has infected the warm kitchen.
Sestina
"Bird-like, the almanac / hovers half open above the child"
Lines 19–20 · Click to flip
Sestina
Personification · Symbolism
"Half open" = the child only ever has half the answers. Nobody has explained what happened to her family in full. The almanac (holding knowledge) hovers menacingly — full of truth she cannot reach. Also: the final stanza is "half length," mirroring this image.
Sestina
"puts in a man with buttons like tears"
Line 29 · Click to flip
Sestina
Symbolism · Art as Therapy
The man = Bishop's father she never knew. "Buttons like tears" = his memory is soaked in loss. The child gives him a place in the drawing she never had in life. Art does what reality cannot. This is the central insight: creative work processes what cannot be spoken.
Sestina
"Time to plant tears, says the almanac."
Line 37 · Click to flip
Sestina
Ambiguity · Personification
Does planting tears mean more grief is coming? Or that something may eventually grow from grief? The ambiguity is intentional — Bishop refuses easy consolation. The almanac "predicts" more sorrow — grief is cyclical and inevitable.
Sestina
"the child draws another inscrutable house."
Line 39 — Final line · Click to flip
Sestina
Symbolism · Form
"Inscrutable" = impossible to understand. "Another" = she keeps trying. She cannot draw a home she has never had. Home remains a mystery. In the final tercet, only objects end the lines — all human connection removed. The people will go; the objects (and memories) remain.

🦔 The Armadillo

The Armadillo
"the frail, illegal fire balloons appear."
Line 3 · Click to flip
The Armadillo
Tension · Irony
"Frail" = beautiful, fragile. "Illegal" = dangerous, forbidden. Both in the same line immediately sets up the poem's central tension: something can be wonderful and harmful at once. Good intentions (religious celebration) don't prevent the harm caused.
The Armadillo
"the paper chambers flush and fill with light / that comes and goes, like hearts."
Lines 7–8 · Click to flip
The Armadillo
Simile · Foreshadowing
The balloons pulse like heartbeats — alive, vital. But hearts can stop. This gentle simile quietly foreshadows the coming death and destruction. The balloons that "live" will "die" when they fall. Beauty and mortality are linked from the poem's opening.
The Armadillo
"suddenly turning dangerous."
Line 20 — Pivot · Click to flip
The Armadillo
Structure · Tone Shift
Placed as the final words of a stanza — maximum impact. "Suddenly" captures the speed of disaster. Up to here: beauty. After here: catastrophe. This is the poem's hinge. Environmental disasters happen fast, even when their causes build slowly.
The Armadillo
"Hastily, all alone, / a glistening armadillo left the scene, / rose-flecked, head down, tail down,"
Lines 29–32 · Click to flip
The Armadillo
Punctuation · Imagery · Repetition
Commas slow the reading — we linger on each detail of its misery. "Head down, tail down" — repeated "down" = total defeat. "Rose-flecked" = beautiful embers on its shell — beauty and horror together. Notes link the armadillo's departure to Bishop being uprooted from Nova Scotia.
The Armadillo
"Too pretty, dreamlike mimicry!"
Line 37 — Italics · Click to flip
The Armadillo
Tone Shift · Italics · Self-critique
Final stanza in italics = different voice, raw emotion. Bishop turns on the beauty she spent stanzas 1–5 admiring. "Too pretty" — she was seduced by the balloons' beauty. "Mimicry" — the balloons were only imitating beauty, not providing it safely.
The Armadillo
"a weak mailed fist / clenched ignorant against the sky!"
Lines 39–40 — Final image · Click to flip
The Armadillo
Personification · Allegory
The armadillo curled in defence = a tiny armoured fist of rage. "Weak" = powerless. "Ignorant" = it doesn't understand why this happened. The injustice is total: maximum suffering, zero comprehension. Allegorical — all innocent creatures caught in human crossfire.

⛽ Filling Station

Filling Station
"Oh, but it is dirty! / —oil-soaked, oil-permeated"
Lines 1–2 · Click to flip
Filling Station
Tone · Conversational Style
Exclamation mark = amusement, not horror. "Oh, but" is chatty, direct — she talks to us like a friend. This conversational openness is Bishop's signature in this poem. Sets up a playful surface that will deepen into genuine emotional revelation.
Filling Station
"to a disturbing, over-all / black translucency."
Lines 3–4 · Click to flip
Filling Station
Paradox · Imagery
Dark but still letting light through — a paradox. Even in the very first description, Bishop refuses to see only the ugly. Light exists in the blackness. This quietly sets up the poem's ultimate revelation: love exists in the most unlikely, grimy places.
Filling Station
"Why oh why the doily?"
Stanza 5 · Click to flip
Filling Station
Rhetorical Question · Repetition
The repeated "why" = genuine puzzlement. This is the pivot question of the poem. The doily is the detail that doesn't fit — which means it requires an explanation. The explanation is the poem's revelation. The doily was made with real care: "daisy stitch," "marguerites," "heavy with gray crochet."
Filling Station
"ESSO—SO—SO—SO / to high-strung automobiles."
Stanza 6 · Click to flip
Filling Station
Sound · Humour · Personification
Oil cans hum a little lullaby. Even a brand name becomes musical in Bishop's hands. Cars are given anxiety ("high-strung") and are "soothed." This playfulness makes the emotional landing of "Somebody loves us all" more powerful by contrast — humour clearing the way for genuine feeling.
Filling Station
"Somebody loves us all."
Final line — THE key quote · Click to flip
Filling Station
Anaphora · Epiphany · Universal
Three "Somebodys" build to this. An absent mother has quietly loved this place into a home. For Bishop, who grew up without her mother, this realisation is enormous: love can be present in the objects it touches even when the person is absent. "Us all" — universal. Love is everywhere if you look.
Filling Station
"Somebody embroidered the doily. / Somebody waters the plant... / Somebody arranges the rows of cans"
Final Stanza · Click to flip
Filling Station
Anaphora · Building Revelation
Three "Somebodys" — each naming an act of love by the absent figure. The repetition builds momentum toward the final revelation. An invisible person has been at work throughout: embroidering, watering, arranging. Love is made visible through its effects on objects.
