Poem 1 · Crossways Collection · 1889

The Lake Isle of Innisfree

A longing to escape from London to an idealised, pastoral peace — one of the most beloved poems in the Irish tradition.

Escapism Nature Longing Self-Sufficiency Biblical Allusion

📋 Background & Overview

Yeats wrote this poem in 1890 while living in London and feeling acutely homesick. Legend has it he was standing on a busy London street when he heard a small fountain in a shop window and it triggered a vivid memory of Sligo and Innisfree, a small island on Lough Gill that he had loved since childhood.

The poem belongs to a long literary tradition of the pastoral ideal — the desire to escape from urban, modern life to a simpler, self-sufficient existence in nature. It is not a concrete plan but a longing, a daydream held at the edge of his mind even while walking on grey London pavements.

🎯 Core Themes

  • Escapism & Longing: The poem is driven by desire rather than action — "I will" is a wish, not a plan.
  • Nature as Sanctuary: Nature is presented as healing, peaceful, and complete — it offers what the city cannot.
  • Self-Sufficiency: The nine bean-rows, the hive — a life that provides for itself, independent of commerce.
  • Memory & the Imagination: Even in the city, nature can be heard "in the deep heart's core" — the imagination transcends geography.
  • Solitude: No other people are mentioned. Yeats wants peace with nature alone.
🎓
Exam Tip

This poem is deceptively simple. Don't just summarise it — focus on the tension between the vibrant, sensory world of Innisfree and the dead, grey London reality. The contrast is the engine of the poem. Always mention the Biblical echo of the opening line and the way sound devices create the dreamlike quality.

📅 Context

In 1890 Yeats was 25, living in London, working hard as an emerging poet but isolated and homesick. His father John Butler Yeats was an artist who had moved the family there to further his career. W.B. felt cut off from Sligo — the place of his happiest childhood memories — and from the Irish cultural identity he was working to promote. The poem captures that very specific ache of an Irish person far from home.

Stanza 1
I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree,
And a small cabin build there, of clay and wattles made;
Nine bean-rows will I have there, a hive for the honey-bee,
And live alone in the bee-loud glade.
Stanza 2
And I shall have some peace there, for peace comes dropping slow,
Dropping from the veils of the morning to where the cricket sings;
There midnight's all a glimmer, and noon a purple glow,
And evening full of the linnet's wings.
Stanza 3
I will arise and go now, for always night and day
I hear lake water lapping with low sounds by the shore;
While I stand on the roadway, or on the pavements grey,
I hear it in the deep heart's core.
Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis
"I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree"

The opening line is deliberately echoing Luke 15:18 in the Bible — the Prodigal Son declares "I will arise and go to my father." This is no casual wish; Yeats is using Biblical language to give his longing the weight and determination of a spiritual vow. The repetition of "and go" is insistent, rhythmic, unstoppable.

The "small cabin" of "clay and wattles" is deliberately humble — not luxury or escape to wealth, but a return to something ancient, elemental, Irish. The nine bean-rows and honey-bee suggest a life of simple self-sufficiency. This is the pastoral ideal: providing for oneself without the commercial treadmill of the city. Note that not a single other person is mentioned — this is solitude by choice.

The "bee-loud glade" is a beautiful compressed image. Sound dominates — we hear the place even before we see it clearly, and that sound is alive, productive, warm. The glade hums.

"peace comes dropping slow, / Dropping from the veils of the morning"

In the second stanza, Yeats is no longer planning — he is already there in imagination. The tense shifts to a present-tense daydream. "Peace comes dropping slow" is one of his most famous lines: peace is liquid, natural, gradual — it cannot be hurried. The word "dropping" is repeated, slowing us down, enacting the very peace it describes.

"Veils of the morning" gives the dawn an almost supernatural, mystical quality — as if the morning is a spirit wearing veils. The full day passes in three images: midnight (glimmer), noon (purple glow), evening (linnet's wings). These are richly sensory — visual, auditory, luminous. The world of Innisfree is self-contained and perfect through all hours.

Note the colour: "purple glow" at noon is unexpected and dreamlike. This is not realistic nature description — it is idealised, mythologised, the place as memory wants it to be.

"While I stand on the roadway, or on the pavements grey, / I hear it in the deep heart's core."

The repetition of the opening line signals a return from the daydream. Now Yeats grounds us in the grim reality he is actually in — the "roadway" and "pavements grey." The colour grey is everything Innisfree is not: lifeless, uniform, man-made. The contrast is absolute.

But the final line rescues everything. Even standing on grey London pavement, he hears the lake "in the deep heart's core." This is profound: the imagination, memory, and love of place are indestructible. They cannot be taken from him by geography. Innisfree lives inside him always. This is what the poem is ultimately about — not an escape plan, but the discovery that home is carried within.

The enjambment in this stanza rushes forward as his imagination runs away with him — before the poem snaps back to the reality of the pavement. The pace momentarily breaks the meditative calm of the first two stanzas.

"peace comes dropping slow"

Peace is personified as something liquid that descends slowly from the morning mists. The sibilance of "peace" and the slow vowels enact the very quality they describe — the line itself slows you down as you read it. This is Yeats at his most musically precise.

Compound AdjectiveAuditory ImageryOnomatopoeia
Technique: Compound adjective that collapses sound into place
"bee-loud glade"

A three-word image that contains an entire atmosphere. The hyphenated compound adjective is Yeats's invention — "bee-loud" is not a real word, but it perfectly captures the experience of a glade alive with the hum of bees. Sound becomes the defining quality of the place. Productive, warm, alive.

Visual ImageryAlliterationColour
Technique: Dreamlike colour imagery spanning the full day
"midnight's all a glimmer, and noon a purple glow"

Midnight "glimmers" and noon is "purple" — neither is realistic, both are magical. Yeats is not describing Innisfree as it literally is, but as memory and desire paint it: luminous, heightened, supernatural. The island glows at every hour. Alliteration of "g" in "glimmer" and "glow" creates a musical, twinkling quality.

ContrastColour SymbolismSetting
Technique: Colour contrast as emotional contrast
"on the pavements grey"

After midnight's "glimmer" and noon's "purple glow," the single word "grey" hits like a bucket of cold water. Grey is everything Innisfree is not — uniform, lifeless, urban, man-made. It is the colour of despair. This is the world Yeats is actually standing in, and one word captures everything wrong with it.

MetaphorAlliterationFinal Line Power
Technique: Final metaphor — memory as deeper than geography
"I hear it in the deep heart's core"

The most important line in the poem. Innisfree is not a plan — it is a state of being. It lives at the deepest level of his identity, beneath conscious thought. The alliteration of "deep" and "heart" and the hard "c" of "core" drive the line home. No amount of grey London pavement can erase what is permanently held inside.

Poetic Techniques

🎵 Sound Devices

Sibilance ("some peace," "slow," "shore," "sounds") slows the poem and creates the hiss of water and wind. Alliteration of "l," "m," and "g" creates a gentle, lyrical, twinkling effect. Onomatopoeia in "lapping" physically mimics the sound of water. Sound is central — Innisfree is as much heard as seen.

📏 Form & Structure

Three stanzas of four lines each — balanced, neat, complete. Alternate rhyme (ABAB) throughout gives a sense of perfect order, reflecting the perfection of the idealised island. The short, even form matches the calm, controlled longing of the poem.

🎨 Colour Symbolism

"Midnight's all a glimmer," "noon a purple glow" — dreamlike, non-realistic colour gives Innisfree a supernatural quality. In contrast, "pavements grey" is the one piece of true colour in the poem, and it strips everything away. The contrast is devastating.

⏩ Enjambment

Used in the final stanza as the imagination runs away — lines tumble forward before crashing back to the reality of the pavement. The momentary loss of control in the metre mirrors the speaker's longing breaking through his composure.

📖 Biblical Allusion

The opening echoes Luke 15:18 (the Prodigal Son). This elevates the poem from homesickness to something closer to a spiritual vow — a return to an originating home. Innisfree is not just a place but a state of grace.

🔁 Repetition

"I will arise and go now" is repeated at the start of stanza 3, reinforcing the determination. "And go" repeated twice in line 1. Repetition of "dropping" in stanza 2 creates the slow, meditative rhythm of peace arriving naturally, at its own pace.

Context & Exam Preparation
🎓
Key Exam Argument

The most important thing to argue about this poem is the tension between longing and reality. Yeats does not escape to Innisfree — he stands on grey pavements. The poem is about the power of the imagination to preserve what the body cannot reach. Always connect to the final line and argue that "deep heart's core" is more powerful than any physical escape.

Links to Other Yeats Poems

  • The Wild Swans at Coole: Both are set in nature; both involve Yeats as a solitary observer mourning what he does not have. But Innisfree is pure longing while Swans is more elegiac and bitter.
  • Sailing to Byzantium: Both are poems of escape. Innisfree escapes to simple nature; Byzantium escapes to art and eternity. Compare as two very different answers to the same question: where do you go when the present is unbearable?
  • An Acre of Grass: Both poems feature Yeats in a place of stillness wanting more. But where Innisfree wants peace, An Acre of Grass wants frenzy. A fascinating contrast in what the poet desires.
💡
Exam Quote to Use

"I hear it in the deep heart's core" — this is always worth quoting. It shows that for Yeats, home is not a place but a permanent inner state. It proves the poem is not about escapism as a plan but as a form of inner survival.
