Poem 3 · Wintering Out · 1972

The Tollund Man

An Iron Age bog body in Denmark becomes a lens through which Heaney examines the ritual violence of the Troubles — connecting ancient sacrifice to modern sectarian killing.

The TroublesSacrifice & ViolenceReligion & RitualPast & PresentPersonal & Political

📋 Background & Overview

From Wintering Out (1972), this poem was written in direct response to the violence of the Northern Irish Troubles. Heaney found a framework for processing this violence in P.V. Glob's archaeological book The Bog People, which described preserved Iron Age bodies found in the bogs of Jutland, Denmark — many ritually killed and sacrificed to the Mother Goddess of the Earth.

The Tollund Man, discovered in 1950, is one of the best-preserved bog bodies in the world. He was hanged — his face serene, his last meal of grain porridge in his stomach. Glob argued these were sacrificial victims married to the goddess of the ground.

For Heaney, the parallel was inescapable: bodies found in Irish bogs; the victims of the Troubles; sacrifices made to "Mother Ireland." The poem makes this connection explicit — ancient Jutland ritual killing = modern Northern Irish sectarian killing. The senselessness of both is his conclusion.

🎯 Core Themes

  • Violence and Sacrifice: Both the ancient ritual killing and the modern Troubles are forms of sacrifice — human lives offered to a cause or a goddess. Heaney questions whether either has meaning.
  • The Personal and the Political: The poem begins as a personal act of devotion (a pilgrimage to see the body) and expands to encompass the public horror of the Troubles.
  • Religion and Sanctity: Heavy religious language (blasphemy, consecrate, holy ground, pray) surrounds both the Tollund Man and the Troubles victims — Heaney treats violence with the seriousness of a sacred act.
  • Home and Alienation: In Section III, Heaney imagines feeling "lost" in Jutland, yet "unhappy and at home" — the violence there is familiar. It reminds him of home. This is deeply disturbing.
  • The Futility of Sacrifice: Neither the ritual killing nor the Troubles produces redemption. Heaney prays for new life from the bodies but wonders if it is blasphemous to look for meaning in senseless death.
🎓
Key Exam Insight

This poem is described as "a personal poem about public events." The power lies in Heaney never directly naming the Troubles — instead, he works by parallel, by association, by implication. The effect is more powerful than direct statement would be. Always discuss the three-section structure: the body (devotion) → the violence (horror) → the connection (alienated belonging).

🏺 The Historical Context

The Tollund Man was found in 1950 in a Danish bog, perfectly preserved, wearing only a leather cap and a belt. He was hanged — a noose of plaited leather still around his neck. His last meal was a ritual grain porridge. P.V. Glob's 1969 book argued he was sacrificed to Nerthus, the Earth Mother goddess. Heaney read the book and immediately connected it to the bodies of Irish victims — found in bogs, killed on roadsides, sacrificed to political causes rather than goddesses. The parallel gave him a way to speak about the Troubles obliquely, through myth and archaeology.

Section I — The Body
Some day I will go to Aarhus
To see his peat-brown head,
The mild pods of his eye-lids,
His pointed skin cap.

In the flat country nearby
Where they dug him out,
His last gruel of winter seeds
Caked in his stomach,

Naked except for
The cap, noose and girdle,
I will stand a long time.
Bridegroom to the goddess,

She tightened her torc on him
And opened her fen,
Those dark juices working
Him to a saint's kept body,

Trove of the turfcutters'
Honeyed archives, he lies
Peat-brown forever,
Naked except for the cap.
Section II — The Violence
Tell me: who will say Aarhus
On the day of his Requiem
That we pray, mouth-fill
Of seeded ground,

To consecrate and resurrect
The scattered, ambushed
Flesh of labourers,
Stockinged corpses

Laid out in the farmyards,
Tell-tale skin and teeth
Flecking the sleepers
Of four young brothers, trailed

For miles along the lines?
Section III — The Journey
Something of his sad freedom
As he rode the tumbril
Should come to me, driving,
Saying the names

Tollund, Grauballe, Nebelgard,
Watching the pointing hands
Of country people,
Not knowing their tongue.

Out here in Jutland
In the old man-killing parishes
I will feel lost,
Unhappy and at home.
Section-by-Section Analysis
"Some day I will go to Aarhus / To see his peat-brown head, / The mild pods of his eye-lids"

"Some day I will go" — a determined, future-tense declaration. This is a pilgrimage. The tone is calm, respectful, quiet. "Mild pods of his eye-lids" — tender, gentle. "Pods" suggests something natural, botanical — as if the eyelids are seeds waiting to grow. The Tollund Man is described with extraordinary gentleness for someone who was murdered.

"Bridegroom to the goddess" — the man is permanently married to his sacrifice. His death was not random murder but a ritual union with the Earth Goddess. Heaney treats this with profound seriousness. "She tightened her torc on him / And opened her fen" — the goddess claimed him, the bog received him. His body has been transformed from victim to "saint's kept body" — the bog's preservation is a form of sanctification, as if the earth itself canonised him.

"I will stand a long time" — contemplation, reverence. Heaney places himself at the museum, standing before the body, understanding that this man and the goddess are now one — permanently joined in a bond that transcends time.

"The scattered, ambushed / Flesh of labourers, / Stockinged corpses / Laid out in the farmyards"

The register shifts dramatically. Dense religious language — "Requiem," "pray," "consecrate," "resurrect" — frames the violence in sacred terms. Heaney wonders if it would be blasphemous to pray that new life might grow from the broken bodies of the Troubles' victims, as seeds germinate from the ground.

The language becomes violently specific: "scattered, ambushed flesh of labourers," "stockinged corpses laid out in the farmyards," "tell-tale skin and teeth / Flecking the sleepers / Of four young brothers, trailed / For miles along the lines." These are real events: the four young brothers is a reference to a 1920s atrocity where four brothers were killed by police and their bodies dragged along railway lines. The graphic violence — flesh, skin, teeth — makes this impossible to aestheticise or distance.

The question "Tell me: will he not say a prayer…" is left syntactically unresolved — the sentence trails into the violence. This formal disruption enacts the disruption of the violence itself: language, like the bodies, is broken.

"Out here in Jutland / In the old man-killing parishes / I will feel lost, / Unhappy and at home."

Heaney imagines driving through Jutland, the Danish landscape where the Tollund Man was found. He does not speak the language — he is a foreigner, lost. The place names — "Tollund, Grauballe, Nebelgard" — are foreign, uncomfortable, slightly threatening sounds on an Irish tongue. He is genuinely alien here.

And yet: "Unhappy and at home." The violence of this place — "the old man-killing parishes" — is familiar. It reminds him of home. The parishes of Northern Ireland are also man-killing; the violence of ancient Jutland is no different from the violence of contemporary Belfast or Derry. This is Heaney's most disturbing and honest conclusion: he recognises the violence here because he has lived with it all his life. Ireland and Jutland are not so different. Home is a place of violence too.

"Something of his sad freedom" — the Tollund Man rode to his death on a farm cart. He was, in a terrible way, free — he had accepted his fate. Heaney wants some of that acceptance, that dignified resignation, that "sad freedom." He is not free; he is trapped between identifying with Ireland and being horrified by its violence.

A bridegroom enters a permanent, life-long bond. The Tollund Man is now permanently married to his sacrifice — to the goddess and to the bog. His death was not meaningless murder but a ritual union. Heaney uses the language of marriage to elevate the victim's status. This is also implicitly a comment on those who die for Ireland — are they also "bridegrooms" to the goddess of Ireland? The violence becomes explicit and graphic. "Ambushed flesh" is deliberately shocking — flesh reduced to scattered pieces. "Labourers" — these are ordinary working people, not soldiers. Their ordinariness makes the violence more horrifying. This is the moment Heaney connects the Tollund Man's ancient ritual death to the contemporary, random, senseless killing of the Troubles.

"In the old man-killing parishes"

"Parishes" — religious, community, Irish-sounding. "Man-killing" — brutally factual. Heaney takes a Danish landscape and makes it sound Irish by using the word "parishes." The connection is explicit: the parishes of Jutland and the parishes of Northern Ireland are both "man-killing." Violence is not confined to one place or one time — it is universal and recurring. The word "old" suggests this killing has always been happening.

"I will feel lost, / Unhappy and at home."

The most powerful line of the poem. "Unhappy and at home" is an oxymoron that perfectly captures Heaney's position: he is a foreigner in Jutland (lost, unhappy) but the violence here is so familiar it feels like home. He recognises it. He has lived with it. This is a profoundly honest and disturbing admission — that for someone from Northern Ireland, violence feels like home.

Poetic Techniques

📐 Three-Part Structure

The poem moves from devotion (Section I: the body, pilgrimage, tender description) to horror (Section II: the violence, graphic imagery, broken syntax) to connection (Section III: the journey, foreign familiarity, disturbing self-recognition). The three sections enact the emotional journey from admiration to anger to painful self-knowledge.

✝️ Religious Language

Section II is saturated with religious vocabulary: "Requiem," "pray," "consecrate," "resurrect," "holy ground." This places the Troubles' violence in a sacred frame — Heaney treats the dead with the seriousness of martyrs. He wonders if praying for meaning from these deaths is blasphemous, or the only appropriate response.

📐 Short Four-Line Stanzas

Short, clipped stanzas — "maybe reminiscent of the layers of digging in Bogland." Each stanza excavates a little further. The form forces precision, prevents excess, creates pauses for reflection between each revelation. The brevity mirrors the controlled grief of the poem.

🔊 Consonance & Sibilance

Harsh sibilance in Section II — "scattered, ambushed," "stockinged corpses," "skin and teeth" — forces the reader to slow down, to sit with the violent images. The sounds of violence are physically uncomfortable to produce in the mouth, making the reader complicit in speaking them.

🗺️ Place Names

"Tollund, Grauballe, Nebelgard" — foreign names that create discomfort in an Irish mouth. They are deliberately alien, emphasising Heaney's foreignness in Jutland. Yet the word "parishes" immediately localises the landscape — making it Irish. The juxtaposition creates the poem's central tension: foreign yet familiar, alien yet at home.

🌿 Contrast: Sacred/Violent

The poem moves between the sacred (saint, altar, bridegroom, goddess) and the violently physical (flesh, teeth, corpses, trailed bodies). The contrast is deliberate: Heaney refuses to choose between seeing violence as sacred sacrifice and seeing it as mere murder. Both are true simultaneously, and that is the horror.

Context & Exam Preparation
🎓
Key Exam Argument

This poem works by analogy and association — Heaney never directly names the Troubles, but uses the Tollund Man as a mythological parallel that allows him to approach the violence obliquely. Always discuss the three-section structure, the religious language framing violence as sacrifice, and the devastating final oxymoron "unhappy and at home." The poem argues that ritual killing — ancient or modern — is ultimately senseless.

Links to Other Heaney Poems

  • Bogland: The most direct companion — both poems use the Irish/Danish bog as a space that preserves bodies and connects past violence to present. Bogland establishes the symbolic geography that Tollund Man inhabits.
  • A Constable Calls: Both are political poems dealing with the Troubles. Constable Calls is intimate and domestic; Tollund Man is mythological and historical. Together they show the Troubles invading both private homes and ancient landscapes.
  • Postscript: Both poems involve journeys — a pilgrimage of sorts — where the physical landscape triggers profound emotional and spiritual revelation.
💡
Essential Exam Quote

"Unhappy and at home" — the poem's most disturbing and most honest line. Use it to argue that for Heaney, violence is not an aberration in Irish/Northern Irish experience but a familiar presence, woven into the landscape of everyday life.
