The Poet — Overview

Elizabeth Bishop (1911–1979)

A deeply personal, observational poet who turned painful experiences — loss, alcoholism, loneliness, and a childhood without a stable home — into carefully crafted, beautifully detailed poetry. She believed that looking closely at the world around you could help you understand yourself.
💡
The Key Thing to Understand About Bishop

Bishop rarely says "I am sad" or "I feel lost." Instead, she describes something in the outside world — a fish, a pig sty, a filling station — and uses that to show what she's feeling inside. The outside world becomes a mirror for her inner world. This is what makes her poetry so clever and so moving.

📖 Her Life — Why It Matters for the Poems

Born 1911 in Massachusetts

Her father died when she was an infant. This meant she never really knew him — you can see this in "Sestina" where the child draws "a man with buttons like tears."

Mother committed to a mental asylum in 1916

Her mother was so devastated by her father's death that she suffered a mental breakdown. Bishop never saw her again. This left her with a lasting sense of loss and no stable home.

Sent to live with grandparents in Nova Scotia

She was happy here. She became a keen fisherwoman — which directly inspired "The Fish." The grandmother in "Sestina" is based on this time. When she was taken away to live with her father's family in Massachusetts, she described it as feeling "like a kidnapping."

Struggled with alcoholism and depression throughout her life

She had to move from friends' homes out of shame. This is why she identified so strongly with the character in "The Prodigal," who is also hiding his drinking and living in shame.

Lived for years in Brazil with her partner Lota

This was the happiest period of her life. She described Lota's later death as devastating. Her love of travel and the natural world is reflected throughout her poetry.

Won the Pulitzer Prize in 1956

Despite publishing only five slim collections in 35 years, she is considered one of the greatest poets in the English language. She cared deeply about quality over quantity.

🎨 Her Style — How She Writes

Observational Style

She describes things in extraordinary detail — the fish's scales, the smell of the pig sty, the oil on the cans in the filling station. This attention to detail makes the reader feel they are really there. It also gives her a way to explore her feelings without stating them directly.

Confessional but Distanced

Her poems are deeply personal, but she often hides herself behind third-person figures — "the child," "the old grandmother," "he." This is a way of writing about painful things while keeping a safe emotional distance.

Vivid Imagery & Colour

She uses lots of colour and sensory detail, likely influenced by her love of painting. Think of the rainbow at the end of "The Fish," the red sunrise in "The Prodigal," or the "dark brown tears" in "Sestina." Colour often signals a change in mood.

Conversational Tone

Despite using complex forms, her poems often feel like someone telling you a story. She uses ordinary language, sometimes even humour. This makes deeply sad content feel very real and close — as if she's talking directly to you.

Moments of Epiphany

Many of her poems build toward a sudden moment of realisation — an epiphany. In "The Fish," it's the moment she sees the rainbow and lets the fish go. In "The Prodigal," it's the growing dark that forces him to face reality. These moments feel earned and genuine.

Variety of Forms

She uses sonnets ("The Prodigal"), sestinas ("Sestina"), and free verse ("The Fish"). Her choice of form always means something — the tight structure of the sestina mirrors the way grief keeps repeating; the loosening of the sonnet form in "The Prodigal" mirrors his uncertain journey home.

Poem 1 · First Collection (1946)

🐟 The Fish

She catches a remarkable, ancient-looking fish and instead of feeling proud, she becomes fascinated by it. She studies it in detail, notices all the old fishing lines stuck in its mouth (signs of previous escapes), and has a sudden moment of realisation — this fish is a survivor, like her. Everything fills with a rainbow of colour, and she lets it go. This poem is about respect, adversity, and finding hope in unexpected places.
🤔
The Simple Version

Think of catching a fish and noticing it has loads of old scars and broken hooks in its mouth — it has been caught many times before and escaped every time. You suddenly feel like this battered old fish deserves to live more than you deserve to keep it. That moment of realisation is the whole poem.

🎭 Poetic Techniques Used

Sound — Alliteration, Assonance, Sibilance Enjambment Repetition ("rainbow, rainbow, rainbow!") No Rhyme Scheme — Narrative/Conversational Vivid Imagery & Colour Similes (domestic & unusual) Personification Specialist Language Triads
📝
Exam Tip — The Poetic Techniques Image

Your notes contain an image listing these techniques. Key ones to mention: Sound devices speed up/slow down the poem based on excitement. Repetition of "rainbow" shows the sudden explosion of joy — the epiphany. Similes like "like full blown roses" and "packed in like feathers" use homely, everyday comparisons to help us understand the fish's body. Personification gives the fish human qualities so we empathise with it. Specialist language ("wire leader," "swivel," "bilge") makes Bishop sound like a real fisherwoman — authentic and trustworthy.

💬 Key Quotes — Full Analysis

"He didn't fight. He hadn't fought at all."
Lines 5–6 — First description of the fish

Repetition The word "fight" is repeated to really emphasise that this fish is doing something unusual — it is completely still. This creates a sense of mystery. Why won't it fight?

This is the first hint that this fish is different. It's so old and experienced, it doesn't bother struggling. It just hangs there with a kind of dignity. Bishop is already giving it respect even before she fully understands it.

He — Notice she calls it "he," not "it." She is already beginning to humanise the fish from the very first lines.

🧠
Remember

The fish not fighting is what makes Bishop look closer. If it had fought back, she might have just pulled it in and been done with it. Its stillness is what starts her journey of observation and respect.

"his brown skin hung in strips / like ancient wallpaper"
Lines 10–11 — Describing the fish's skin

Simile The comparison to "ancient wallpaper" is a very unusual choice. Wallpaper is something found in a home — it is domestic and familiar. But "ancient" tells us it is old, worn, even tattered.

This is a perfect example of Bishop's homely similes. She uses ordinary household objects to describe nature. This helps us visualise something unfamiliar (fish skin) through something familiar (old wallpaper peeling off a wall).

Crucially, she then immediately sees beauty in this: "shapes like full-blown roses / stained and lost through age." She is not disgusted by the fish's age and deterioration — she finds it beautiful. This teaches us to look at damaged, old things with respect rather than pity.

Imagery The roses are a striking image — even in decay, there is beauty. This mirrors Bishop's own life, where she found beauty in difficult, damaged experiences.

"the frightening gills, / fresh and crisp with blood, / that can cut so badly"
Lines 23–25 — Describing the fish's gills

This is an important moment. Bishop doesn't look away from the ugly, painful realities of nature. The gills are "frightening" and dangerous. She is honest about this.

Notice how she describes the oxygen as "terrible oxygen" — from the fish's point of view, the air is deadly. This is a moment where she begins to truly empathise with the fish, imagining how frightening and painful this experience must be for the fish.

Perspective Shift This is key to understanding the poem. Bishop moves from observer to empathiser. She stops seeing the fish as her catch and starts seeing it as a living creature in distress.

"hung five old pieces of fish-line... Like medals with their ribbons / frayed and wavering, / a five-haired beard of wisdom / trailing from his aching jaw."
Lines 51–64 — Discovering the old fishing lines

Personification The old fishing lines in the fish's mouth are described as "medals" — comparing the fish to a war veteran who has survived many battles. This is the moment the poem shifts completely.

The fish has been caught five times before and escaped. Each time, it broke free, leaving behind a bit of line. These broken lines are now its battle scars, its proof of survival.

"beard of wisdom" — The fish is now like an old, wise man. "Wisdom" comes from experience and survival. This fish has earned its place in the world.

This moment clearly connects to Bishop's own life — she too had survived enormous hardships (the loss of both parents, alcoholism, depression, loneliness). She sees herself in the fish.

🎖️
Think of it like this

Imagine meeting someone very old who has scars all over them. Instead of feeling sorry for them, you realise each scar is a story of survival — they got knocked down five times and got up six. That's how Bishop sees this fish. The scars are proof of resilience, not weakness.

"until everything / was rainbow, rainbow, rainbow! / And I let the fish go."
Lines 74–76 — The ending

Repetition "Rainbow, rainbow, rainbow!" — the three-fold repetition (a triad) suggests an explosion of joy and wonder. The whole boat is suddenly filled with colour and light. This is the epiphany — the sudden moment of realisation.

The rainbow is formed by oil spreading on the bilge water. Even in something dirty and ugly (oily water), Bishop finds a rainbow. This is her whole philosophy: beauty exists in unexpected, overlooked places.

Colour Imagery The poem begins in dull, earthly tones (brown, grey) and ends in a burst of rainbow colour. This reflects her emotional journey — from detached observer to joyful understanding.

"I let the fish go" — Simple, understated, enormously powerful. After 76 lines of close observation, this five-word sentence carries all the emotional weight. She gives the fish its freedom because it has earned it — and perhaps, in letting it go, she finds a kind of freedom herself.

📝
Exam Tip

This ending is one of Bishop's most famous moments. If asked about her use of language to create effect, or about moments of insight in her poetry, always reference this ending. The contrast between the dull reality of the scene (dirty boat, oily water) and the beauty of the rainbow perfectly captures her ability to find wonder in the ordinary.

Poem 2 · A Cold Spring (1955)

🐷 The Prodigal

Based on the biblical story of the Prodigal Son — a man who runs away from home and ends up living in poverty with pigs. But Bishop updates it to be about alcoholism. A man lives in a pig sty, hiding his shame, hiding his bottles, and slowly — painfully slowly — comes to terms with the fact that he needs to go home. Bishop wrote this because she understood it personally: she struggled with alcoholism and often had to leave friends' homes out of shame.
🤔
The Simple Version

Think of someone who has hit rock bottom. They know they need help, but admitting that is the hardest thing in the world. So they keep telling themselves things are fine, that tomorrow will be better. This poem is about that long, painful journey from denial to finally, finally deciding to go home and face reality.

📐 The Form — Two Sonnets, Not One

Sonnet 1 (Lines 1–14) — The Shakespearean Sonnet

More ordered and structured. Three quatrains (four-line sections) and a rhyming couplet. This represents the man telling himself everything is okay — imposing a false sense of order on his chaotic life. The structured form mirrors his denial.

Sonnet 2 (Lines 15–28) — The Looser Sonnet

Less ordered. The rhyme scheme breaks down. This represents reality crashing in — the dark evening, the nightmarish imagery, the dawning truth. The unravelling form mirrors his unravelling denial. The final words "home" and "time" don't properly rhyme — this is called slant rhyme, suggesting all is still not well even at the end.

📝
Exam Tip — Form Question

If asked why the poem is split into two sonnets with different structures, say: The first sonnet's ordered structure mirrors the Prodigal's denial and false hope. The second sonnet's looser structure mirrors the collapse of that denial as reality — represented by darkness and nightmarish imagery — closes in. The slant rhyme at the end ("home"/"time") suggests recovery is not complete — the journey is only just beginning.

💬 Key Quotes — Full Analysis

"The brown enormous odor he lived by"
Line 1 — Opening line

Imagery The poem opens not with a beautiful image, but with a smell — an enormous, brown smell. This immediately puts us inside a dark, suffocating space.

"Brown" is not a vibrant colour — it is dull, earthy, associated with dirt and decay. The word "enormous" suggests this smell has taken over everything; it is inescapable.

Starting with the word "The" (not "A") suggests we are entering a world that already exists — as if we have been dropped into the middle of his miserable situation. There is no romantic build-up. This is his life.

"Light-lashed, self-righteous, above moving snouts, / the pigs' eyes followed him, a cheerful stare — / even to the sow that always ate her young"
Lines 5–8 — Describing the pigs

Personification The pigs are described as "self-righteous" — as if they are judging him morally. Even animals that do terrible things (like a sow that eats her young) somehow seem to be looking down at him.

This creates a deeply disturbing and dark-comic effect. The man is so low, so deeply in denial, that even the worst animals seem to have moral authority over him.

It also conveys his state of mind — when you are struggling with addiction, you feel that even animals are judging you. His sense of shame is so extreme it is distorting his perception of reality.

"till, sickening, he leaned to scratch her head" — Despite knowing this pig eats her young, he is so lonely that he tries to make friends with her anyway. This is a heartbreaking image of loneliness and desperation.

"the sunrise glazed the barnyard mud with red; / the burning puddles seemed to reassure. / And then he thought he almost might endure / his exile yet another year or more."
Lines 11–14 — End of the first sonnet

There is a sudden burst of colour — "red," "burning." The sunrise makes the muddy barnyard briefly beautiful. This is a moment of hope.

But look carefully: "seemed to reassure" — not "did reassure." "Seemed" is doing important work here. It is not real reassurance. It is only an appearance of hope.

"almost might endure" — even more qualified. Not "could endure," not "will endure" — "almost might." This is the language of someone who is trying very hard to convince themselves that things are okay, but is not fully convincing even themselves.

Colour Imagery The red sunrise is a brief flash of beauty in a dull, brown world. But it is still the sunrise over a pig sty — he is still hiding his bottles and living in filth. Can hope sometimes be misplaced?

⚠️
Key Idea for the Exam

This is Bishop's central question about addiction and recovery: can hope be misplaced? He sees the beauty of the sunrise as a reason to endure another year. But this is dangerous — he is using a beautiful moment to justify staying in a terrible situation. Real recovery requires facing reality, not beautiful sunrises.

"But evenings the first star came to warn."
Line 15 — Opening of the second sonnet

"But" — This single word instantly destroys everything the first sonnet built up. "But" signals a reversal. Whatever hope or comfort was found in the morning, the evenings bring something different.

Personification The star "warns" him — nature itself is sending him a message. The evening represents reality, truth, the darkness he has been avoiding.

Where the morning was red and warm (false hope), the evening brings storm imagery: "overhanging clouds of hay" (foreboding), "pitchforks as faint forked lightning" (storm, threat), "shutting away the animals in the Ark" (shelter, but also the sense of a coming disaster).

The animals are "safe and companionable" inside — but he is outside, isolated. This contrast between the warmth inside and his isolation outside is painfully clear.

"But it took him a long time / finally to make his mind up to go home."
Lines 27–28 — Final lines of the poem

The poem ends very quietly, almost understated. After all the nightmarish imagery (slimy boards, bats, "shuddering insights"), this simple, almost plain statement carries enormous weight.

"a long time" — This is the honest truth about addiction and recovery. It doesn't happen quickly. It is a long, difficult process. Bishop is not romanticising recovery — she is showing how hard it actually is.

"home" — Note that for Bishop, "home" was not always a safe or happy place (her father died, her mother was committed, she was moved around as a child). Yet home represents recovery, facing the world, reconnecting. That it is still the destination says something important.

Slant Rhyme "home" and "time" do not perfectly rhyme — this is deliberate. It suggests that even after this decision, everything is not neatly resolved. The journey is only beginning. The broken rhyme mirrors the unfinished recovery.

🧠
Why Bishop Understood This Poem

She wrote this from personal experience of alcoholism. She didn't judge the Prodigal — she understood him. That understanding and empathy is why this poem feels so real and so compassionate rather than preachy or moralistic.

Poem 3 · Questions of Travel (1965)

🏠 Sestina

A child sits in a kitchen with her grandmother in Nova Scotia. It is raining. The grandmother reads from an almanac. The child draws pictures. Everything seems cosy — but underneath, both the child and grandmother are hiding enormous grief. The child draws a house, a winding path, and a man with "buttons like tears." This poem is about how grief surrounds us even when we try to stay cheerful, and how art — drawing, poetry — is a way of processing things we can't yet say out loud.

📐 The Sestina Form — Why It Matters

A sestina is a very strict, ancient poetic form. It has six six-line stanzas (sestets) plus a final three-line stanza (tercet). The same six words must end every line of every stanza — but they rotate in a specific pattern so the same word never ends the same line twice.


The six words in this poem are: house, grandmother, child, stove, almanac, tears.


Why does this matter? The constant repetition of these words is like a nursery rhyme — familiar, childlike, circular. But it also reflects how grief works: you keep coming back to the same things, the same people, the same objects. The rigid structure also gave Bishop (writing about her own painful childhood) a way to stay focused and process grief in a structured way, rather than being overwhelmed by it.

🎠
The Carousel Idea

Think of the poem like a carousel — the same six words keep coming around and around, just in a different order each time. This creates a dizzying, repetitive quality that mirrors the way grief and childhood memory work. You don't move past them; you just keep encountering them from different angles.

💬 Key Quotes — Full Analysis

"laughing and talking to hide her tears."
Line 6 — End of the first stanza

The first stanza seems warm and cosy — grandmother and child reading jokes from an almanac. Then the final line reveals everything: they are laughing to hide their tears.

Dramatic Irony We now have to re-read the whole first stanza differently. The "failing light," the September rain — these weren't just atmosphere. They were foreshadowing the grief underneath the cheerfulness.

This is typical of Bishop's style — she sets up a scene that seems ordinary, then reveals a deeper, sadder truth hiding beneath it. The contrast between the cosy domestic scene and the hidden grief makes the grief feel more real, not less.

"hide" — This is key. Neither the grandmother nor the child talks about their grief directly. Instead, they perform cheerfulness. This is exactly what Bishop herself did — she hid her personal struggles behind careful, beautiful poetry.

"the teakettle's small hard tears / dance like mad on the hot black stove"
Lines 14–15 — Stanza 3

Pathetic Fallacy The boiling water droplets from the kettle are described as "tears" — as if even the kettle is crying. The domestic objects in the poem begin to reflect the emotions of the people around them.

"dance like mad" — The word "mad" is very carefully chosen. Bishop's mother had been committed to a mental institution. The word carries an enormous amount of weight. The child watching the "mad" dancing droplets may be unconsciously connecting this to her mother's illness.

Personification The stove, which was cosy and warm in stanza 1, now feels more menacing. "Hot black" is threatening, not comforting. As the child's sorrow grows, the domestic objects that were once comforting begin to feel dangerous.

"Bird-like, the almanac / hovers half open above the child"
Lines 19–20 — Stanza 4

An almanac is a book that tells you things — the weather, predictions, useful knowledge. It "holds answers," as your notes say. So what does it mean that it "hovers half open"?

"half open" — The child only ever has half the answers. She doesn't fully understand what has happened to her family. She knows something is wrong, but nobody has explained it to her in full. This is the condition of her childhood — perpetually searching for meaning, never quite getting the whole picture.

Personification The almanac hovers "bird-like" above her — alive, slightly threatening, full of knowledge she can't quite reach. Your notes make a clever link here: the poem itself is "half length" in the final stanza (a tercet, not a sestet) — as if the poem, like the almanac, is also half open, unfinished.

"With crayons the child draws a rigid house / and a winding pathway. Then the child / puts in a man with buttons like tears"
Lines 27–29 — Stanza 5

The child draws a picture. The house is "rigid" — straight, structured, like the sestina form itself. But the pathway is "winding" — like Bishop's own uncertain life journey.

The man with "buttons like tears" is almost certainly the child's father — whom Bishop never knew. The buttons are like tears because his memory is associated with loss. He is in the drawing, but decorated with sadness.

Symbolism Art becomes the way the child processes loss. She can't talk about her father, so she draws him. The drawing is her way of giving him a place in her world, even if she never knew him in real life.

This connects to the broader theme: art and creativity as therapy. The child uses drawing; Bishop uses poetry. Both are ways of making sense of grief.

"Time to plant tears, says the almanac. / The grandmother sings to the marvellous stove / and the child draws another inscrutable house."
Lines 37–39 — Final stanza (the tercet)

"Time to plant tears" — This is a remarkable image. You plant seeds to grow something. Are tears being "planted" here — suggesting more grief is coming, that sadness grows? Or is this something more hopeful — that from grief, something can eventually grow?

"inscrutable house" — "Inscrutable" means "impossible to understand." The child can't draw a house she understands, because she doesn't know what a real home feels like. She has never had a stable, permanent home. So every house she draws is "another" one — she keeps trying to understand home, but it remains a mystery.

Disconnect Notice the final stanza: the grandmother is linked to the stove (warmth, home, domesticity) and the child is linked to her art. There is a gentle disconnect between them — the grandmother belongs to the home; the child belongs to her imagination and her art.

In the final tercet, only objects end the lines (stove, almanac, house) — no people. All human connection has been removed from the line endings. This subtly suggests that the people in this scene will eventually be gone, but the objects — and the memories — will remain.

📝
Key Exam Point

The poem was originally called "Early Sorrow" but later renamed "Sestina." Why? The title draws our attention to the form rather than the content. By calling it "Sestina," Bishop is saying: the form itself — its repetitiveness, its rigidity, its circling back — is the meaning. The structure IS the grief.

Poem 4 · Questions of Travel (1965) · Dedicated to Robert Lowell

🦔 The Armadillo

Set in Brazil on St. John's Day (June 24th), when locals traditionally release fire balloons into the sky as offerings to saints. The balloons are beautiful — but they are also illegal and dangerous. One crashes into a cliff, setting fire to an owls' nest and driving an armadillo from its home. The poem begins in wonder and ends in horror. It is an allegory — the fire balloons represent how human actions (often done with good intentions or in celebration) can destroy the natural world.
🤔
The Simple Version

Imagine beautiful fireworks being set off in a nature reserve. They look wonderful in the sky. But one crashes and starts a wildfire, burning the nests of owls and forcing animals to flee. The people releasing them never meant harm — but that doesn't stop the harm happening. That tension between beauty and destruction is what this poem is about.

💬 Key Quotes — Full Analysis

"the frail, illegal fire balloons appear. / Climbing the mountain height,"
Lines 3–4 — First stanza

Two words immediately stand out: "frail" and "illegal." The balloons are beautiful but fragile and technically not allowed. This sets up the central tension of the whole poem: something can be lovely and harmful at the same time.

The balloons "climb" — as if they are alive, purposeful. They are "rising toward a saint" (line 5) — being sent up as a religious offering. This is important: the people releasing them mean well. This makes the destruction that follows even more tragic.

Irony These balloons are sent up as an act of devotion to a saint — but they end up causing suffering to innocent animals. Good intentions do not prevent harm.

"the paper chambers flush and fill with light / that comes and goes, like hearts."
Lines 7–8 — Second stanza

The "paper chambers" are the inside of the balloons — they fill with hot air from the candle, making them glow. The light "comes and goes" — flickering as the candle inside pulses.

"like hearts" — This is a startling and beautiful simile. The glowing, pulsing balloons are compared to hearts beating. This gives them a living quality — as if they are alive, with heartbeats.

But it is also ominous: hearts can stop. The balloons can fall. This small simile foreshadows the destruction to come.

"or, in the downdraft from a peak, / suddenly turning dangerous."
Lines 19–20 — End of the fifth stanza

This is the pivot point of the entire poem. Up to this point, the balloons have been described with wonder and beauty. "Suddenly" — everything changes.

"suddenly" is a very ordinary word, but placed at the end of a stanza like this, it acts like a turning point or cliff-hanger. The next stanza shows us what "dangerous" means: a balloon crashes against a cliff.

Structure This transition mirrors how quickly beauty can turn to catastrophe. There is no warning, no gradual build-up. "Suddenly" it is all different. This is true of many environmental disasters — they happen fast, even when the causes have been building slowly.

"Hastily, all alone, / a glistening armadillo left the scene, / rose-flecked, head down, tail down,"
Lines 29–32 — The armadillo appears

Punctuation The commas slow down the line: "Hastily, all alone." This draws out the sadness of the image. The pausing rhythm makes us look at the armadillo carefully, feeling its isolation and distress.

"head down, tail down" — The repeated "down" emphasises defeat, sorrow, shame. The armadillo is fleeing with its head bowed — like someone walking away from a disaster with nothing left.

"rose-flecked" — The armadillo is covered in glowing embers from the fire. Beautiful word, terrifying reality. Even here, in a moment of destruction, Bishop finds visual beauty — which makes the horror more powerful, not less.

Your notes connect this to Bishop's own life: the armadillo leaving the "ancient owls' nest" mirrors Bishop leaving her grandparents' home in Nova Scotia — an "ancient," settled family — feeling devastated. The armadillo, hard on the outside but soft inside, may also represent Bishop herself.

"Too pretty, dreamlike mimicry! / O falling fire and piercing cry / and panic, and a weak mailed fist / clenched ignorant against the sky!"
Lines 37–40 — Final stanza (in italics)

The final stanza is written in italics — it is a different voice, more emotional, more direct. This is Bishop finally dropping her observational composure and expressing her anger.

"Too pretty" — She is turning on herself, or on the situation. The balloons were too beautiful, too "dreamlike." We were seduced by their beauty and missed the danger.

Slant Rhyme After the regular rhyme scheme of the earlier stanzas, the rhyme scheme becomes rougher here, more jagged — just as the situation has become rougher and more brutal.

"mailed fist / clenched ignorant against the sky" — The armadillo curled into a ball (its natural defence) looks like a small armoured fist raised at the sky. But it is "ignorant" — it doesn't understand why this is happening to it. It has done nothing wrong. Its rage and confusion are heartbreaking and futile.

Allegory The poem is an allegory. The armadillo represents all innocent creatures — and perhaps all vulnerable people — caught in the crossfire of human actions they cannot understand or control.

📝
Exam Tip

This poem was dedicated to Robert Lowell, who then wrote his own poem "Skunk Hour" as a response. If asked about links between poets or influences, you can mention this. The poem also connects to themes in "The Fish" — Bishop's respect for and empathy with animals.

Poem 5 · Questions of Travel (1965)

⛽ Filling Station

She visits a dirty, oil-covered filling station somewhere in California. A father and his sons work there, covered in grease. She is playfully appalled. But as she looks more closely, she notices things that don't belong: a doily, a plant (begonia), oil cans arranged prettily. Someone has tried to make this ugly place feel like a home. Someone cares. By the end, she realises there is an absent figure — likely a mother — whose love has quietly shaped this place. Someone loves these people, and loves the world itself.

💬 Key Quotes — Full Analysis

"Oh, but it is dirty! / —oil-soaked, oil-permeated / to a disturbing, over-all / black translucency."
Lines 1–4 — Opening

"Oh, but it is dirty!" — The exclamation mark signals Bishop's characteristic humour. She is not horrified — she is almost delighted by how dirty it is. This conversational, playful tone is typical of her style.

The word "little" (used elsewhere in the poem) gives a kind of affectionate, almost "cute" quality to the filling station — as if she finds it endearing despite its filthiness.

Imagery "Black translucency" is a striking phrase — something that is dark but still lets light through. Even at the start, even in the dirt, there is a hint that light and beauty exist here. This prepares us for the poem's conclusion.

"Father wears a dirty, / oil-soaked monkey suit / that cuts him under the arms, / and the greasy sons assist him"
Lines 10–14 — Introducing the family

The family is introduced with gentle humour. The father and his sons are "quite thoroughly oily" — completely covered in grease. But she describes the sons as "quick and saucy" — she seems to enjoy their energy and hustle.

"monkey suit" — An informal, playful term. Despite the grim surroundings, there is a warmth to how Bishop describes this family. She is not looking down on them.

The family "all quite thoroughly dirty" but alive and energetic — this mirrors Bishop's tendency to find vitality and worth in overlooked, unglamorous places. Just as she found the fish beautiful despite its battered appearance, she finds this family admirable despite their grimy work.

"Why oh why the doily? / (Embroidered in daisy stitch / with marguerites, I think, / and heavy with gray crochet.)"
Lines 28–31 — Stanza 5

"Why oh why" — The repetition of "why" shows Bishop's wonder and confusion. What is a delicate, hand-embroidered doily doing in a dirty, oil-covered filling station? It doesn't belong — and yet it is there.

The doily is described in loving detail: "daisy stitch," "marguerites," "heavy with gray crochet." Whoever made it put time, skill, and care into it. This is not a practical object — it is a loving one.

Imagery The doily, the begonia plant, the prettily arranged oil cans — these are all domestic touches. Someone has been trying to make this ugly, functional place feel like a home. But who?

"Somebody embroidered the doily. / Somebody waters the plant, / or oils it, maybe. Somebody / arranges the rows of cans / so that they softly say: / ESSO—SO—SO—SO / to high-strung automobiles. / Somebody loves us all."
Lines 35–42 — Final stanza

This is the emotional climax of the poem. "Somebody" — she doesn't know who, but somebody has done all these careful, loving things.

Anaphora The repeated "Somebody" (three times) builds to the final revelation. It creates a sense of mystery — this person is unseen, unnamed — but their love is visible everywhere in the small details they have left behind.

"ESSO—SO—SO—SO" — This is playful and musical. The oil can labels seem to hum a little lullaby. Even the brand name on a can becomes a gentle sound of comfort. This is Bishop's humour and her ear for language at their best.

"Somebody loves us all." — This simple line is one of the most moving endings in Bishop's poetry. The "somebody" is likely an absent mother figure. For Bishop, who grew up without a mother, this realisation — that even in the most overlooked, dirty, ordinary place, love can be quietly present — was enormously meaningful.

Your notes suggest she may have realised through this poem that her own mother, despite her absence, had loved her. The filling station becomes a kind of universal symbol for the invisible, taken-for-granted love that keeps the world bearable.

🧠
The Key Idea

Bishop enters the filling station thinking it is just a dirty, ugly place. By the end, she sees that love has been quietly at work there all along, in the smallest domestic details. This is her epiphany — and it connects to her whole life: even in the most difficult, seemingly loveless circumstances, there is someone who cares.

Connections Across the Poems

🔗 Thematic Links & Connections

Bishop's poems are deeply connected to each other. She returns to the same themes, the same preoccupations, the same techniques again and again. Understanding these connections is key to doing well in the exam — it shows you can think about her work as a whole, not just poem by poem.

🏠 Theme 1: Home, Belonging & The Search for Stability

Sestina

The child draws an "inscrutable house" — she cannot understand what home means because she has never had a truly stable one. The house in her drawing is always rigid, always incomplete. Home is something she searches for but cannot fully picture.

The Prodigal

"Home" is mentioned at the very end — it takes "a long time" to decide to go there. For Bishop, home was not always safe or welcoming (she described being moved to her father's family as "kidnapping"). Yet home remains the destination, the place of recovery. Ambivalent but necessary.

Filling Station

An unlikely, dirty place is transformed into something home-like through love and care. The doily, the plant, the arranged cans — somebody has made a home out of this. Bishop suggests that home is not a building; it is what love does to a place.

The Armadillo

The armadillo's home — the "ancient owls' nest" — is destroyed by forces beyond its control. This mirrors Bishop's own experience of losing her home in Nova Scotia. The nest represents a settled, established home; its destruction represents the loss that came with being uprooted.

🔍 Theme 2: Observational Style and the Epiphany

One of Bishop's most distinctive qualities is that she observes something closely and builds toward a moment of sudden understanding — an epiphany. This pattern appears in almost every poem.

The Fish

Observes the fish in minute detail for 75 lines → Epiphany: sees the rainbow in the oily water, realises the fish is a survivor deserving of freedom → "And I let the fish go."

Filling Station

Observes the dirty station and the domestic details that don't fit → Epiphany: realises someone has been quietly, lovingly caring for this place → "Somebody loves us all."

The Armadillo

Observes the beautiful balloons → Observes the destruction they cause → Epiphany (with anger): "Too pretty, dreamlike mimicry!" — beauty that causes harm is not innocent.

🪞 Theme 3: Bishop as Character in Her Own Poems

A crucial point your notes make: Bishop sees herself as a character in her poems. The poems serve as metaphors for her own life and struggles. She doesn't always say "I" — she might use "he" or "the child" — but she is always there.

The Fish = Bishop herself

The fish, battered but surviving, carrying the marks of previous escapes "like medals" — this is Bishop's own resilience. She too survived alcoholism, depression, the loss of her partner, a chaotic childhood. She looks into the fish's eyes and sees herself.

The Prodigal = Bishop herself

The Prodigal hiding his bottles, living in shame, finding brief moments of hope in sunrises, slowly deciding to face reality — this is Bishop's own experience of alcoholism. She understood this character from the inside, not the outside.

"The Child" in Sestina = Bishop herself

The child in Sestina is never named "Elizabeth" — she is distanced as "the child." But the setting (Nova Scotia, her grandmother), the lost father, the hidden grief — all of this is autobiographical. The distancing is a way of writing about pain with some protection.

🌿 Theme 4: Nature, Destruction & Human Impact

The Armadillo

The most direct treatment of this theme. Human celebration (fire balloons) causes environmental destruction. The armadillo is innocent; the owls are innocent; the rabbit is innocent. None of them chose this. Human actions — even well-intentioned ones — can destroy nature.

The Fish

The act of fishing — catching a creature from its habitat — is the starting point. Bishop's decision to observe the fish rather than immediately act on it (throwing it in the cooler) is what leads to her epiphany. Pausing to truly look at nature changes how you treat it.

💔 Theme 5: Grief, Loss & Recovery

Sestina — Childhood Loss

Loss of father, loss of mother (effectively), loss of a stable home. The child processes this through drawing. The grief is everywhere — in the rain, the tears, the dark teacup — even when the characters try to hide it through cheerfulness.

The Prodigal — Addiction and Redemption

Loss of self, dignity, and home through addiction. The recovery is slow and not fully complete by the poem's end. But the decision to go home — however long it takes — represents hope. Recovery is possible, even if the rhyme at the end ("home"/"time") doesn't quite work yet.

Filling Station — The Absent Mother's Love

Bishop grew up without a mother. The realisation at the end of Filling Station — that an absent person's love can still be felt in small domestic details — may be her way of coming to terms with her own mother's love for her, despite the absence.
